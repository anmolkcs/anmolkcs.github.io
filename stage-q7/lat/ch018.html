The card was addressed, in a fine, even hand, To the honoured new disciples of the Gu clan of Jade Ridge.

Inside, it said that Lu Mingyuan, of the Lu clan of Cangdu, would be grateful for the pleasure of their company at a small dinner at the Pavilion of Listening Pines, in the Academy’s eastern gardens, on the evening of the fifth day hence. It said nothing else. It did not need to.

“Don’t go,” said Mo Ji.

“It’s dinner.”

“It’s the Lu clan.” Mo Ji was sitting on his bed with his knees drawn up, his face dark. “Do you know how my father got that debt? Four years ago, the Lu clan’s iron office came to every forge on the southern bank and offered spirit iron at a third off the market price. Generous. Neighbourly. Pay in two years, they said, no hurry. Then the contracts came with a clause in very small writing about delivery dates. Then the iron was always late. Then the interest started.” He thumped the bed-frame. “Half the forges on the southern bank belong to them now, except for the name over the door. They didn’t break a single anvil.”

“I know.”

“Then why go?”

Gu Yan turned the card over in his hands. “Because Gu Feng and Gu Lan were invited too,” he said, “and if they go without me, I won’t know what was said. And because if we refuse a polite invitation from the heir of a ducal clan in our first month, that will be written down as well.”

Mo Ji glared at him. Then the glare cracked. “You think like a merchant,” he grumbled.

“I think like a man whose grandfather once spent three years getting paid for a wedding chest.”

Gu Feng had already decided to go. Gu Lan said she would go because the Pavilion of Listening Pines was supposed to serve crystal-skin dumplings and she had never had a crystal-skin dumpling, and if she was going to be bought, she wanted to be bought with something worth eating.

They went together, on the fifth evening, in their best robes, which were not very good.

The eastern gardens were a part of the Academy Gu Yan had not yet visited: a fold of the mountain where streams had been coaxed into a chain of ponds, with willows trailing in them and little bridges of carved white stone. The Pavilion of Listening Pines stood on a spur above the largest pond, its upturned eaves hung with silk lanterns. Inside, a low table of red lacquer had been laid for six. Through the open lattice of the walls came the sound of wind in the old pines above, rising and falling like distant surf.

Lu Mingyuan was standing at the rail when they came up the steps. He turned and smiled.

“Disciples Gu,” he said. “Thank you for coming. Please, sit. I’m told the wind is good tonight.”

He was even more pleasant up close than he had seemed from across the arena. His voice was low and unhurried. He wore white again, simple and well-cut, without any ornament beyond a small silver deer at the collar. He bowed to each of them, a fraction more deeply than a senior disciple and a ducal heir needed to bow to three outer disciples from a branch-family town, and it was impossible to tell whether that fraction was courtesy or calculation.

There were two others at the table already. One was Qu Hai, his ankle evidently healed, who gave Gu Yan a short nod with no resentment in it. The other stood a little apart, by the serving table, overseeing the two maids who were laying out the dishes.

Steward Pang.

He bowed to them without a flicker of recognition. “Disciples.”

Gu Yan returned the bow and sat.

The food was extraordinary. Gu Lan got her crystal-skin dumplings, translucent as frost, with whole river-prawns visible inside; then there was a soup of white fungus and pear; then fish steamed with ginger; then slices of some meat so tender it came apart at the touch of the chopsticks and carried a faint warmth of qi—spirit-beast meat, Gu Yan realised, and expensive beyond anything his clan could afford. Lu Mingyuan ate little and talked easily. He asked about Jade Ridge, about the journey, about the examination. He listened to the answers. He laughed at Gu Lan’s jokes, and they were good jokes, and the laughter seemed real.

He did not mention the debt, or the smithy, or Mo Ji. He did not mention the duel, except once, lightly, to Qu Hai: “You’ll have to tell me how it felt, losing to a man a full step below you. I’ve never had the privilege.” And Qu Hai, to Gu Yan’s surprise, smiled wryly and said, “Humbling, Young Master.”

It was only when the maids had cleared the dishes and brought tea that Lu Mingyuan set down his cup and folded his hands.

“I won’t insult you by pretending this is only dinner,” he said. “You’re from a small clan in a small town. You came to the capital with nothing but your talent. The Academy will feed you and teach you, but the Academy does not care, very much, which of its five hundred new disciples succeeds. It has five hundred more coming next year.” He spoke gently, without any trace of condescension. “My clan cares. We look for people of ability and we invest in them. Pills. Resources. Introductions. Protection, when protection is needed. Qu Hai’s family has been with us for three generations, and in three generations they’ve gone from a merchant house to a family with two Heaven Seal elders.” He turned his hand palm upward on the table, an open gesture. “I’m offering patronage. Nothing is signed. Nothing is owed. If you accept, you’ll have a friend in Cangdu. If you refuse, you’ll still have had a good dinner.”

There was a silence. The pines sighed overhead.

Then Lu Mingyuan looked at Gu Yan. “And I understand,” he added mildly, “that you’ve become friends with the son of a blacksmith on the southern bank. Master Mo. A fine craftsman. I’ve seen the account. I’d be very happy, as a gesture between friends, to have that matter set aside.”

Gu Yan felt Gu Lan go still beside him.

It was a beautiful offer. That was what made it terrible. A word, and the anvil was safe, and Master Mo’s hand, and Mo Ji’s whole family. A word, and a pill every month instead of every three months. A word, and Grandpa’s medicine paid for, and—

He did not answer at once. He lifted his teacup and drank, to have something to do with his face.

Steward Pang had come forward to refill the pot. He lifted the iron kettle from its brazier, found the water a little too cool, and frowned slightly; and without any fuss he laid his free hand flat against the kettle’s side and sent a pulse of qi into it to warm it.

It was a tiny thing. The kind of thing any servant in a cultivator household did twenty times a day.

But Gu Yan had the sight open. He had opened it the moment he saw Pang, carefully, at the very edge of his strength, the way he had learned to do when he did not want the effort to show. And for the space of that one pulse of qi, as the steward’s power flowed outward through his palm, the oiled-silk sheen over his meridians thinned.

Like cloth stretched over a lamp.

Beneath it, coiled in the steward’s channels, were threads of dark red.

Not one. Dozens. Fine as hair, tangled through his meridians from wrist to shoulder, and they moved. They were curled tight on themselves like sleeping worms, exactly like the single thread at the heart of the token in Gu Yan’s pouch; and they were drinking. He could see it. They were drawing something into themselves from the qi that flowed past them, a dark warmth that was not qi at all but something thicker, something that tasted, to the sight, like iron and salt.

Blood essence.

The same drained, hollowed look he had seen in the dead beast in the Black Pine Forest, its qi lines sucked empty rather than torn. Only here it was the other way around. Here was the mouth.

The pulse ended. The sheen closed over again, smooth and unreadable. Steward Pang poured the tea.

Gu Yan set his cup down very carefully, so that it would not rattle.

“Senior Brother Lu,” he said. “Thank you. It’s very generous. But I’m afraid I’m not able to accept.”

Lu Mingyuan’s expression did not change. “May I ask why?”

“My grandfather is a carpenter,” Gu Yan said. “He taught me that a man should only put his weight on a joint he made himself, or one he’s seen made.” He bowed his head politely. “I don’t know your clan well enough yet. That’s my failing, not yours.”

There was a pause.

“A sensible principle,” said Lu Mingyuan. He smiled; it seemed entirely genuine. “And Master Mo?”

“Master Mo,” said Gu Yan, “would rather pay his own debts. I think. You’d have to ask him.”

“I’ll do that.” Lu Mingyuan turned to the others. “Disciple Gu Lan?”

Gu Lan put down her chopsticks with great dignity. “Senior Brother,” she said, “those were the best dumplings I’ve ever eaten in my life. But my cousin is very annoying and I have to stay with him or he’ll get into trouble.”

“And Disciple Gu Feng?”

Gu Feng had said almost nothing all evening. He sat straight-backed, his hands on his knees, and Gu Yan could see the struggle in his face: the stair, the four hundred and fiftieth step, the salt-merchant’s son with his lacquered box of pills.

“The Gu clan of Jade Ridge,” said Gu Feng slowly, “has been a small clan for three hundred years. We have stayed a small clan because we never sold ourselves to a bigger one. My grandfather says that’s our stupidity and our pride.” He lifted his chin. “I’d like to see if it can also be our strength. I’ll decline, Senior Brother. With thanks.”

Lu Mingyuan looked at the three of them in turn, and then he laughed, softly and with what sounded like real pleasure.

“Jade Ridge,” he said. “I’ll remember the name.” He rose, and they rose with him. “The offer doesn’t expire. If you change your minds, any of you, send a word.” He walked them to the steps. And there, as Gu Yan passed him, he added, quite casually, in the tone of a man mentioning the weather:

“I was sorry to hear about the trouble your town had. With the beasts. And that bell.”

Gu Yan’s foot found the next step without missing it. “Bell, Senior Brother?”

“Ah. Was it not a bell? I must have heard it wrong.” Lu Mingyuan smiled. “Cangdu is full of rumours. Good night, Disciples Gu.”

“He knew,” said Gu Lan, on the path back. She was walking very fast, hugging her arms. “We never told anyone about the bell. The deacon told the Academy, maybe, but the Academy doesn’t tell the Lu clan—”

“He wanted us to know he knew,” said Gu Feng. “That’s why he said it.” He looked at Gu Yan. “What did you see? At the end. You went white. You think I didn’t notice?”

Gu Yan hesitated. The truth was a thing of lines and threads he could not explain without explaining everything.

“The steward,” he said at last. “There’s something wrong with him. Something like what was wrong with the beasts.”

Gu Feng stopped walking.

“Are you certain?”

“No,” said Gu Yan honestly. “I’m certain enough not to eat his food again.”

Gu Feng looked at him for a long time in the lantern light. Then he nodded once, grimly, and walked on.

He could not sleep that night either. Mo Ji was snoring like a forge-bellows. Gu Yan climbed out of the window onto the roof of the fourteenth building, and sat on the cold tiles with his back against the ridge, and looked up.

The sky over Cangdu was hazed with the light of its lanterns. The stars were fewer here than on the plain. But they were there.

He opened himself to the Lattice, and the second node woke at once, cool and eager, and the thread of starlight came down.

He was angry. He had not realised how angry until the starlight hit him, and the anger rose to meet it, and for a moment the two of them burned together in his chest like oil and flame. A thing that drank blood, pouring tea. A pleasant young man in white offering to save a smith’s hand with the same mouth that mentioned the bell. A town on a ridge he would not see again for a long while, where an old man was coughing in a dark workshop because one of those things had come to Jade Ridge.

Be careful, he thought, with Grandpa’s voice. Anger is a crooked saw. It cuts fast and it cuts wrong.

He breathed. He let the anger settle, the way you let sawdust settle before you measure. And he let the starlight have him.

It went into his bones, deep, deeper than it had gone since Jade Ridge. He felt it in the marrow of his thighs, his spine, the small bones of his hands; felt it press and burn and harden. And then, as it had on the carriage roof, it overflowed. It ran sideways into the four opened meridians, and the qi wheel in his chest spun faster, faster, until it was roaring, and the fifth meridian, running from his collarbone down his right arm, began to strain against its seal like a door with a gale behind it.

He did not need to find the grain this time. The starlight found it for him. It ran along the seam like water along a crack, and froze, and expanded.

The meridian split open with a sound like the first crack of ice in a cold well. Qi flooded down his right arm, into the hand that held a sword like a chisel, and the wheel in his chest became a five-spoked wheel, turning smooth and heavy, and the night’s qi poured into him so fast that the tiles around him frosted faintly white.

5th Vein.

He sat there a long time afterward, breathing, with frost melting under his palms and the taste of iron in his mouth from a nosebleed he had barely noticed.

Five meridians. Four left. And then the Star Sea, the sky inside a person.

And somewhere in this city, a thing with red threads in its veins was pouring tea for a young man who knew about the bell.

When he finally climbed back through the window, just before dawn, there was a second note on the threshold of room nine. Not cream paper, this time. A plain strip of blue, folded once, without seal or name.

It said, in small, precise characters:

Observatory Archive, west wing. The hour of the snake. Come alone. Bring your nosebleeds; I would like to measure them. — S.L.
