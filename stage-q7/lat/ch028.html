The Royal Star Observatory kept its archive underground, which Gu Yan had always found strange for a family that spent its life looking up.

“Paper hates light,” Su Lingxi said, the first time he had remarked on it. “And starlight is worse than sunlight. It fades ink and makes old charts lie.” She had said it as though the charts were her personal acquaintances, and in a way he supposed they were.

He came down the long spiral stair at the first bell with his ribs still bound and his breath short, and found her already waiting in the lowest chamber, a long vault of grey stone lined from floor to ceiling with lacquered drawers. A single lamp of cold blue starstone burned on the reading table. Beside it lay a ledger bound in faded indigo cloth, closed.

She did not say good morning. She looked at his bandages, then at his face.

“Sit down before you fall down,” she said.

He sat. She did not open the ledger at once.

“I’ve been searching the expedition records since the dead stone,” she said. “I wanted to know who brought it back. The Observatory keeps a copy of every report from every opening of Fallen Star Valley for the last eight hundred years. Most of them are dull. Lists of beasts. Lists of what was found. Lists of who didn’t come back.” She paused. “I found it. Six hundred years ago, a deacon named Fu carried it out of the Valley on his back and died of its cold a month later. That’s not why I sent the crane.”

“The record from ten years ago.”

“Yes.” She put her hand flat on the indigo cover. “Gu Yan. Before I open this. Do you want me to read it to you, or do you want to read it yourself?”

It was an oddly gentle question from someone who was rarely gentle. He looked at her hand on the cover, at the precise white knuckles, and understood that she had already read it, and that whatever was inside, she had thought carefully about how to give it to him.

“I’ll read it,” he said.

She opened the ledger to a page marked with a thin strip of silver paper and turned it toward him.

The hand was a clerk’s hand, neat and impersonal, the ink faded to brown.

Record of the Opening of Fallen Star Valley. Year 4,002 of the Cang Calendar, Eleventh Month.

Expedition of Starfall Academy and the Royal House, led by Elder Gao Shen (Heaven Seal, Seventh Seal). Disciples: fourteen. Royal guards: twenty. Independent cultivators admitted under Academy token: three.

There followed two pages of the dull lists Su Lingxi had promised. Shard beasts sighted and slain. A ruined tower in the outer zone. Fragments of crystal that rang when struck. A disciple named Xu who lost an arm in the middle zone. Gu Yan read every word. He did not know what he was looking for until he found it, on the third page, in the same impersonal hand.

Ninth day. Independent cultivator Gu Changfeng (Heaven Seal, formerly inner disciple of this Academy, of the Gu clan of Jade Ridge) separated from the expedition at the boundary of the middle zone, against the express instruction of Elder Gao. He stated his intention to enter the inner zone alone. Elder Gao forbade it. Gu Changfeng bowed to the Elder and went in.

Eleventh day. Gu Changfeng did not return.

Fourteenth day. The Valley’s boundary began to close. Expedition withdrew. Gu Changfeng not recovered.

Status: presumed dead.

That was all. Four entries. Fifty-odd characters for a man.

Gu Yan read them again. Then a third time. The words did not change. Bowed to the Elder and went in.

He thought of a tall man in a grey robe crouching in the workshop doorway in Jade Ridge, a long time ago. The smell of rain. A cold stone pressed against a child’s chest, and a voice saying something he could not remember now, no matter how hard he tried. Then footsteps on the wet road, going away.

He had always imagined his father walking into the distance. He had never imagined where the road ended. It ended here, in a ledger, in a vault under the ground. Presumed dead.

“There’s more,” Su Lingxi said quietly.

She turned the page. Here the hand changed: not a clerk’s now, but something swifter and more cramped, the characters crowded together as if written in haste by lamplight. The ink was a different colour, a faint blue-black.

Observatory addendum. Night of the ninth day. Recorded by Su Wenyuan, Star Reader of the Second Rank, from the western platform.

“My grandfather,” said Su Lingxi.

At the second hour after midnight, the star Tianshu, which stands above Fallen Star Valley in the western sky, dimmed for the space of a quarter hour, then returned to its former brightness. No cloud. No haze. All other stars unchanged. At the same hour, the needle of the Great Compass on the western platform ceased to point to any star and turned to face directly upward, toward the zenith, where no star stands. It held there for the same quarter hour.

I have consulted the records of eight hundred years. This has not happened before.

I cannot interpret it. I record it so that someone may.

Below that, in the same hand but smaller, almost an afterthought:

Upward. Not toward a star. Toward nothing.

Gu Yan sat very still.

“I asked him,” Su Lingxi said. “Last night. My grandfather is ninety-one and doesn’t remember what he ate for dinner, but he remembers every star he ever read.” She looked at the page. “He said it was the strangest night of his life. He said for a quarter of an hour it felt as though someone had opened a door in the sky and gone through it, and then closed it behind them.”

They sat in the blue lamplight for a long time without speaking.

Gu Yan’s hand had gone to his chest, to the inside of his robe, where a small square packet of oiled paper lay against his heart. He had carried it there since the morning he left Jade Ridge. The seal on it was plain wax, unmarked. The paper was soft and creased with ten years of keeping.

To open when you can see the sky’s edge, his grandfather had said, putting it into his hands on the last night. Not before. Your father was very clear.

He took it out and laid it on the table beside the ledger.

Su Lingxi looked at it, then at him.

“My father’s,” he said. “He left it with my grandfather. I’m not supposed to open it yet.”

“When are you supposed to open it?”

“When I can see the sky’s edge.”

She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, without a trace of mockery, “That’s a very inconvenient instruction.”

He laughed. It came out cracked, half a breath, and hurt his ribs, and he did not care.

“I could open it now,” he said. “Nobody would know. My grandfather’s a thousand li away. My father—” He stopped. “The ledger says presumed dead.”

“Do you think he’s dead?”

He looked at the cramped blue-black characters. Upward. Not toward a star. Toward nothing. He thought about the Principal’s dome melting into sky, the three thousand lanterns in the dark, the thread of the Sky Pillar touching the Membrane like a needle through silk. He thought about the endless grid in the dead stone, going on and on, and the wound in it.

“No,” he said. “I think he went somewhere.”

“Then wait.” Su Lingxi’s voice was precise, the way it always was. But she did not look at him as she said it. “He knew things you don’t. If he told you to wait, there was probably a reason. And if you open it now and it says something you can’t use yet, you’ll have spent it.” She hesitated. “It’s the only letter you’ll ever get from him until you find him. Don’t waste it.”

He looked at the packet for a long time. Then he picked it up and put it back inside his robe, against his heart, and the seal was unbroken.

“Thank you,” he said.

“For what?”

“For reading it first. For thinking about how to give it to me.”

Her ears turned faintly pink in the blue light. “I’m a scholar. I check sources.” She closed the ledger briskly. “There’s one more thing. You won’t like it, or you’ll like it too much.”

“What?”

“The Valley opens once every ten years,” she said. “This record is from the Eleventh Month of the year four thousand and two. It’s now the Ninth Month of four thousand and twelve.” She met his eyes. “By the Observatory’s reckoning, the boundary of Fallen Star Valley will begin to weaken again in about six weeks.”

He did not go straight back to the dormitory. He walked instead, a long way, out past the Stele Forest and along the ridge above the Academy until the wind was cold and the city below was only a pattern of roofs. There he sat on a rock and took out paper and charcoal and, for the first time in a month, wrote to his grandfather.

He wrote about the Academy, and Mo Ji, and Gu Lan’s recovery, and the Star Tower. He wrote that Elder Han Qiu had taken him as a disciple, and that the Elder said he cut like a good carpenter, which Grandpa would like. He wrote that he had lost a sparring match to the Third Prince and learned more from losing than from any fight he had won. He did not write about drained bodies, or blood-red threads, or a dead stone that had grown warm.

At the end, he stopped for a long time with the charcoal above the paper.

I found the record of Father going into the Valley, he wrote at last. It says he bowed to the Elder and went in. I think that sounds like him, though I don’t remember him well enough to be sure. I hope you are taking the medicine. I am sending more with this letter. Please don’t give it to the neighbours.

Your grandson, Yan.

He folded it and sat with it on his knee, looking west. Somewhere out there, three hundred li beyond the edge of the plain, lay a valley where the sky was the wrong colour and the stones were bigger than houses. In six weeks it would open. In six weeks, the Academy would send a team.

He was Ninth Vein. Zero stars. A disciple who had just been thrown on his back in front of twenty thousand people.

No one sent a Ninth Vein disciple into Fallen Star Valley.

Three days later, when the bandages came off and his ribs had knitted clean, he climbed the hill to Han Qiu’s courtyard before dawn.

The Elder was sitting at the stone table under the plum tree in the dark, as if he had been waiting all night. Perhaps he had.

“You said that when my ribs were healed, we’d talk about what a Star Sea costs,” Gu Yan said.

“I did.” Han Qiu did not invite him to sit. “Opening a Star Sea is not like opening a vein. A vein is a door in a wall. You break the door, and the room beyond it was always there. A Star Sea is not a room. It’s a new space that did not exist inside you until you made it. You take all nine of your meridians, every thread of qi you have, and you pour them into the dantian at once, and you force the dantian to become something it was never meant to be.” He looked up. “If you fail, the qi has nowhere to go. It turns back on the meridians and tears them. Some cultivators survive that. Some cripple themselves. A few die. Most people wait three years at Ninth Vein to make sure their foundation will hold.”

“My foundation will hold.”

“How do you know?”

Gu Yan thought about the starlight in his bones, the three lit nodes in a row, the grid in the dead stone pressing its pattern into his channels. He thought about the spear-butt hitting his chest and the sky very blue above him. He thought about four entries in a ledger, and a valley that would open in six weeks.

“I know how it’s joined,” he said.

Han Qiu looked at him for a long time in the dark.

Then he rose from the stone table, walked to the back of the courtyard, and laid his hand on a section of the rock face behind the plum tree that Gu Yan had always taken for bare stone. Lines blazed under his palm, a formation Gu Yan had never noticed, so fine and so old that even his eyes had slid past it. The rock split along a seam and swung inward. Beyond it, a narrow stair led down into darkness.

“My closed room,” said Han Qiu. “Nobody has used it in thirty years. The last person who did was me.” He stepped aside. “Eat nothing today. Drink water. Settle your mind. Come back at sunset.”

“Elder—”

“You asked what a Star Sea costs,” said Han Qiu. “Tonight, we find out.”
