On the morning of the final, the Arena of Ten Thousand Steps was so full that people sat on the hillsides above the topmost tier, and on the roofs of the hawkers’ stalls, and in the branches of the pines.

Su Lingxi won third place at the first bell. Her opponent, the Duke of Pingyang’s heir whom Lu Mingyuan had beaten in four exchanges, lasted somewhat longer against her and then found nine needles of starlight humming in his meridians, and conceded with good grace. She bowed to the royal pavilion, and to the envoy, and walked back to the Starfall box, and sat down beside Gu Yan without a word. Under the cover of her sleeve, she pressed something small and cold into his hand. It was a single needle, unfired.

“For luck,” she said, looking straight ahead. “It isn’t really. Don’t throw it.”

He put it in his collar.

The platform had been rebuilt overnight. Its jade was new and very white, and the four formation pillars around it had been re-carved by royal masters, and Gu Yan could see, when he let the lines come, that every strand of them was tight in its mortise.

Cang Yi was waiting at its centre.

He wore no armour. He wore the plain dark-blue training robe of a Starfall inner-court disciple, the same robe he had worn in their exhibition match at the academy, and he held his red spear: a long, straight shaft lacquered the colour of cinnabar and bound in bronze, with a narrow leaf-shaped head of dark steel. Gu Yan had seen it a hundred times. It had touched his throat once.

His board read: Cang Yi. Third Prince of the Cang Kingdom; Starfall Academy, inner court. Peak Star Sea. Stars: 7.

Gu Yan’s read as it always had. Late Star Sea. Stars: 0.

They bowed. The judge — the Principal himself, today, standing at the platform’s edge with his hands folded in his sleeves — said only, “Begin,” and stepped back.

Neither of them moved.

Gu Yan had thought a great deal, in three days of lying in the physicians’ pavilion with his side stitched shut, about how Cang Yi fought.

The prince did not read lines the way Gu Yan did. But he read something. When he fought, his spear arrived not where you were but where you were going to be, and it was there first, waiting. Mo Ji had called it seeing the future. Gu Yan did not think it was. He thought it was simpler and harder than that: Cang Yi understood, more deeply than anyone Gu Yan had ever met, that every movement had to go somewhere. And he simply stood at the end of the road.

So Gu Yan did not move along a road.

He came forward without a line. He had learned it from Ye Han and Lu Mingyuan: choose, don’t follow. He chose a cut that went nowhere sensible, high and wide, aimed at nothing, and at the last instant chose again and turned it into a thrust at Cang Yi’s lead hand.

The spear was there. It knocked the thrust aside with a sound like a struck bell.

He chose again. Low cut, then a turn into a rising slash. The spear was there. He feinted with his whole body, committed to the feint, and abandoned it a hair before contact. The spear was there.

But Cang Yi’s feet had moved.

It was only half a step. Gu Yan doubted anyone in the stands had seen it. But in their exhibition match at the academy Cang Yi had barely shifted his stance, and in the round of sixteen he had not moved his feet once, and now, in the fifth exchange, he had had to.

Cang Yi had seen it too. Something came into his face — not alarm, but a kind of bright, contained pleasure, like a man who has opened a book and found it better than he expected.

“Good,” he said. And then he came forward.

The next hundred exchanges were fought at a speed Gu Yan had never reached before.

He would not remember them clearly afterwards. He would remember the sound — the bell-note of iron on steel, over and over, so fast it became a single ringing hum. He would remember the spear’s head flickering in his eyes like a moth around a lamp. He would remember that every time he chose a line, the spear was already arriving, not at the end of it now but along it, meeting him halfway; that Cang Yi had begun, too, to choose rather than wait. And he would remember the moment, perhaps sixty exchanges in, when the prince’s spear-shaft took him across the healing wound in his side, and the stitches tore, and he felt warm blood soak his robe and thought, distantly, the physicians are going to be very cross.

He gave as well as he got. The iron sword opened a cut on Cang Yi’s forearm, then a second across his collarbone. Once, cutting with real intent, Gu Yan drove the spear-shaft so hard aside that Cang Yi was turned half around, and for an instant his back was open — and the spear-butt came round on its own like a tail and cracked Gu Yan across the knee before he could take it.

The crowd had stopped making separate noises. It was one sound now, rising and falling with the exchanges, like breath.

At the hundred and twelfth exchange they broke apart, both bleeding, both breathing hard, and stood three zhang apart in the centre of the platform.

“You’re still holding back,” said Cang Yi.

“So are you.”

“Yes.” The prince wiped blood from his eyes with the back of his wrist. “I’m going to stop now. You should too.”

He set the butt of his spear on the jade.

It was not a domain. Cang Yi was Peak Star Sea; he would not have a domain for years. But it was the thing a domain grows from — intent, pure and deep and long-practised, poured out until it filled the air. Gu Yan felt it settle over the platform like a mountain settling over a valley. The jade under his feet grew heavy. The air grew heavy. Every motion he thought of making seemed, before he made it, to run into something vast and immovable that had already decided where he would be stopped.

Spear intent. The intent of a mountain that does not move, and of a spear that is the mountain’s single point.

The seven stars projected above Cang Yi’s head blazed azure, all at once.

Gu Yan felt the crack in his seal widen.

He felt it as one feels a joint give under load. The pressure of Cang Yi’s intent was pressing in on him from every side, and his own Star Sea was pressing back — and the seal between them, the Remnant’s grey lid that had held for two months, was the weakest part of the structure. A thread of silver leaked through. Then a second.

Don’t hold anything back. I’ll know if you do.

The spear came.

It came along every line at once. That was how it felt. There was nowhere it was not going to be. And Gu Yan did the only thing left: he opened the door Han Qiu had told him to keep shut, and stepped through space.

The world folded. He came out of it a pace to the right of the spear’s point, his sword already moving for the prince’s open side —

— and the spear was there.

It had been waiting. Of course it had. Cang Yi had been in the Valley; Cang Yi had watched the Ye Han fight. He had known about the step for months, and he had known that Gu Yan, pressed to the wall, would use it, and he had simply stood at the end of that road too. The spear’s head came across and took Gu Yan in the right shoulder, not deep, but hard enough to spin him and drop him to one knee, and the pain of the step and the pain of the wound arrived in his skull together like two hammers meeting.

The seal broke.

Silver came out of him.

He did not choose it. It simply poured out, the way water pours from a burst dam — out of his Star Sea, through his meridians, through his skin, a cold clear light like the light of stars in a winter sky. On the platform it threw his shadow and Cang Yi’s in nine directions at once. And above his head, where the arena’s formation had for six days projected nothing at all, nine points of light appeared.

Nine stars. Not grey. Silver. Arranged not in a scattered constellation like every other cultivator’s, but in a perfect square frame, three by three.

Ten thousand steps of people drew breath together, and for a moment the arena was the quietest place in the world.

In the royal pavilion, the envoy of the Taixu Holy Land set down his teacup and stood.

Gu Yan did not see any of it. He was on one knee on the white jade with blood in his eyes, and the silver was roaring through him, and in his sea of consciousness the Lattice had lit up as it had not lit up since the Valley — every line of the frame burning, every lit node blazing. He could see the platform. He could see the spear. He could see Cang Yi, lifting it for the last time, his seven azure stars gathering into a single point of light at its head.

And between them, for the first time, he could see the thread.

It ran from the point of the iron sword to the point of the spear. It ran from his eyes to Cang Yi’s eyes. It ran from where he knelt to where Cang Yi stood. It was not a line in either of them, nor in the stone, nor in the air. It was the line between — the connection that existed because two things faced each other, the path that any blow between them had to travel, and it had always been there. In every fight he had ever fought, it had always been there. He had been reading the ends of it, and never the thread itself.

Every cut is a line chosen, Han Qiu had said. But no line runs from nowhere. Every line runs from one point to another, and whoever holds the thread between them holds both ends.

The sixth node lit.

It lit beside the fourth and the fifth, in the middle row of the frame, and when it lit, the whole row rang. Starlight, what the stars remember; space, the sliver he had touched in the Valley; and now the thread that tied one point to the next — and the three of them resonated together like the strings of a single instrument, and Gu Yan’s perception leapt outward with a force that nearly knocked him flat. For a heartbeat he could see the thread between himself and Cang Yi, and between Cang Yi and the Principal, and between the Principal and the King, and between every person in the Arena of Ten Thousand Steps and every other, ten thousand threads, a hundred thousand, a net of silver laid over the world.

Then Cang Yi’s spear came, along the thread, with everything the prince had.

Gu Yan rose to meet it.

He did not dodge it. He did not parry. He laid the edge of the iron sword along the thread itself — along the line the spear had to travel to reach him — and slid down it, inside the spear’s path, the way a carpenter’s saw runs down a chalk line. The spear-shaft grazed his blade. The spear-head went past his heart by the width of a finger, and its point tore through his robe and the skin beneath it and stopped there, a drop of blood welling bright around it.

The iron sword had stopped at Cang Yi’s throat.

Neither of them moved.

The Principal walked onto the platform. He looked at the spear, and at the sword, and at the drop of blood on Gu Yan’s chest and the thin red line on Cang Yi’s neck where the iron’s edge had touched it a hair’s breadth before the spear-point had touched skin. He looked for a long time.

“The sword arrived first,” he said. “By a thread.”

Cang Yi lowered his spear.

He was bleeding from the forearm and the collarbone and the neck, and his seven stars had dimmed above him to embers, and he had just lost the Grand Tournament of the Cang Kingdom before his father, his brothers, ten thousand of his people and the envoy of the Taixu Holy Land. He looked at Gu Yan. He looked at the nine silver stars still burning above Gu Yan’s head in their perfect square.

And he smiled — a wide, unguarded, boyish smile, the first Gu Yan had ever seen on him.

“There you are,” said the Third Prince.

The arena broke open like a storm.

Gu Yan barely heard it. He was already turning inward, gathering the silver back, hauling it down into his Star Sea with every scrap of will he had left and pressing the Remnant’s seal back over it, piece by broken piece, the way one shoves shutters closed against a gale. The nine stars above his head flickered and greyed and faded out. The light drained from his skin.

But it was too late for that, and he knew it. Ten thousand people had seen.

And in his ear, through the roar, so quiet and so close that it seemed to come from inside his own head, a mild and slightly tired voice said: Mend that seal before tonight’s stars come out, boy. I am not the only one who looks up.

Gu Yan lifted his head. In the royal pavilion, Qin Shang was sitting down again, and reaching for his tea.
