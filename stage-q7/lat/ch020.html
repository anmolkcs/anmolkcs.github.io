The plum yard at dawn was grey and cold, and the old trees had begun to drop their leaves.

They came down slowly in the windless air, one at a time, brown-gold and curling at the edges, and lay on the beaten earth like scattered coins. Forty disciples stood among them in four silent rows. Not the hundred and fifty of the first lesson; over the past month, Elder Han Qiu had sent most of those away, one at a time, with a word or a look. Those who remained had learned to arrive before dawn and stand without moving. Nobody sat.

At the edge of the yard, on the low wall by the gate, a slight figure in pale blue sat with a small notebook on her knee and a brush in her hand. Two or three disciples had glanced at her when they came in. Su Lingxi had looked back at each of them with such calm, flat interest that none of them glanced a second time.

Han Qiu came through the plum trees as the sky turned from grey to white. He walked to the middle of the yard and stood there, and the edge in the air came with him, thin and cold as frost on a blade.

“Once a year,” he said, “I hold this trial. Most years, no one passes. That is not because the trial is difficult. It is because most of you do not yet understand what a sword is.” He looked up into the branches above him. “Today you will show me whether you do.”

He raised one hand and flicked a finger.

There was no sound. But in the tree above him, a single branch shivered, and a dozen leaves let go and came drifting down.

“Each of you will step forward in turn,” said Han Qiu. “A leaf will fall. Cut it.” He paused. “Do not touch it.”

A murmur went through the rows.

“Do not touch it with your blade. Do not touch it with your qi. Cut it.” His flat eyes moved over them. “Anyone who sends a qi-blade at a leaf will be sent back to the outer lessons. I can make a leaf burst into pieces with a qi-blade from thirty paces. So can any 5th Vein swordsman with a decent technique. That is not cutting. That is throwing a stone very hard.”

He stepped back to the edge of the yard.

“Begin.”

The first disciple was a tall girl from the lake country, one of the best in the class. She stepped forward, drew, and waited. Han Qiu flicked his finger; a leaf fell. She watched it come down, and as it passed level with her eyes she swept her blade in a horizontal arc a hand’s breadth beneath it, very fast, with no qi in it at all, so that the wind of the stroke caught the leaf and sent it spinning.

It did not cut. It only spun, and landed on the earth whole.

“No,” said Han Qiu.

She bowed, and stepped back, her face tight.

The second tried the same, faster. The third tried a slower, more careful stroke. The fourth, unable to help himself, let a thread of qi slip along his blade at the last instant, and the leaf burst into brown flakes, and Han Qiu said, “Go,” in a voice that made the boy flinch, and the boy went.

One by one, they stepped forward. One by one, the leaves fell whole, or spinning, or shredded. Han Qiu said “No” twenty times, and “Go” six times, and nothing else.

Gu Feng was the twenty-seventh. He stood longer than any of the others before the leaf fell, his eyes half-closed, his breathing slow; and when he struck, his blade went past the leaf so close that the leaf trembled along its whole length as though something had brushed it. But when it landed, it was whole.

Han Qiu was silent for a moment. Then he said, “No. But closer.” And Gu Feng, who had gone very white, let out his breath and bowed, and walked back to his place with his fists clenched and his eyes shining.

Gu Yan was the thirty-fourth.

He had been watching. Not the disciples; the leaves. He had watched them through the sight, at the barest edge of his strength, as each one fell; and he had seen what every one of them was.

A plum leaf was a structure. Like a roof, like a pillar, like a spear-art. A single strong line ran down its centre from the stem to the tip, and from that midrib, smaller lines branched out to either side like the ribs of a boat, and from those ribs, finer lines again, branching and branching, until the whole leaf was a net of threads so fine the eye could not see them. And all of them, every one, had been dried by the autumn and weakened, so that the whole leaf now held together only by habit.

The disciples had all been trying to cut through the leaf. Across its lines. Like a man trying to saw a plank across the grain with a blunt blade. And a leaf in the air did not resist, so it did not cut; it simply moved away.

But a chisel did not cut across the grain. A chisel found the grain and followed it.

He stepped forward. He drew his father’s sword. He did not raise it. He held it low, angled, the point toward the earth, his grip exactly as he would grip a chisel set against a board, and his eyes on the branch above.

“You hold it like a chisel,” said Han Qiu, very quietly, from the edge of the yard. It was not a criticism this time. It sounded almost like a question.

Han Qiu’s finger moved. The leaf fell.

Gu Yan watched it come.

It turned slowly in the still air, edge over edge, showing him its face and then its back. Its lines lit up in the sight, the midrib, the branching ribs, the dried web of finer threads between them. He did not look at it as a thing falling. He looked at it as a thing joined. As Grandpa looked at a piece of timber before he put an edge to it, turning it in the light, finding the line along which, if he struck, the wood would part as though it had always wanted to.

There. The midrib. Dried, weakened, holding the two halves of the leaf together along a single line, the line on which everything else depended.

He did not swing the sword.

He chose.

That was all it was. He chose that line, the way a carpenter chooses where the cut will go before the chisel ever touches the wood; chose it so completely, with his whole mind and eye and the arm and wrist and fingers behind the blade, that for an instant the line in the leaf and the line of his intention and the line of the iron edge in his hand were not three things but one.

The sword moved. A small motion. A single short stroke, upward, angled, the point passing perhaps two fingers’ breadth from the falling leaf without touching it.

The leaf fell to the earth in front of him.

It fell in two halves.

They lay side by side on the brown ground, curling slightly, divided precisely along the midrib from stem to tip, as cleanly as if they had been sliced with a razor. The cut edges were not torn. They were not shredded. Each half was whole in itself, its own lines intact, as if the leaf had simply decided to become two.

The plum yard was absolutely silent.

Gu Yan stood with the iron sword in his hand and stared at the two halves of the leaf. His arm was trembling. Not from effort; there had been almost no effort. From something else. For that one instant, when the three lines had been one, he had felt something run out from him along the blade and beyond it, thin and cold and very sharp, like a thread drawn tight. It was gone now. He could not find it. He could not even remember properly what it had felt like, only that it had been there.

A flicker. A spark in a dark room.

There was a soft sound from the wall by the gate: a brush, dropped onto stone.

Elder Han Qiu walked across the yard. He crouched, slowly, and picked up the two halves of the leaf, one in each hand, and held them up to the light.

He looked at them for a long time.

“How many disciples in this Academy,” he said at last, “do you suppose could do this?”

Gu Yan did not know what to say. “I don’t know, Elder.”

“Inner court disciples at Star Sea, with sword intent cultivated for five years. A handful. Most of them would shred it.” Han Qiu set the halves down on the earth, very gently, side by side. He stood. “You are at 5th Vein. You have never been taught sword intent. You do not know what sword intent is. You held a sword like a chisel and cut a leaf along its grain from two fingers away.” His long, severe face was quite expressionless. “Do you understand what you did?”

“No, Elder.”

“Good. Neither did I, the first time.” He looked at Gu Yan for a moment longer, and then he turned and spoke to the yard. “Sword intent is not a technique. It is not qi. It is not strength. It is the will of the swordsman, made sharp enough to cut on its own. Every sword art in the world is only a way of carrying it. Most swordsmen spend their whole lives trying to make their will sharp. They sharpen it like a blade, by grinding.” He glanced down at the leaf. “This one did not sharpen anything. He simply chose where to cut. A cut is a line chosen. Remember that. All of you.”

He turned back to Gu Yan.

“Kneel.”

Gu Yan knelt on the cold earth among the fallen leaves.

“I am Han Qiu,” said the Elder, “of the 6th Seal of Heaven Seal. I teach the sword in Starfall Academy. In twenty-two years I have taken three disciples, and I have recorded none for the last eleven.” He paused. “As of today, you are my recorded disciple. You are not my true disciple. That will depend on what you do. But from this morning you will come to me for instruction, and when you are in trouble, you will say my name, and any elder in this Academy who hears it will know who has an interest in you.” His voice did not soften at all. “Do not make me regret it.”

Gu Yan bowed until his forehead touched the earth. “Master.”

“Elder,” said Han Qiu. “Until I say otherwise. Get up.”

He got up. The rows of disciples were staring at him. Gu Feng was staring hardest of all; and then, slowly, Gu Feng’s tight face cracked, and something came into it that was not envy. He put his fist into his palm and bowed, the formal salute of the Gu clan yard, and held it.

On the wall by the gate, Su Lingxi had picked up her brush again. She was writing very fast, and she did not look up, and Gu Yan saw that her ears had gone faintly pink.

“The trial is over,” said Han Qiu. “Those who did not pass: come back next year. Gu Feng. Stay a moment. I want to talk to you about how close you came.”

Mo Ji found out before breakfast. By the end of breakfast, the whole fourteenth building knew. By noon, Gu Yan had been congratulated by people he had never met, glared at by several more, and asked to fix two stools.

“Recorded disciple,” Mo Ji kept saying, in a tone of wonder. “Of Han Qiu. The boy in room eight says his uncle says Han Qiu once refused a duke’s son for three years running, and the duke offered to build a new sword hall.”

“He didn’t take the hall?”

“He said the old one had a good draught.” Mo Ji shook his head. “I’m rooming with Han Qiu’s disciple. People will think I’m important. I’ll have to start walking slowly.”

“You’ll never manage it.”

“I’ll manage it for a day.”

That night Gu Yan sat on the roof again, without drawing starlight, and simply looked at the sky, at the faint stars through the haze of the lanterns, at the place where somewhere, impossibly far away, a pillar rose through the ceiling of the world. He thought about lines. About three of them becoming one. A cut is a line chosen. He felt the Lattice at the back of his mind, the nine nodes in their square, and it seemed to him that one of the seven empty ones, the one beside the second, was no longer quite so dark. As if something in it were listening.

He went to bed late and slept deeply, for the first time in days.

The bell woke him.

Not the dawn bell. It was still dark. This bell was harsh and fast and went on and on, a sound he had never heard in the Academy before, clanging out from the outer court’s watchtower and echoing off the hillside, and under it, faintly, people were shouting.

Mo Ji was already at the window. “Something at building six,” he said. “Lanterns. Lots of them.”

They pulled on their robes and ran.

There was a crowd in the narrow lane between buildings five and six, disciples in their nightclothes with lanterns held high, and a ring of grey-robed deacons holding them back. Gu Yan pushed through until he could see past the deacons’ shoulders.

A disciple lay on the flagstones at the mouth of the lane.

He was a young man in the blue-grey of the outer court, perhaps nineteen, lying on his back with his arms flung out. His eyes were open. His face was the colour of old wax, and his cheeks had fallen in, and his lips had drawn back from his teeth, and the skin of his hands was dry and creased like paper that has been wetted and left in the sun. He looked less like a dead man than like a dead man’s husk.

There was no wound on him anywhere. Not a drop of blood on the stones.

Gu Yan did not mean to open the sight. It opened by itself.

The dead disciple’s meridians were empty. Not broken. Not torn. Empty, every one of them, like the dry beds of streams, and the fine web of lines that ran through every living body was there still but collapsed, flattened, sucked hollow from the inside, exactly as the lines had been collapsed in the beast he had found in the Black Pine Forest, when the hunters were still talking about red eyes and nobody yet knew what was coming.

Drained.

And on the stones beside the body, so faint that no one else would ever see it, curled one single thread of dark red, already fading, like a hair dropped by something that had leaned close, and fed, and gone.
