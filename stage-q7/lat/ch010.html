The patriarch found them on Crow Hill an hour before dawn.

He came up the slope alone, his grey robe torn and black with the rhino’s blood, and stopped at the summit and looked for a long time without speaking: at the fallen pine split cleanly down its length, at the two halves of the man in the crimson robe, at Captain Zhao Rui sitting against a stone with his withered hand bound against his chest, at Gu Feng kneeling beside the broken pieces of his spear. And at Gu Yan, who had not moved from where he sat, holding his grandfather’s head in his lap.

“He’s alive,” Gu Yan said, before anyone could ask.

Gu Hong knelt and put two fingers to the old man’s wrist and held them there. His face did not change, but after a while he let out a slow breath.

“Barely,” he said. “What did he do?”

“He killed that man.”

The patriarch looked at the line of ruin that ran through the summit and the tree and on out into the dark valley, where, Gu Yan would learn in the morning, a straight black scar had been drawn across three fields and through the corner of a stone barn. He did not ask how. Perhaps he knew. He had been a young man in this clan when Gu Shouyi was its strongest cultivator.

“Ten years,” Gu Hong said quietly, to no one. “And he still had that in him.” Then he stood, and was the patriarch again. “Carry him home. Gently. Zhao, can you walk? Good. Feng.” His grandson looked up. “Search the body.”

It was Gu Feng who found the token.

It was tucked inside the dead man’s robe on a cord around his neck: a crescent of dark red jade the length of a finger, smooth and cold, with a single character carved on its face that none of them could read, and on the reverse, in common script, a number. Outer Hall. Forty-one.

The patriarch turned it over in the grey light of dawn, in the courtyard of the ancestral hall, where the dead from the night were laid out under white sheets. There were eleven of them. Nine guards and two farmers. It would have been many more, the townsfolk were already saying, if the tide had not broken all at once in the middle of the night, and nobody knew why it had, except four people, and they were not saying.

“Blood Moon,” said Gu Hong.

He said it in the way a man might say the name of a disease.

“The Blood Moon Sect. In the mountains west of the capital. Far west.” He closed his hand over the token. “They harvest blood essence. From beasts, from men, from anything with life in it. The royal court has been hunting them for two hundred years and has never once found their gate.” He looked at the white sheets. “Outer Hall. Forty-one. That was not even one of their elders. That was a servant.”

Nobody spoke.

“I will write to the prefecture,” the patriarch said, “and the prefecture will write to the capital, and the capital will send someone in three months to ask questions we have already answered.” He tucked the token into his sleeve. “And the Academy envoy is arriving in four days. So. We will clean the streets.”

His grandfather woke on the second day.

He did not wake for long. He opened his eyes in the workshop, where they had laid him on Gu Yan’s own cot carried down from the loft, and looked at the beams overhead, and then at his grandson sitting beside him, and said, “Did they bury the door?”

“What door?”

“The granary door. I put a door on a man once in the Year of the Ox to carry him home and they buried him with it. Waste of good elm.” He closed his eyes. “Don’t let them bury anything of mine.”

Then he slept again.

The clan physician came twice a day. He was a careful, honest man, and on the third day he took Gu Yan out into the yard and told him the truth: that Elder Shouyi’s meridians, which had been crippled ten years ago, had now very nearly been destroyed; that what he had done on Crow Hill had burned most of what was left; that he would live, probably, for a while, if he was careful, if he did not lift anything heavier than a cup of tea, if he did not do anything like that ever again.

“How long is a while?” said Gu Yan.

The physician looked at him with pity and said he did not know.

The envoy of Starfall Academy arrived on the fourth day, at noon, in a carriage drawn by two horses that were not quite horses.

They were the size of ordinary carriage horses, but their coats were the soft grey-blue of a winter sky, and their manes moved slightly when there was no wind, and where their hooves struck the cobbles of the gate street they left faint marks of frost. Spirit horses, Gu Lan breathed, hanging off Gu Yan’s arm at the front of the crowd. Grade-2. Each one worth more than the Gu clan’s whole tea crop.

The envoy was a lean, dry, sharp-eyed woman of about forty in a plain dark blue robe with a silver star embroidered on its breast, who introduced herself to the patriarch as Deacon Fang Ru of the Outer Court, and then, with the air of a woman who had come a long way and intended to leave soon, asked to see the candidates.

She examined them in the ancestral hall. It did not take long.

She held Gu Lan’s wrist for ten breaths and said, “8th Forging, good foundation, needs discipline,” and Gu Lan glowed. She held Gu Feng’s for ten breaths and said, “9th Forging, solid, spear arm stronger than the other, fix that,” and Gu Feng, who had been told the same thing by everyone in the clan for three years, bowed with commendable composure.

She held Gu Yan’s wrist, and frowned.

“1st Vein,” she said. “Open very recently. Torn open, not coaxed. You did this under pressure?”

“Yes, Deacon.”

“Hm.” She did not let go. Her fingers were cool and very still, and he felt a thread of her qi slip into his meridian like a fine needle, and travel up his arm, and touch the bones, and stop. Her eyebrows rose a fraction. He did not know what she felt there. He kept his breathing even, and his mind well away from the frame.

“Your bones are dense,” she said at last.

“I’m a carpenter’s grandson, Deacon. I lift timber.”

She looked at him for a moment longer, with an expression that suggested she had heard a great many explanations in her time and found this one only moderately entertaining. Then she let go.

“All three,” she said to the patriarch. “We leave at dawn the day after tomorrow, and join the eastern caravan at the prefecture city. I’ll want a full account of this Blood Moon business before then, Patriarch. And that token.”

On his last night in Jade Ridge, Gu Yan carried his grandfather out into the yard.

The old man had asked for it. He weighed almost nothing now; Gu Yan lifted him in his arms like a child and carried him out through the workshop door and set him on a pallet of quilts by the step, propped against the wall, where they had eaten their millet porridge every fine evening for ten years. It was a clear night. The cold had come down from the mountains, sharp and clean, and the stars were out in their heaps.

They sat for a long time without speaking. Below them, the town was quiet. The fires on the outer farms had been put out three days ago. Someone, somewhere, was playing a bamboo flute, badly, the same four notes again and again.

“Are you still doing it?” his grandfather said. “The roof?”

“Every night.”

“Do it here. I want to see.”

Gu Yan hesitated. Then he sat down cross-legged on the cold earth of the yard and closed his eyes.

The frame turned to the sky as it always did, and the starlight came down.

It still hurt. It would always hurt, he had come to think; that was not something that would change. But it was different tonight. Since the first meridian had opened on Crow Hill, the qi of the world had been moving through him, faintly, constantly, like breath. And tonight, when the starlight came down through the frame and into his bones, it met the qi on the way.

He watched them meet. He watched the silver lines of starlight pass into his body and the qi flow through the open meridian and mingle with them, and he watched the joints in his bones, the thousands of silver points the starlight had set there over these last weeks, take the mingled light and hold it. Not just hold it. Arrange it. Each point drew a little toward the next, the way the blocks and arms of a bracket set draw together under weight, until the bones of his arms and his spine and his legs were no longer a collection of points but a frame. A structure inside his body, like a building’s beams, joined at every point, carrying the starlight the way a bracket carries a roof.

He had been thinking of it, all these weeks, as something the starlight was doing to him. It was not. It was something his body was doing with the starlight. The bones were timber. The sky was where the timber came from. And the Lattice in his mind was the hand that joined them.

The body is a frame. Starlight is what it is built from.

In the black sea behind his eyes, the second node along the top row of the frame, the one that had flickered and stuttered for weeks, caught, and held.

Silver. Steady. Beside the first, so that the top-left corner of the frame and the point beside it both glowed, and the line between them, the first line of the frame to be lit along its whole length, ran bright as a drawn wire. And warmth ran from that line down into his body, into the frame of starlight in his bones, and he felt it lock into place. He felt the Starbone settle into him, not as a thing he had to endure each night but as a thing that was simply his, the way his hands were his.

He opened his eyes.

His grandfather was looking at him. The faint silver sheen of starlight lay over Gu Yan’s skin and was sinking into him, quietly, without pain, and in the old man’s face there was no fear tonight. Only a kind of exhausted wonder.

“Two,” Gu Yan said. “Two of the nine.”

“Hm,” said Gu Shouyi. He fumbled at the front of his robe with his good hand, and after a while drew out a packet: thin, folded, sealed with a blob of black wax, and wrapped in oiled silk against the damp. He held it out.

“This,” he said, “is from your father.”

Gu Yan did not take it at once. He looked at it, the plain paper, the black seal.

“He left it with me the night he left. He said it was for you. Not to be opened when you were grown, or when you were strong, or when I died. He was very particular.” The old man’s mouth twisted, half a smile. “He said, give it to him when he leaves home, and tell him to open it when he can see the sky’s edge.”

“The sky’s edge.”

“I don’t know what it means. I’ve had ten years to think about it, and I still don’t know.” He pressed the packet into Gu Yan’s hand and closed the boy’s fingers around it with his own. “But I think you will.”

Gu Yan held the letter. It weighed almost nothing.

“Come back,” his grandfather said. “When you’ve seen it. Whatever it is. Come back and tell me.”

“I will.”

“Good.” The old man leaned his head against the wall and closed his eyes, and after a while, under the heaped and endless stars, he slept.

They left at dawn.

The whole compound came to the gate to see them off, and a good part of the town, and Gu Lan’s mother wept, and Gu Lan pretended not to, and Gu Feng’s father clasped his son’s forearm without a word and then embraced him, which the clan would talk about for a month. Captain Zhao Rui came with his left hand bound in black cloth and saluted them with his right. Instructor Gu Tieshan gave each of them a packet of dried meat and told them that if they embarrassed the clan in front of Starfall Academy he would hear about it, and walk eight hundred li to the capital, and personally hit them with a stick.

The old man did not come to the gate. He had said his goodbye in the yard.

Gu Yan had his father’s sword at his hip and a bundle on his back with two changes of clothes, a set of chisels, the battered manual of the Flowing Cloud Sword, and, wrapped in oiled silk at the very bottom, a letter with a black wax seal. He climbed into the spirit-horse carriage after his cousins. Deacon Fang Ru said a word to the horses, and they moved, smooth and silent and very fast, frost blooming in their hoofprints on the gate street, out past the north wall, out along the valley road, up the long slope toward the ridge.

At the top of the ridge, where the road crossed the pass and the whole valley of Jade Ridge lay spread out below them for the last time, Gu Yan turned on the seat and looked back.

The town was very small. The roofs of the Gu compound, the ancestral hall, the line of the walls, the black scar across the northern fields that the people were already calling the Elder’s Line. At the very edge of the compound, a single grey roof with a chimney, and a thin thread of smoke rising from it into the still morning air.

He looked past it, west and north, over the Black Pine Forest and the mountains, toward the far horizon where the sky was still pale with dawn.

And for one heartbeat, he saw it.

A line. Silver, impossibly thin, impossibly straight, finer than the finest thread of the frame in his mind. It rose from somewhere beyond the farthest mountains, so far away that the distance itself could not be measured in li, and ran straight up into the sky. Up through the dawn clouds. Up past where the cranes went. Up into the blue, and on, and on, until it vanished into the height of heaven, and he could not see where it ended.

Then he blinked, and it was gone, and there was only the morning, and the long road west.
