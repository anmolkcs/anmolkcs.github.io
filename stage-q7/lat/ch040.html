For the first nine days after the audience, Gu Yan did very little except heal.

The Academy’s physicians set his cracked collarbone and bound his ribs and frowned over his meridians, which they said were wider than a Mid Star Sea’s ought to be, in the tone people use for a roof that has somehow not fallen in. He drank their bitter medicines and slept fourteen hours a night. On the roof of the dormitory, after Mo Ji’s snoring had settled into its steady forge-bellows rhythm, he drew starlight into his bones—carefully, slowly, the Dusk-Veil Seal laid tight over his Star Sea so that not a glimmer of silver leaked out.

The stars of the Cang Kingdom were thin after the Valley’s heavy, unfamiliar ones. But they were his own, and now that the fourth node was lit, he could hear them. Faintly, at the edge of perception: the Plough, the Weaver, the Nine Lamps, each remembering where its light had been. Some of them remembered a long, long way.

Mo Ji healed faster than anyone had any right to. His hand came out of its splints on the seventh day, the fingers crooked where two of them had set badly, and he flexed them and grunted and went straight down to the Academy’s forge to hammer something until the healer came and shouted at him.

“She broke four bones,” he said to Gu Yan that night, with enormous satisfaction, holding up the crooked fingers. “Four. With one hand. And I headbutted her. I’m going to tell my children about this.”

“You don’t have children.”

“I’m going to have them so I can tell them about this.”

Gu Lan came to see them on the second day, and cried, and then hit Gu Feng in the shoulder for having cracked ribs, and then cried again. Gu Yan gave her the thing he had brought back for her: a sliver of crystal the length of his little finger, pried from the shattered body of the small dog-sized Shard Beast that Mo Ji had thrown into a wall. It was clear as water and had a single thread of gold at its heart, and when she turned it in her fingers, the gold thread did not turn with it.

“It’s shiny,” she said, holding it up to the window. “You remembered.”

“You asked.”

She looked at him over the crystal for a long moment, and her round cheerful face grew serious.

“You’re different,” she said. “Since you came back. You stand differently.” She lowered her voice. “Gu Feng won’t tell me anything. He says it’s not his to tell. What happened in there, Cousin?”

“I fell into a hole,” said Gu Yan, “and met someone who’d been waiting a long time.”

She did not ask again. That was one of the things he liked best about Gu Lan.

On the tenth day, Han Qiu sent for him.

The Elder’s quarters were at the top of a bare stone tower on the Academy’s north side, a single round room with a window facing west and nothing in it but a sleeping mat, a sword rack, and a low table. On the table, when Gu Yan entered, lay four crystals.

He knew them at once. They were Valley Hearts—the law crystals from the Shard Beasts the team had killed in the outer zone. The clearest of them, the plum-sized one with its steady gold line, was the heart of the wolf-shaped beast he and Cang Yi had brought down together on the Avenue of Giants.

“The Academy’s share of the expedition’s gains,” said Han Qiu. He was sitting cross-legged on the far side of the table, his sword across his knees. “The Formation Hall wanted all four. I told them the disciple who found the beasts’ weakness has first claim on one. They argued. I won.” He pushed the clearest crystal across the table with one finger. “That one.”

Gu Yan bowed and took it. It was cold in his palm, much heavier than it looked.

“Sit.”

He sat.

For a long time Han Qiu said nothing. The western light came in through the window and lay across the floor between them. Gu Yan waited. He had learned, over the past months, that Han Qiu’s silences were not empty. They were the Elder deciding exactly how few words he needed.

“I don’t want to know what it is,” said Han Qiu at last.

Gu Yan looked up.

“The silver. The thing you closed. Whatever let you walk out of the inner zone with your shoulder healed and a stage higher than when you fell in.” The flat, closed-door eyes held his. “If I knew, I’d have to decide what to do about it. I’m a sword teacher. I’m not wise enough to decide things like that, and I’ve seen what happens to people who think they are.” A pause. “Principal Wen asked me what happened on the plaza. I told him you found the formation’s weakness. That was true. He didn’t ask anything else. I think he doesn’t want to know either.”

“Thank you, Elder.”

“Don’t thank me. Close it tighter.” Han Qiu’s gaze dropped to Gu Yan’s chest, to his dantian, as if he could see through flesh and the fine nine-sided mesh to whatever burned beneath. “I can still feel something when I look at you closely. Not see. Feel. Like an edge under a cloth. Anyone at Heaven Seal or above who stands near you long enough and pays attention may feel it too. Most won’t pay attention. You’re a zero-star, after all.” Something that was nearly humour touched the corner of his mouth. “Stay one, for now.”

He stood, and went to the window, and looked west, toward the hills, toward the Valley three hundred li away beneath its sealed Veil.

“Your father,” he said, without turning. “The Observatory’s record. Gu Changfeng, Heaven Seal, entered the inner zone ten years ago and never came out.” A pause. “Did he come out?”

Gu Yan thought of the plumb mark on the dead Immortal’s wrist. If you are Yan’er: I am sorry.

“Not that way,” he said.

Han Qiu was quiet for a moment. Then he nodded, as if that answer were sufficient, and did not ask anything more.

He studied the Valley Heart for seven nights.

He did it on the dormitory roof, by starlight, with the Lattice open. He had known since the Avenue of Giants that the gold line in the crystal’s heart was strange—that it did not turn when the crystal turned, but stayed fixed, pointing always in the same direction, as if tied to something outside the stone. On the first night he only watched it. On the second he turned the crystal slowly in his fingers, every angle, every side, while the gold line hung motionless within like a fish holding its place in a current.

On the third night he understood that the line was not in the crystal at all.

The crystal was moving around the line. The line itself was fixed—not to the crystal, not to the roof or the Academy or the Cang Kingdom, but to something larger and deeper. It was a strand of the fallen Domain’s own space, the grid on which the Violet Dusk had once been laid out, preserved in the crystal the way an insect is preserved in amber. The crystal was a window, and through it he was looking at a single straight line of another world’s where.

On the fourth night, his nose bled so badly that Mo Ji woke up and came up the ladder and sat with him, saying nothing, handing him rags, until it stopped.

On the fifth night, he began to see the same kind of line outside the crystal.

Faintly. So faintly he thought at first that he was imagining it. Not the grain of the roof tiles or the qi in the night air, but something beneath them: the space itself, the emptiness between things, which he had always assumed was simply nothing. It was not nothing. It had a structure, like everything else. Here in the Cang Kingdom it was loose, soft, its lines spread far apart—thin, as Yu Xingzhou had said of everything in the Lower Worlds. But it had joints.

He remembered the spatial ripples of the middle zone, the way they had bent the world like a reflection in a pond. He remembered folding through them. He remembered thinking, with the needles numbing his pain, that the ripples gathered along fault lines.

Space had fault lines. Space had joints.

And a joint, his grandfather had taught him at the age of six, holding his small hands around a chisel, was simply two pieces fitted so close together that they looked like one. If you knew where to find it, you could slip a blade between them.

On the seventh night, it came together.

He was sitting on the roof with the crystal in his lap and the Lattice wide open, and the Plough was turning slowly overhead, and he was looking at a place in the air an arm’s length in front of him, where two of the soft loose lines of the Cang Kingdom’s space ran very close together. So close that between them was only a sliver. A seam. A place where here and a step from here touched without quite meeting.

He did not reach for it with his hand. He reached with the Lattice.

And the fifth node lit.

It lit sideways, if that was possible—not opening like the Starlight node, not blazing like Structure, but shifting, the way a sliding door shifts in its groove. A cool, faint, uncertain silver. And with it came understanding, narrow but true: Space. A sliver. Not the vast folding of the middle zone’s ripples. Not the walking-between-worlds that his father had attempted. Just the smallest, thinnest edge of comprehension—that the where of things was a structure too, and had joints, and that a mind which could see the joint could, for a moment, slip through it.

The five lit nodes settled into their frame. The first row was full; the second row now had two of its three. The silver lines between them thickened, and the Lattice hummed, and in his Star Sea, beneath the Veil, the fifth silver star brightened with a comprehension at last.

He did not test it that night. He was not stupid.

He tested it two days later, in the Academy’s sword yard at dawn, against Fang Rui.

Han Qiu stood at the yard’s edge with his arms folded. Fang Rui, healed now but still moving with a slight stiffness on her left side, held her practice sword in a low guard and said nothing at all, as usual. Gu Yan held his father’s iron sword.

They fought for forty exchanges. She was Peak Star Sea, a full two stages above him, and she was very, very good—fast and economical and utterly without wasted motion—and his Starbone body and his sword intent and the Lattice’s lines kept him in the fight, but only just. On the forty-first exchange she drove him back toward the wall of the yard with a sequence of short, hard cuts, and he saw the end coming: a feint high, a cut low, and nowhere to step.

So he stepped through.

He found the seam in the air beside him—a sliver, a joint in the thin loose space of the Cang Kingdom—and he pushed the Lattice into it the way he had pushed a chisel into a mortise at nine years old, and he moved.

It felt like being turned inside out and then right side out again in the space of a single heartbeat. There was no honey-thick pressure, as there had been in the Valley’s ripples. There was only a sharp, wrenching click, deep inside him, as if every bone in his body had been dislocated and reset in the same instant—and then he was standing a single pace to Fang Rui’s left, beside her, outside her guard, with his sword at her ribs.

She froze.

For a long moment, nobody in the yard moved.

Then Gu Yan’s knees gave out, and he sat down hard on the packed earth, and blood poured out of his nose onto his robe. His head felt as though someone had put a spike through it. His Starbone ached from skull to heel as if it had been wrung like wet cloth. He could not have lifted his sword again if his life depended on it.

“One pace,” said Han Qiu from the edge of the yard. His voice was very quiet.

“One pace,” Gu Yan agreed thickly, through the blood. “Once. I don’t think I can do it twice in a fight.”

“Then don’t.” The Elder unfolded his arms. “And don’t waste it on a spar again. A trick that only works once is worth everything the first time anyone sees it, and nothing after.” He looked at Fang Rui. “Senior disciple. You saw nothing.”

“I saw nothing,” Fang Rui agreed. She was looking at Gu Yan with something very close to an expression. Then she offered him her hand and pulled him to his feet.

The announcement came that afternoon.

It was read out in the great courtyard beneath the Nine-Story Star Tower, by Principal Wen Taiyuan himself, to every disciple of Starfall Academy assembled in their ranks. The old scholar’s voice was thin, but it carried to every corner of the courtyard as if he were standing at each disciple’s shoulder.

“By decree of His Majesty,” he read, “in two months’ time, at the first new moon of winter, the Cang Kingdom will hold its Grand Tournament in the capital. It is open to every cultivator of the kingdom under the age of twenty-five, of any clan, sect, school, or none.”

A murmur went through the ranks. The Grand Tournament was held once a decade. Gu Yan had heard of it in Jade Ridge as a child, the way one hears of comets.

“This year,” the Principal continued, and here he paused and looked up from the scroll, and the courtyard went silent, “the Taixu Holy Land has informed His Majesty that it will send an envoy to observe. The top three finishers of the Tournament will be examined by that envoy, and may be selected to enter the Taixu Holy Land as disciples.”

The silence broke into uproar.

Gu Yan stood in the ranks between Mo Ji and Gu Feng, and did not shout, and did not move. He was thinking of the Remnant’s thin, sour voice. Your Azure Dust is one. A middling one. Thin laws, thick sky. The Taixu Holy Land was the greatest power of the Eastern Wastes, a quarter of one continent of one middling world. A step outward. A step up.

Beside him, Mo Ji was gripping his arm hard enough to bruise. Two ranks ahead, Su Lingxi had turned her head and was looking back at him, her compass closed in her hand, her face perfectly composed.

And on the far side of the courtyard, beneath the eaves of the Hall of Merit, a slim figure in pale blue stood alone with his right arm bound in a sling of black silk.

Lu Mingyuan was not looking at the Principal. He was looking across the courtyard at Gu Yan, and when their eyes met, he smiled and inclined his head, courteous as ever, the way one greets an old acquaintance one fully intends to see again.
