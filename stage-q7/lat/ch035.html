The needles went in cold and the pain went out like a snuffed candle.

For the space of perhaps three hundred breaths, Gu Yan’s head was clear—clearer than it had been since he entered the Valley. The throbbing behind his eyes was gone. The Lattice hung in his sea of consciousness, four nodes blazing, and he could hold it wide open without bleeding. He did not waste the gift. He ran with the Lattice open, and the mist became a map.

It was full of lines. Broken, tangled, shifting lines—the Valley’s shattered laws of space, still twitching after a hundred thousand years like the nerves of a severed limb. Before, they had only given him headaches. Now he could see their pattern. The spatial ripples were not random. They gathered along fault lines in the old city’s structure, the places where the fallen Domain had cracked on impact, and they pulsed outward from those faults in slow waves, like ripples from a dropped stone.

Behind him, the smell of iron and rot was closing.

“Little mouse,” Crimson Moth called. She sounded as if she were enjoying herself. “You run well for an Early Star Sea. What’s in your bones? Starlight? I can taste it on the air behind you.”

He did not answer. Ahead, a fault line crossed the street—he could see it, a seam of twisted silver running through the paving and up the face of a ruined wall—and along it a ripple was gathering, the lines beginning to bend.

He timed it the way Grandpa had taught him to time a falling tree. Then he jumped into it.

The honey-thick pressure took him. The world folded. He came out rolling across a different stretch of paving with his ears ringing and his stomach in his throat, and when he got to his feet the mist was thinner, and the gravity was a crushing four times the outside, and in front of him, rising out of the ground like the teeth of some monstrous jaw, stood a ring of black spires.

They were enormous even by the Valley’s standards. Each was a single needle of lightless stone, smooth as glass, so tall that their points were lost in the violet sky. They stood a hundred paces apart in a circle so vast he could not see its curve. Between them the air shimmered.

And in his chest, the tug that had been calling him every night since he passed the Veil suddenly became a pull, so strong it nearly pulled him off his feet.

“There you are.”

She came out of the ripple behind him without any visible effort, her red robe settling around her like folded wings. Thirty paces. Her domain spread out ahead of her and the air thickened, hot and wet, and his limbs became heavy as wet sand.

“The spire ring,” Crimson Moth said, glancing up at it with something almost like reverence. “Do you know what happens to anyone who tries to pass between the spires? They come apart, child. Not like a blade. Like a knot being untied. My Sect sent a Heaven Seal 4th Seal through ten years ago, and all that came back out was his shadow.” Her bloody eyes turned to him. “Come here. I won’t waste you. Blood with no fate in it will make the array sing.”

He looked at the spires with the Lattice.

He had expected a wall. What he saw was a lock. The lines of the ring were tight and gleaming and immensely old, wound around and between the spires in a pattern of interlocking loops so intricate that it made his mind ache even through Su Lingxi’s needles. And at the center of each loop, where the pattern closed, there was a shape he knew.

It was a grid. Three by three. Nine nodes.

It’s keyed to the Lattice.

He did not let himself think. He turned and ran toward the gap between the two nearest spires.

Behind him, Crimson Moth shouted something sharp and wordless. A whip of red light cracked across the space where his head had been. Then the gap was in front of him, the air shimmering, and he felt the ring’s lines reach for him like the fingers of a great cold hand—

—and pause—

—and part.

He went through. He heard, behind him, the crack of her red whip striking the ring and the shriek of something tearing, and her voice, suddenly not amused at all: “What are you?”

Then the ground beyond the spires was not there, and he was falling.

He fell for a long time.

Long enough to stop being afraid, and then to start again. The inside of the spire ring was not flat ground but a pit, a vast funnel of black stone spiralling downward, and he tumbled down it, slid, struck ledges, rolled, fell again. The Starbone took each blow and rang. The gravity grew heavier with every zhang he descended. The needles’ numbness was wearing thin. By the time he struck the bottom he could not feel his left arm, and his mouth was full of blood.

He lay on his back for a while.

When he opened his eyes, he was looking up at a sky.

Not the violet sky of the Valley. Something else. The funnel opened above him into a circle of darkness so deep it seemed to have no bottom, and in that darkness hung lights. Not stars. Lanterns. Thousands of them, dim and golden and very far away, strung along threads of faint silver that crossed and recrossed in a pattern that went on, and on, and on, until his eyes could no longer follow it.

“It isn’t real,” said a voice. “It’s only a memory. I’ve been looking at it so long, you see, that it’s sunk into the stone.”

Gu Yan turned his head.

He was lying at the foot of a hill. Then his eyes adjusted, and he saw that the hill had fingers.

It was a hand. Open, palm up, the fingers curled slightly, each one as long as a Cangdu street. Beyond the hand lay an arm, and beyond the arm a shoulder rising like a ridge, and beyond that a face, turned toward the false sky, eyes closed. The body lay at the bottom of the pit as if it had been thrown there. It was clothed in robes that had once been violet and gold and were now the colour of dust. It was perhaps a hundred and sixty zhang long from crown to heel.

Its chest was open. Something had torn it from collarbone to hip, and in the wound, a hundred thousand years after its death, the blood still glistened. It was not red. It was gold, dark and thick as honey, and it gave off a faint light of its own.

Sitting on the corpse’s open palm, cross-legged, with its chin propped on one fist, was a figure no taller than Gu Yan. It was made of light. Not bright light—grey, faint, frayed at the edges like an old garment, so translucent that Gu Yan could see the corpse’s palm-lines through it. Its face was thin, sharp, and ageless, with a mouth permanently on the edge of saying something unpleasant.

“Oh,” said the figure, looking at him properly for the first time. “Oh, no.”

It began to laugh.

It laughed for a long time, high and cracked, the echoes coming back strangely from the walls. Gu Yan waited.

“Another one,” the figure gasped. “Another one. A hundred thousand years I lie in this hole waiting for the Wardens to finish the job, or for someone to come and tell me what happened to the rest of my city, and what do I get? Bandits. Blood-drinkers. Little grasping Lower World sects with their little grasping knives. And now, twice in ten years, two idiots carrying Lattice Seeds walk through my door.” It wiped its eyes, which produced no tears. “Heaven above, Heaven below. Do you have any idea what you are?”

“I have a Lattice,” Gu Yan said carefully. Speaking hurt. “My father gave it to me.”

The figure stopped laughing.

“Your father,” it said. “Yes. You have his face.” It tilted its head and looked at him with eyes that were nothing but faint grey light. “I am Yu Xingzhou, or what is left of him. Heaven Immortal of the Thirteenth Celestial Domain, called the Violet Dusk. Star-Recorder of the Ninth Observatory of the City of Shuangque. You’ve been walking through my city for ten days. I hope you wiped your feet.” He gestured at the corpse beneath him. “That was me. I was considered quite handsome.”

Gu Yan managed to sit up. His left arm hung useless. He looked from the faint grey figure to the vast body and back.

“You were ten times our size.”

“I was ten times your size. In the Violet Dusk I was perfectly ordinary. Our laws were heavier. Bodies grow to fit the weight of the laws they live in, boy—every cultivator in the Domains knows that. You Lower Worlders are small because your world is thin.” Yu Xingzhou sniffed. “No offense meant.”

“Some taken.”

The Remnant blinked, then grinned unpleasantly. “Ha. Yes. You are his.”

“Tell me what the Lattice is,” Gu Yan said.

The grin faded.

For a while the Remnant said nothing at all. He looked up at the false sky full of lanterns, and the frayed edges of his form stirred as if in a wind Gu Yan could not feel.

“Look up,” he said finally. “Look properly. With the thing in your head. You’re carrying the key to it. You might as well see the door.”

Gu Yan opened the Lattice.

The false sky answered.

The lanterns were worlds. He had understood that, dimly, in the observatory; now he saw it plainly. Each golden point was a world, and each hung from silver threads, and the threads ran upward to larger lights—vast, slow-burning lamps, violet and white and green and gold, each of which held up a cluster of the smaller ones beneath it like a chandelier holds its candles. And those larger lights hung in turn from something larger still, a darkness above them that was not empty but full, layer upon layer upon layer of structure, each layer a vast flat brightness like a continent of light, stacked one above another into heights his mind could not hold.

And everything—every lantern, every lamp, every layer—was joined by the lines. The silver frame ran through it all, endless, infinitely fine, an architecture of laws on which the whole of existence hung like fruit on a vine.

“The Heaven-Ascending Lattice,” said Yu Xingzhou softly. “Everything that is, is hung on it. No one knows who built it. The Dao Lords might, if you could find one and if they’d tell you, and they wouldn’t. Below—” a frayed hand waved at the countless golden points “—the Lower Worlds. Three thousand of them, give or take a few hundred that have died. Your Azure Dust is one. A middling one. Thin laws, thick sky.”

The hand rose.

“Above them, the Celestial Domains. One hundred and eight. Each Domain holds up some twenty-eight Lower Worlds on its strands, the way a lord holds up his villages. Immortals live there. Earth Immortals, Heaven Immortals, Golden Immortals. A Golden Immortal could take your little kingdom in his palm and blow on it, and it would be gone, and he wouldn’t think himself cruel; he’d think it was dust.” A wry twist of the mouth. “I was a Heaven Immortal. I could have blown on a province, at least.”

Higher.

“Above the Domains, the Thirty-Three Heavens. Each Heaven is one world, alone, as large as a hundred Domains, stacked one upon the next. True Gods walk there. God Kings rule. From the highest towers of the Violet Dusk, on a clear night, you could look up and see the underside of the First Heaven, and it wasn’t a sky. It was ground. Mountains, hanging upside down, so far away they looked like frost on a window.” He was silent a moment. “I used to sit and look at them. I thought I’d go there someday.”

“And above that?”

“The Origin Sea. Rumours. Beyond that—” he shrugged “—the Lattice. Going up. Forever, as far as anyone knows.”

Gu Yan was shaking. He did not know whether it was the cold, or the pain, or the size of what he was seeing. The nine nodes in his sea of consciousness, his small three-by-three frame, seemed suddenly very small indeed, a single knot in a net that covered the sky.

“What happened?” he asked. “The Shattering. What broke it?”

The false sky changed.

Yu Xingzhou did not seem to do anything, but the memory sunk into the stone shifted, and the lanterns dimmed, and one of the vast violet lamps above them—a Domain, his Domain—grew brighter, closer, until it filled the dark.

“One hundred thousand years ago,” the Remnant said, “someone walked the Lattice.”

Through the violet lamp, a single thread of silver light was moving. Upward. Fast. Passing through the Domain’s strands as if they were open doors, climbing toward the stacked brightness of the Heavens above.

“A Seed-bearer. Like you. Like your father. I never knew his name. Nobody did. He came up out of some Lower World nobody had heard of and he went through the Domains like a needle through cloth, and he kept going. Up. Toward whatever’s at the top.”

Above the climbing thread, the darkness opened.

Gu Yan felt it before he saw it, even in memory. A pressure from above like a mountain pressing down on a single grain of rice. Out of the layered brightness of the Heavens came shapes—tall, silver, faceless, armoured in something that was not metal, that was the Lattice, its lines folded and worn like mail. There were seven of them. Where they moved, the lines of the Lattice moved with them, obedient.

“Wardens,” said Yu Xingzhou. His frayed voice had gone very quiet. “That’s what we called them. They come down out of the Thirty-Three Heavens whenever a Seed wakes. They don’t speak. They don’t negotiate. I’ve never heard of anyone who saw one’s face. And they do not care, boy, what stands between them and the Seed.”

The seven silver shapes struck.

They did not strike the climbing thread. They struck the Lattice. They cut the lines around it, sheared them away, severed the strands of the frame itself to cage him—and the Violet Dusk Domain, which had hung from those strands for a million years, tore.

Gu Yan watched it happen. He watched a quarter of a world come loose. He watched cities the size of kingdoms slide off its edge, burning. He watched the climbing thread of silver light flare once, defiantly, and go out. He watched a piece of the Violet Dusk—a single city, Shuangque, its observatories and its avenues and its hundred-zhang doors—drop away through the dark, down past lantern after lantern, and plunge like a falling star through the thin bright skin of a small blue-green world.

“Sixty-one Domains lost pieces that day,” Yu Xingzhou said. “Some lost everything. Nine Lower Worlds fell entirely and are only dust now. I was in my observatory when the strands went. I remember the floor tilting. I remember the burning. I remember thinking, at least I’ll see the Lower Worlds up close.” A cracked laugh. “And here I am.”

The false sky faded back to the dim lanterns. The Remnant was looking at Gu Yan now, and there was no laughter in his face at all.

“So when I tell you that you are carrying the most dangerous thing in three thousand worlds in your skull, boy, understand that I’m not being dramatic. If that Seed wakes fully—if it opens far enough for the Lattice to feel it—they’ll come. And they will not care how many cities hang from the strand they cut to reach you.”

Gu Yan’s mouth was dry. “My father,” he said. “You said twice in ten years. You said I had his face.”

Yu Xingzhou looked at him for a long moment. Then he lifted one frayed grey hand and pointed—not at the walls of the pit, not at the way out, but straight up, through the false sky, toward the stacked and distant light.

“Your father passed here,” he said. “He sat where you’re sitting. He asked me the same questions, though more politely.” A thin smile. “And then he went up.”
