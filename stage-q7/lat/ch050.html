The ship of the Taixu Holy Land left Cangdu at dawn on the twentieth day, and the whole city came out to watch it go.

Gu Yan stood at the rail of the lowest deck with Cang Yi on one side of him and Su Lingxi on the other, and looked down at the Heavenward Plaza a thousand zhang below. From here it was a white tile in a mosaic of grey roofs. The people filling it were a fine dark sand. He could not pick out faces. He did not need to. He could hear one voice, quite clearly, rising above all the others, because it belonged to someone who had been born at a forge and had never in his life learned to speak quietly.

“GU YAN!” Mo Ji was bellowing. “TEN YEARS! YOU HEAR ME? TEN YEARS, AND I’LL COME UP THERE MYSELF, AND IF YOU’VE GOT FAT I’LL—”

The rest was lost in the wind.

“What will he do?” said Cang Yi, interested.

“Something involving a hammer, probably.”

“He should come,” said Su Lingxi. “In ten years he will be strong enough. The next envoy will take him.” She paused. “If he doesn’t walk there first.”

Gu Yan thought of Bao from the Iron Mountains, who had walked to Cangdu from a mining camp. He thought of Mo Ji’s face the night four golden stars had lit in his Star Sea. He did not think it was impossible at all.

They had said their farewells the night before, in the dormitory courtyard, with wine Mo Ji had bought at a price he refused to name. Gu Lan had cried, and then made Gu Yan promise to write once a month, and then made him promise again. Gu Feng had shaken his hand and said, “I’ll ignite before you finish that seal,” and Gu Yan had said, “I hope you do.” Han Qiu had not come to the courtyard. But at the fourth watch, when Gu Yan climbed to the roof for the last time, he had found the elder standing on the ridge of the Star Tower across the way, a lean dark shape against the stars, looking toward the cliff. Neither of them had said anything. After a while, Han Qiu had raised one hand, and gone in.

Below, the plaza fell away. The ship’s three great fans of light began to turn faster, throwing rainbows across the lifting roofs, and Cangdu tilted and shrank and became a city on a map, and then a smudge beside a silver thread of river, and then nothing at all.

The ship flew west for six days.

It was larger inside than out. Gu Yan had suspected as much the first time he saw it, with its lines folding into directions he had no words for; now he walked its decks and found it confirmed at every turn. There were corridors that went on longer than the hull. There was a courtyard on the fourth deck with a pond and a willow tree and a sky overhead that was not the real sky. There were three hundred people aboard — disciples returning from errands, deacons, crew in plain grey, and the handful of young selections from other kingdoms the envoy had already collected on his circuit: a silent girl from a desert kingdom in the south who never took off her veil, two brothers from a fishing kingdom on the coast who argued constantly in a dialect no one else understood, a boy of fifteen from the mountains who had eight stars and blushed whenever anyone spoke to him.

None of them had heard of the Cang Kingdom. Gu Yan, asking, had heard of none of theirs.

On the second day they crossed the border of the Cang Kingdom, and Gu Yan stood at the rail and watched it pass beneath: a range of brown hills, a river, a line of old watchtowers. On the other side the land looked exactly the same. On the third day they passed over a kingdom whose capital was larger than Cangdu, and on the fourth over one whose capital was smaller than Jade Ridge. On the fifth they crossed a desert so wide that it took from dawn to dusk, and that night, Su Lingxi said, they had crossed the territories of eleven kingdoms since leaving home.

“There are a hundred and eighty,” she said. “We’ve seen eleven, and a desert.”

“And the Eastern Wastes is a quarter of one continent.”

“And the continent is one of — the archives disagree. Four, or five.”

“And the world is one of three thousand.”

She was quiet. Then she said, “I used to think the Observatory roof was the highest place in the world.”

On the sixth evening, the envoy came down to the lowest deck and found the three Cang Kingdom selections at the rail, where they generally were.

“Tonight we will climb,” Qin Shang said. “There are storms over the Central Mountains that even this ship would rather not argue with, so we will go above them. Very high. Higher than most people in this world will ever go.” He looked at Gu Yan, briefly. “Those of you who wish to see something worth seeing should be on deck at dawn.”

Gu Yan did not sleep.

He stood at the prow of the highest deck all night, wrapped in a heavy cloak against a cold that grew sharper with every hour. The ship climbed. He felt it in his ears and in his chest: the air thinning, the qi thinning with it, so that even with his starbone body he had to breathe slowly and deeply to keep the dizziness away. Below, the storms over the Central Mountains spread out like a black ocean shot with lightning, the thunder arriving long after each flash, faint as the grumbling of something asleep. Then the storms were far below, and then they were only a dark floor, and above the ship there was nothing at all but stars.

They had never been so bright. They had never been so many. He lay back against the rail and looked up at them, and the Lattice stirred faintly behind his seal, and the second node drank at the starlight greedily even through the grey.

At some point Su Lingxi came and stood beside him without a word, wrapped in her own cloak, her compass in her hand. Some time after that, Cang Yi came too, and leaned on the rail, and did not speak either.

The sky to the east began to pale.

It came up slowly — first grey, then a deep and luminous blue, then rose along a thin line at the bottom of the world. And Gu Yan, who had spent his whole life looking at horizons — over the roofs of Jade Ridge, from the walls of Cangdu, across the drop of Han Qiu’s cliff — looked at this one and felt something in his chest go perfectly still.

It was curved.

Not much. If he had not been watching for it, he would not have seen it. But the line where the dark floor of the storms met the paling sky was not straight. It bent. Very gently, from far in the south to far in the north, it arched, like the rim of an enormous bowl seen from inside — and above it, as the light grew, there was something else.

It was not a cloud. It was not a colour. It was the faintest shimmer in the sky, high above the curved horizon, very far away — a sheen, like the film on a soap bubble, like the surface of water seen from beneath. It ran from horizon to horizon. It curved, too, the other way: down over the world from above, in an arch so vast that he could see only the barest hint of its bending. And when the first rays of the sun came up over the rim of the world, they struck it, and it glowed for one moment along its whole length with a thin pale line of light, like a single hair of silver drawn across the sky.

The ceiling. His grandfather had said so, on a roof in Jade Ridge, a lifetime ago.

“The World Membrane,” whispered Su Lingxi. Her compass was trembling in her hand. Its needle, which for years had shown nothing at all when she turned it toward him, was standing straight up.

Gu Yan reached into his robe and took out his father’s letter.

It was sealed with plain wax, gone dark and brittle with ten years’ keeping. He had carried it from Jade Ridge to Cangdu, into Fallen Star Valley and out again, through every round of the Grand Tournament, sewn into the lining of his robe. It weighed almost nothing.

Open it when you can see the sky’s edge.

He broke the seal.

There was one sheet inside, folded twice. The brush hand on it was strong and slanting and very fast, the hand of a man who had always been in a hurry to get somewhere. Gu Yan had not seen it since he was six years old. He knew it at once.

Yan’er,

If you are reading this, you have seen it. Good. Your grandfather will have told you not to look. He was right to, and you were right not to listen.

I am sorry I left. I am sorry for the stone. I would do both again.

Here is the one thing I have learned that is worth the paper. The world is a cell in a cage that is also a ladder.

Come up.

— your father

He read it once. Then again. The wind at the prow tugged at the paper, and he held it tight, and his eyes stung, and he told himself it was the cold.

A cell in a cage that is also a ladder.

And behind the Remnant’s seal, in his sea of consciousness, the Lattice woke.

It did not flare, as it had in the final. It did not burn. It opened.

He saw the frame he had known since he was a boy: three by three, nine nodes, six of them lit — the grain of things, the starlight in his bones, the cut; what the stars remember, the sliver of space, the thread between. He saw its silver lines, which he had always thought were its edges.

They were not edges.

They ran on. Past the border of the frame, where for ten years he had seen only darkness, the silver lines continued — faint at first, then clearer, then branching, meeting other lines, forming other nodes. Another frame of nine beside his own. Another above it. Another beyond. And beyond those, more, and more, spreading outward from the small lit square in his mind in every direction at once, frame after frame after frame, so many and so far that his mind could not hold them — not nine nodes, not ninety, not nine thousand, but a grid without end, stretching out into a darkness that was not empty at all but full, laced through and through with silver.

His nine nodes were one cell. One tiny cell, in one corner, of something infinite.

And in the grid, here and there, very far apart, things hung. He saw them only for an instant, and only as points of light, like lanterns hung on a frame on a festival night. One of them was very close — so close that it filled half his mind, a bright dim pearl wrapped in its own shimmering film. He understood, without knowing how, that he was inside it. That it was Azure Dust World, and that the shimmer around it was the Membrane he had just seen with his own eyes. And beyond it, above it, below it, strung along the endless silver lines, were others. Thousands. And far, far above them all, so remote that they were less lights than rumours of light, there were greater shapes, stacked one upon another up the scaffold into a height that had no top.

A cage that is also a ladder.

The seal over his Star Sea grew cold. Very cold. The mended scar across it ached, like an old wound before snow.

Gu Yan closed his eyes and pulled the vision back with everything he had, drawing it down, folding it away behind the grey, and his nine stars dimmed under the weave until they were nothing but a quiet glow in the dark. When he opened his eyes again, the sun was fully risen, and the shimmer in the sky had faded into ordinary blue, and Su Lingxi was holding his arm very tightly, and Cang Yi was looking at him with an expression he could not read.

“What did you see?” the prince asked quietly.

Gu Yan folded his father’s letter and put it back inside his robe, over his heart.

“The way up,” he said.

Far away — further than the ship could fly in ten thousand years, further than any light from Azure Dust World would ever reach — beyond the Membrane, beyond the three thousand Lower Worlds hanging in the dark like lanterns, beyond the hundred and eight Celestial Domains, high in the stacked law-realms of the Thirty-Three Heavens, there was a hall built of silver.

It had no doors. It had no windows. It had stood empty and silent since before the Shattering, and the dust of a hundred thousand years lay on its floor without a single footprint.

At the far end of the hall, on a throne of the same silver, there sat a figure in silver armour. It had sat there so long that the armour had grown into the throne, and the throne into the floor. Where its face should have been, there was only smooth, featureless metal, like the surface of a mirror that had never been asked to reflect anything.

Somewhere, very far below, on one small node of the endless Lattice, a single cell had lit.

The faceless Warden opened its eyes.
