“Your Highness,” Lu Mingyuan went on, still pleasant, “I’d step back if I were you. Elder Crimson Moth has agreed not to touch you, and I’d like to keep that agreement. Brother Gu is a different matter.”

Cang Yi rose from the rubble and stood in full view, his red spear held loosely at his side. “You’ve killed three of my guards tonight, Lu Mingyuan. Your uncle is out in the mist trying to kill my captain. You’ve put six of our classmates in a blood formation. And you want to talk to me about agreements?”

“I want to talk to you about the future.” Lu Mingyuan spread his hands. “The kingdom doesn’t need to know any of this. Lu Qing died bravely. The others were lost to the Valley. You and I go home and continue being the two finest young cultivators in the Cang Kingdom, and in ten years one of us is a Mandate and the other is grateful to him. Isn’t that better than dying here over a blacksmith’s son?”

Gu Yan was not listening. He was looking at the formation.

He had studied it for two hours from behind a column, the night before, with an Early Star Sea’s mind and a Lattice that bled him every time he opened it wide. He had seen its three load-bearing nodes—the iron basin at the center and the two hidden points on the rim where the red roots grew deepest—and he had seen that they protected each other. Break one, and the other two took up its load through the channels between them.

He looked now with a Mid Star Sea’s mind and nine silver stars and four lit nodes, and the formation was laid out before him like a roof seen from inside the rafters.

The nodes protect each other through the channels, he thought. So don’t break the nodes. Break the channels.

There were three. Thick red lines that ran from the basin to each rim node and between the rim nodes, forming a triangle beneath the great circle. Cut those, and each node would stand alone. And a node standing alone could be broken.

“Cang Yi,” he said, very quietly. “There’s a point under the paving, forty paces to the left of the third circle. Where the red is thickest. When I tell you, put your spear into it with everything you have. Don’t stop until it breaks.”

The prince did not turn his head. “And you?”

“The basin.”

“There’s a Late Heaven Seal standing next to the basin.”

“I know.”

“Good,” said a cold voice from the mist behind them. “Then I’ll take her.”

Han Qiu came out of the mist like a drawn blade.

He was not running. He walked, his sword still sheathed at his hip, his grey robe dark with blood that was not his own, and behind him, light-footed and breathing hard, came Su Lingxi with her compass in her hand. Gu Yan’s heart lurched at the sight of her. She met his eyes for one instant across the rubble, and something in her shoulders eased.

At the basin, Crimson Moth turned.

“Han Qiu,” she said. Her voice had gone flat. “The Academy’s sword. I’d hoped the Lu retainers would delay you longer.”

“They tried.” Han Qiu stepped down off the rubble onto the plaza. “There are fewer of them now.”

He drew.

His domain unfolded across the plaza like frost spreading over a window. Cold, clean, sharp—Gu Yan felt it against his skin as a thousand fine edges, every one pointing outward, every one held perfectly still. It met Crimson Moth’s domain of iron and rot in the middle of the formation, and where they met, the air screamed.

She was the stronger. Gu Yan could see it in the lines. Her Heaven Seals were more numerous, her qi deeper, her domain pressing Han Qiu’s back step by step. But Han Qiu’s domain did not bend. It gave ground the way a blade gives ground, by cutting through what came at it, and every step it lost cost her something.

“Su Lingxi!” Gu Yan called. “The second rim node—there, under the seventh circle. Can you freeze it?”

She did not ask how he knew. Her needles were already in her hand.

“Now,” he said. “Cang Yi—now!”

Three things happened at once.

Cang Yi leapt. His seven stars blazed in the red-stained mist, and his spear came down like a falling star, every scrap of qi he had poured into its point. It struck the paving forty paces left of the third circle and the stone exploded upward in a fountain of glassy shards, and beneath it the knot of red roots shrieked.

Su Lingxi’s needles flew. Nine of them, thin as hairs, trailing threads of pale blue light. They struck the paving above the second rim node in a perfect circle, and frost bloomed out from them across the stone, and the red light beneath went sluggish and dim.

And Gu Yan ran straight across the formation toward the basin, through the edge of Crimson Moth’s domain, with his sword raised.

Her domain hit him like a wall of wet meat. Every step was a struggle. The air was so thick with blood-intent that he could taste it, iron on his tongue, and his limbs slowed as if he were running through a flooded field. He did not stop. His body was a Mid Star Sea’s body now, Starbone-hardened, denser than it had any right to be, and the domain that had frozen Gu Feng only slowed him.

He did not go for the basin. He went for the channels.

Three red lines ran from the basin, beneath the paving, toward the three nodes. With the Lattice open he could see them as clearly as ink on paper. He reached the first and his sword came down along it—not across, along, the edge following the line of the channel the way a chisel follows the grain—and the silver star of Sword in his Star Sea blazed, and his sword intent went into the red channel like a thread drawn through a needle.

The channel split.

He turned and cut the second. The third.

The formation howled.

The three nodes, suddenly alone, tried to bear the whole weight of the array. Under Cang Yi’s spear the first one shattered. Under Su Lingxi’s frost the second cracked and went dark. And the basin—the basin was in front of him, iron and blood, with the swollen red sphere of six disciples’ life-force turning above it, and Crimson Moth’s furious face beyond.

He put his sword into the joint where the basin’s legs met its bowl, and leaned.

It broke.

The red sphere burst. It did not explode outward; it came apart, the gathered qi and blood and starlight unravelling into six bright threads that whipped back across the plaza along the broken channels, and plunged into the six disciples lying in their circles. Mo Ji’s back arched off the paving. Wei Zhen cried out. Lu Qing gasped like a man coming up from deep water.

And Crimson Moth, whose own blood had been woven into the formation at its heart, staggered as if she had been struck.

That was all Han Qiu needed.

His sword moved once. Gu Yan never saw it; he only saw the line it left in the air, cold and silver-white and perfectly straight, running from the Elder’s blade through the space where Crimson Moth stood. Her left arm and the ragged wing-sleeve that hung from it fell to the paving. She screamed—a terrible high sound, like a moth burning in a lamp.

Gu Yan was turning toward Mo Ji when Lu Mingyuan hit him.

He had forgotten about the heir of the Lu clan. Lu Mingyuan had not forgotten about him. The palm came from his blind side and struck him under the ribs with the force of a falling beam, and Gu Yan felt bones crack and went sliding across the paving with his breath gone.

“I did think,” said Lu Mingyuan, walking after him, “that you were simply odd. A zero-star with clever eyes. I’ve known clever people with no power before. They’re useful.” He flexed his fingers. Faint red threads stirred at their tips, thin as hair, seeking. “But that was not a zero-star’s sword. Was it?”

Gu Yan got his feet under him. His ribs screamed. “No.”

“What are you?”

“A carpenter’s grandson.”

Lu Mingyuan smiled, and came at him.

He was fast. Faster than Gu Yan, by a margin—he was Late Star Sea, a full stage above, six stars blazing pale blue, and his palms moved in the long, rolling, deceptive arcs of some Earth-grade water technique that made them seem to arrive from two directions at once. Gu Yan blocked the first with his sword’s flat and felt his arm go numb to the shoulder. He slipped the second. The third took him on the hip and spun him half around.

And with every strike, the red threads at Lu Mingyuan’s fingertips reached for his blood.

He could see them with the Lattice. They were not part of Lu Mingyuan’s own art. They were grafted on—forced into the meridians of his hands and arms like iron nails driven into green wood, the joints ugly and inflamed, the wood around them cracking. A blood art, given to him by the Blood Moon Sect, sewn into him to make him stronger than he had any right to be. It worked. It was also wrong in every way a structure could be wrong.

Lu Mingyuan’s palm came at his throat.

Gu Yan let the Dusk-Veil slip.

Not all of it. Only a corner of the mesh, for only a moment. But the light of nine silver stars came up through his Star Sea like the sun through a torn curtain, and for one breath the whole of him blazed—silver light running from his dantian through every widened meridian, into his arms, into the plain iron sword in his hand, so bright that across the plaza Su Lingxi gasped and Cang Yi turned his head and even Han Qiu, holding a maimed Late Heaven Seal at the point of his sword, glanced over.

Lu Mingyuan’s eyes went wide.

“Silver—”

Gu Yan’s sword went along the line.

Not at Lu Mingyuan. Not at his heart, or his throat. At the joint. At the place in his right forearm where the grafted blood-art was nailed into his own meridians, where the wood was split and the nail was loose—the weakest point in the whole structure of him. The edge of Gu Yan’s sword intent followed the line of the graft from his elbow to his wrist as cleanly as a plane following a board.

The blood art tore out of him.

It came loose like a splinter pulled from flesh, the red threads unravelling, spraying from his fingertips in a fine mist. The backlash went up his arm and into his chest. Lu Mingyuan’s water technique collapsed in mid-strike. His palm, which should have crushed Gu Yan’s throat, struck his collarbone with a fraction of its power—enough to crack it, enough to knock him to his knees—and Lu Mingyuan himself went staggering back three steps, clutching his ruined arm, and dropped.

Gu Yan knelt on the paving with his sword point resting on the stone. He closed the Veil. It took all of his strength. The silver vanished. He was gray again; ordinary; a smudge.

He was not sure he could stand.

“Lu Mingyuan.”

Crimson Moth’s voice was a hiss. She was on her knees at the plaza’s center, her stump clutched to her side, her red robe soaked dark. Han Qiu’s sword was at her throat. But she was smiling.

“You were right, little duke,” she said. “He’s very interesting.”

Then she burned.

Gu Yan had heard of blood-escape arts. He had never seen one. Her whole body flared crimson, as though every drop of blood in her had caught fire at once, and the heat of it drove Han Qiu back a step—a single step, all she needed. A red sleeve lashed out across the plaza like a whip and wrapped around Lu Mingyuan’s chest. And then both of them were gone, a streak of red light rising into the mist and vanishing westward at a speed nothing in the Valley could match.

Han Qiu stood looking after them for a long moment. Then he wiped his sword on the hem of his robe and sheathed it.

“She’ll lose two Seals to that,” he said. “And the arm doesn’t grow back.” His voice was perfectly flat. “Not enough.”

He turned, and his flat closed-door eyes found Gu Yan, still kneeling on the paving. They held there for a long time. Gu Yan met them. He did not know what he expected the Elder to say.

“Close it tighter,” said Han Qiu at last, very quietly. “I could still see the edge.”

Then he walked past toward the wounded disciples, and Gu Yan let himself breathe.

Mo Ji was sitting up in the third circle when Gu Yan reached him. His broad face was the colour of ash and his right hand was a swollen ruin, but his eyes were open and clear, and when he saw Gu Yan limping toward him, he grinned.

“You look terrible,” he croaked.

“You look worse.”

“I headbutted a Heaven Seal.” Mo Ji seemed very pleased about this. “Nobody else can say that.”

Su Lingxi knelt beside them both without a word and began setting the bones of Mo Ji’s hand. Cang Yi stood a little way off, leaning on his spear, watching Gu Yan with an expression of deep, private thoughtfulness. Nobody said silver. Nobody said anything about it at all.

It was then that the ground shuddered.

Not a ripple. Something deeper. The whole plaza, the whole broken city, the whole Valley, gave a long slow groan like an old house settling in a storm. The mist around them began to move—drifting for the first time, thinning, pulling back toward the outer zone like a tide going out.

Han Qiu’s head came up sharply. He looked toward the Valley’s heart, toward the black spires, then the other way, toward the Veil they could not see.

“The Valley’s anchor is gone,” he said, and for the first time since Gu Yan had known him, the Elder sounded shaken. “Whatever held this place open—it’s let go. The Veil is closing early.” He turned to the disciples. “Pick up the wounded. All of them. We have until it closes, and I don’t know how long that is.”
