The first Shard Beast came for them on the morning of the third day, out of a street that had no end.

They were crossing what Wei Zhen of the Formation Hall had named, half-jokingly, the Avenue of Giants: a road paved with slabs of the same gold-veined glassy stone, each slab thirty paces on a side, running straight as a ruled line between rows of collapsed buildings. The gravity had eased overnight to a mere twice the outside, and the disciples had grown bolder. Tang Bo and Shen Yu were ranging ahead, prying at cracks with their sword tips in search of anything that glittered. Lu Mingyuan walked with Lu Qing at the column’s center, speaking softly. Cang Yi walked alone.

Gu Yan was looking at a doorway.

It stood on the left side of the avenue, in the face of a building whose upper storeys had sheared away. The doorway itself was intact: a rectangle cut into the stone, with a lintel carved in a band of interlocking spirals. It was twelve zhang high and five wide.

“Big door,” said Mo Ji.

“It’s not a big door,” said Gu Yan. “It’s an ordinary door.”

Mo Ji looked at him. Then he looked at the doorway again, and Gu Yan watched the idea arrive in his friend’s face like a slow cart rolling into a yard.

“No,” said Mo Ji.

“Look at the step.” Gu Yan pointed. At the threshold, worn into the stone, was a shallow depression, smooth and polished, the kind of hollow left by countless feet going in and out over centuries. “Look at where it’s worn. Look at the height of the handle-socket on the frame. Look at the lintel—that carving is meant to be seen from below, but not from very far below. Whoever built this didn’t build it big to impress anyone. They built it to fit.”

He had been seeing it since the first night, and it had taken him two days to believe it. The lines did not lie. When he looked with the Lattice at the ruins of the outer zone, they resolved into the ghosts of a city, and the ghosts had proportions. Stairs, windows, benches, railings. A carpenter’s grandson learned early that a building was a shape wrapped around a body. Grandpa Shouyi could look at a chair and tell you the height of the man it had been made for, within half a cun.

These buildings had been made for people who stood perhaps fifteen or sixteen zhang tall.

“Ten times,” Gu Yan said. “Give or take. This was a city meant for people ten times our size.”

Behind them, Wei Zhen had stopped walking. Su Lingxi had too. Qiao Ning, the other Formation Hall disciple, made a small sound in her throat.

“Immortals aren’t giants,” said Lu Qing uncertainly. “Are they? The books never say.”

“Maybe they weren’t,” Su Lingxi said. She was studying the doorway with her compass open, and for once her voice had lost its precision. “Maybe in their own world they were our size, and the world was larger. The Observatory has old theories. That higher worlds are denser. That their laws are heavier, so everything in them is—” She stopped and shook her head. “I don’t know. I don’t know what I’m saying.”

“Or maybe,” said Lu Mingyuan pleasantly, “the zero-star brother has a vivid imagination. It’s a large door. Large doors exist.” He smiled to show there was no malice in it. “Still. It’s a pretty thought.”

Gu Yan smiled back and said nothing. Over the past months he had learned that Lu Mingyuan never argued with anything he could simply dismiss.

That was when Shen Yu screamed.

It came out of the avenue ahead, where the paving slabs ran on into violet haze, and for a moment Gu Yan’s eyes refused to tell him what it was.

It was shaped something like a wolf, if a wolf were the size of a carriage and carved from glass. Its body was a lattice of crystal planes that caught the copper sunlight and split it into thin, cold rainbows. Its legs were long blades. Its head was a wedge with no eyes, only a deep inner glow, gold-white, that pulsed like a heart. Where it moved, the air around it bent; the slabs it walked on did not crack, but the gravity under its paws seemed to sag, so that dust slid toward it.

Shen Yu was on the ground, clutching a leg that ended below the knee in a clean, glassy stump. There was very little blood. The wound had been sealed by something that frosted the flesh white.

“Shard Beast!” Han Qiu’s voice cracked across the avenue like a whip. “Formation—now!”

The disciples moved. They had drilled for this. Cang Yi was first, already in the air, his spear—a slender Earth-grade weapon of red lacquer and steel—flashing down in an arc that left a trail of hot gold. Fang Rui went low and wide without a word. Tang Bo dragged Shen Yu back by the collar. Mo Ji planted himself in front of the Formation Hall disciples like a door slamming shut.

Cang Yi’s spear struck the beast’s flank.

The crystal did not break. It rang, a long pure note like a temple bell, and the gold trail of Cang Yi’s qi slid off the planes of the beast’s body and scattered harmlessly into the air. The prince landed, rolled, came up with his eyes wide.

“My qi won’t bite!” he shouted. “It’s like hitting a mirror!”

The beast turned toward him. Its glow brightened.

“Don’t let it face you,” Han Qiu snapped. The Elder had not drawn. He stood at the back of the column with one hand on his sword hilt and his eyes half-closed, watching. “Fang Rui, the legs. Gu Feng, Lu Qing, left flank. The rest of you, cover the wounded.”

He’s letting us fight it, Gu Yan realised. He wants to see what we do.

He opened the Lattice.

The world sprang into lines—and the Shard Beast lit up like a lantern.

It was not a beast. Not in the way the Iron-Back Boar had been a beast, with muscle-lines and bone-lines and the hot red web of its qi. There was no flesh inside the crystal at all. The Shard Beast was made entirely of lines: thin, taut, interlocking strands of something that was neither qi nor stone, folded and bound together into the shape of a wolf. And the lines had a pattern. They were the same tight, hard, ancient grain he had seen in the ruins and in the Veil. The beast was a piece of that other world’s law, crystallised, knotted into a body by—what? The Valley’s broken laws trying to mend themselves? Something old and mindless, gathering itself together in the dark for a hundred thousand years?

He did not know. He did not need to know. What he needed was the joint.

And there it was. Every structure has a place where the forces meet, Grandpa used to say. A building, a boat, a chair. Find where the load goes and you know where it will fail. In the beast’s chest, just behind where a wolf’s breastbone would be, the lines converged in a knot the size of a fist. The gold-white glow was not its heart. The glow was the light leaking out of that knot.

And the knot was not sitting square. It was loose, slightly, on its upper side—where the beast’s own strands had been joined imperfectly, a hundred thousand years ago or a hundred days, by whatever had made it.

“Its chest,” Gu Yan shouted. “Upper side of the glow. Don’t use qi—use the edge. Cut along the planes, not across them!”

Cang Yi did not hesitate. That was what Gu Yan would remember about the Third Prince afterward: that he did not stop to ask how a zero-star disciple knew anything. He simply changed his grip.

The beast lunged at Fang Rui. She met it with a low sweep that rang off its foreleg and drove it a half-step sideways—just enough. Mo Ji roared and hurled himself into its flank, not striking, but shoving, nine thousand jin of 9th Vein muscle and Earth-attribute qi braced against the crystal like a man pushing a cart out of a ditch. The beast’s blade-legs scraped across the paving. It twisted to slash at him, exposing its chest.

Gu Yan was already moving.

He had not intended to. His body had read the opening before his mind did. The Starbone in his legs flexed and he crossed fifteen paces of the Avenue of Giants in a heartbeat, his father’s plain iron sword drawn and held low, and in his Star Sea the third node—Sword, the cut, a line chosen—hummed.

He did not push qi into the blade. He did not need to. He laid the edge against the beast’s chest at the exact angle of the planes, where two crystal faces met along a seam he could see and no one else could, and he drew it upward along that seam as cleanly as Grandpa drawing a plane down a board.

The crystal parted.

Not shattered—parted, the two faces sliding apart along their natural line like a split log. The knot was exposed: a fist-sized cluster of glowing strands, pulsing gold-white. The beast shrieked, a sound like a thousand glasses breaking at once.

Cang Yi’s spear-point went into the knot from the side.

The light went out.

The Shard Beast came apart. Its crystal body did not fall so much as unravel, the planes sliding away from each other and toppling onto the paving in a long glassy cascade that rang and rang and finally went quiet. Where the knot had been, a single object dropped onto the slab with a soft clink.

For several breaths no one said anything.

Gu Yan stood among the shards with his sword in his hand and his heart hammering. His nose was bleeding again; he could taste it. Mo Ji was sitting on the ground, panting, rubbing a shoulder that had a long white frost-burn across it. Fang Rui had already sheathed her sword and was looking at him with an expression he could not read.

Cang Yi bent and picked up the object.

It was a crystal, no larger than a plum, clear as water, with a single line of gold running through its heart. When the prince turned it in the light, the gold line did not move with it. It stayed pointing the same direction, steady as a compass needle, as if it were fixed to something outside the stone.

“A law crystal,” said Han Qiu.

The Elder had come up beside them without a sound. He took the crystal from Cang Yi, held it to the copper sunlight for a moment, and gave it back.

“The records call them Valley Hearts. Each Shard Beast has one. They’re fragments of the laws that once held this place together. Alchemists in the capital will pay a hundred mid-grade spirit stones for one this clear, and the Formation Hall will pay more.” He looked at the disciples one by one. “Shen Yu will live, but he will not walk on that leg again without a Heaven-grade regrowth pill. Tang Bo, Qiao Ning, take him back to the Veil camp. The Lu retainers will escort you.”

Lu Hengshan, who had arrived somewhat late with his men, inclined his head graciously. “Of course, Elder Han.”

Han Qiu turned to Gu Yan last.

“Along the planes, not across them,” he said. His voice was quiet. “How did you know?”

Gu Yan had been expecting the question. He had decided, months ago, what he would say when someone asked it.

“My grandfather is a carpenter, Elder. You learn to look for grain. Crystal has grain like wood does. If you cut with it, it splits. If you cut against it, it fights you.”

It was true, as far as it went. Han Qiu regarded him for a long moment with those flat, closed-door eyes.

“Crystal,” the Elder said, “does not have grain like wood does.” Then he turned away and raised his voice to the column. “We move. Stay in pairs. If you see light in a doorway, do not go near it.”

Gu Yan let out a breath he had not known he was holding.

Su Lingxi was beside him, offering a square of white cloth. He took it and pressed it to his nose.

“That’s the third time since we came through the Veil,” she said under her breath.

“It’s the gravity.”

“It’s not the gravity.” She turned her compass so he could see it. The needle, which had been pointing steadily toward the Valley’s heart since they arrived, had swung. It was pointing at the gold line in the Valley Heart in Cang Yi’s hand. “When you cut that thing open, the needle jumped. Not toward the beast. Toward you. Just for a moment. As if for a moment you had a fate after all.” She closed the lid. “Then it was gone.”

He did not know what to say to that, so he said nothing.

They killed four more Shard Beasts over the next five days.

The second was a thing like a crane, all spindly crystal legs and a beak as long as a spear, and it took Lu Qing’s sword-arm open to the bone before Gu Feng and Fang Rui brought it down. The third and fourth came together, a pair of serpentine shapes that flowed through the rubble like water through a gutter, and that fight was Cang Yi’s: he had learned the trick of the planes, and his spear found the seams of their bodies with a precision that made Fang Rui grunt in something like approval. The fifth was small, barely the size of a dog, and Mo Ji killed it by simply picking it up and throwing it into a wall.

They found other things too. A bronze wheel, half buried in rubble, whose spokes were inscribed with characters no one could read, and which was so heavy that four disciples together could not lift it. A shallow basin of the glassy stone in which water had collected and somehow never evaporated, water so dense with qi that a single sip sent Wei Zhen into a trance for an hour. A fragment of something like cloth, woven from threads of light, that dissolved when Lu Mingyuan touched it.

And everywhere, the proportions. The doorways. The stairs. The window ledges three zhang above the street at the height of a giant’s elbow.

Gu Yan stopped telling anyone what he saw. But at night, lying under the unfamiliar stars, he drew down their heavy starlight and let the Lattice show him the ghost-city in silver—the towers whole, the avenues full of shadows that might once have been people—and every night the tug in his chest from the Valley’s heart grew a little stronger.

On the evening of the eighth day, Han Qiu gathered them at the edge of the outer zone.

Ahead of them, across a last stretch of broken paving, a wall of white mist rose from the ground and stood perfectly still. It did not drift. It did not thin. It was as solid-looking as the Veil had been, and from somewhere within it came a faint, rhythmic sound, like water dripping into a deep cistern.

“The middle zone,” Han Qiu said. “Tomorrow we go in.”

As he spoke, Gu Yan’s Lattice gave a sudden, violent shudder—and on the far side of the mist, for one instant, he saw a line he had never seen before. It was not silver, and it was not the tight gold-veined grain of the fallen world.

It was red, and it ran in a great circle, and it was pulsing, slow and wet, like a vein.
