“He went up,” Gu Yan repeated.

The words would not settle. They sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

“Up,” said Yu Xingzhou. “Do keep up, boy. It’s the direction above you.” The Remnant unfolded his frayed legs and stood on the corpse’s palm, and somehow, despite being no taller than Gu Yan and made of nothing but grey light, he managed to look down his nose. “First, though, you should fix your arm. It’s hanging like a broken shutter and it’s making me ill, and I don’t have a stomach.”

Gu Yan looked at his left arm. The shoulder had come out of its socket somewhere on the fall. He had seen Grandpa reset a dislocated shoulder once, on a timber-hauler who had slipped from a cart; he had held the man’s legs.

He wedged his hand into a crack in the corpse’s thumb, braced his feet, set his teeth, and leaned back.

The sound it made was terrible. The pain was worse. For a moment the false sky went white and then black. When he could see again, the shoulder was in, and his arm burned from neck to fingertip, and he could move his fingers.

“Good,” said the Remnant, who had watched with clinical interest. “Your father did the same thing with his knee. Must run in the family, falling into my pit.”

“Tell me about him.”

“I’d rather show you.” Yu Xingzhou pointed down along the vast curve of the corpse’s wrist. “There. He left you something. He said you’d come. I said nobody comes. He said, my son will. I laughed at him.” The thin mouth twitched. “Apparently I owe him an apology.”

It was on the inside of the dead Immortal’s wrist, where the skin had long since gone hard and grey as old stone.

The first thing Gu Yan saw was the mark.

It was small—no wider than his palm—and cut with a fine blade, clean and shallow. A square, with a single straight line drawn through it from corner to corner, and a short tick at the line’s end. To anyone else in three thousand worlds it would have meant nothing. To Gu Yan it was as familiar as his own name. It was the plumb mark of the Gu workshop in Jade Ridge Town. Grandpa Shouyi cut it into the underside of every chair, every chest, every roof-beam he ever made. A square for the work. A line for the truth of it. A tick so they know who to blame.

Gu Yan had cut it himself, on his first finished stool, at the age of nine.

He had not known his father could cut it at all.

He knelt and touched the mark. His fingers were shaking. Beneath it, carved in the same clean hand, were characters:

Gu Changfeng passed here. Jiashen year, the tenth day of the Valley’s opening.

The stone is with Yan’er.

If you are Yan’er: I am sorry. Look under the thumb.

He sat back on his heels and did not trust himself to speak.

“He was a long time carving that,” said Yu Xingzhou behind him. His voice was unexpectedly quiet. “Longer than the rest of it. He kept stopping. I asked him what the difficulty was, and he said he didn’t know how to apologize to a child he’d left behind in a sentence short enough to cut into stone.” A pause. “I’d been dead for a hundred thousand years. I didn’t have any advice for him.”

Under the thumb, in a hollow where the great curled finger met the palm, lay a slip of jade.

It was broken. It had been a recording slip, the kind cultivators used to store techniques or messages—a flat green tablet the length of his hand—and it had snapped neatly across its middle. Only one half was there. Gu Yan picked it up and held it, and it was warm, faintly, in the way a stone is warm when someone has held it for a long time.

He pressed it to his forehead and sent a thread of qi into it.

A voice spoke inside his mind. Low, a little hoarse, with the flat Jade Ridge vowels that Grandpa still had and that Gu Yan had mostly lost in Cangdu. He had not heard it in ten years. He had thought he had forgotten it. He had not.

—so if this half reaches you and not the other, at least you will know where I went.

The Valley is a piece of something that fell from above. The Immortal here will tell you more than I can, if he’s in the mood. What matters is this. When this city was cut away, the strand that held it was severed. But a severed strand still has two ends. One end runs up into the Domain it fell from, and that end is dead, sealed by whatever cut it. The other end didn’t go up. When the city fell through our world’s sky, it dragged its strand down with it, and the strand tangled in the World Membrane, and it runs along the inside of the sky like a thread caught on a nail.

I followed it with the Seed. It goes west, and then toward the center of Azure Dust World. Toward the Sky Pillar.

They say only a Sovereign can climb the Pillar. I think that is true for most people. I do not think it is true for someone who can see the lines the Pillar is made of. There is a crack in the Lattice here, Yan’er, where the strand tore loose. It is very narrow. I am going to go through it, and follow the strand to the Pillar, and see what happens when a Heaven Seal with a Seed in his chest tries to climb.

If I am wrong, I will die, and you will be safe, because the stone is asleep in you and asleep it cannot be found.

If I am right—

The voice stopped. Where the slip had broken, the recording broke too, mid-breath.

Gu Yan lowered it slowly.

“The other half?” he said, and his own voice sounded strange to him.

“He took it.” Yu Xingzhou had come to stand beside him on the stone wrist, frayed edges stirring. “He said he’d finish the message on the other side, if there was another side. I don’t know whether he did. I don’t know whether he lived. I only know that he went into the crack alive, and the crack didn’t spit him back out.” The Remnant looked at him sidelong. “That’s more than most people get, boy. A door he walked through on his own feet.”

Inside his robe, against his ribs, Gu Yan could feel the shape of the other thing his father had left him. The sealed letter. He had carried it every day since Jade Ridge, in an oiled-silk pouch Gu Lan had sewn for him. Open it when you can see the sky’s edge, Grandpa had said.

He was at the bottom of a pit, under a false sky, beside a dead god. He could not see any edge at all.

He left it where it was.

“Now,” said Yu Xingzhou briskly, “the warning. Then the lesson. Then I intend to stop existing, and I’d like to do it while I still remember how.”

Gu Yan looked up sharply.

“Don’t make that face. You’re a Lower Worlder; you’ll make the face, I suppose, but do it quickly.” The Remnant sat down on the stone beside him, cross-legged again. “I have been lying here for a hundred thousand years with my blood still wet, because I was too stubborn to let go of it. I told myself I was waiting for someone from the Violet Dusk to come and find me. Nobody came. Nobody was ever going to. The strand was cut.” He shrugged. “The only callers I get are people who want to drink me. That woman with the moth sleeves is up there right now, drilling at my ring with children’s lives. If I keep my blood wet, sooner or later someone like her gets through.”

“You can stop it?”

“I can stop being. When I go, my blood will go with me. It will dry and turn to stone like the rest of this flesh, and all she will find at the end of her drill is a very large rock.” A thin, sour smile. “I find I like that idea. One last bit of spite. It’s very nearly the only pleasure left to me.”

He held up one frayed finger.

“The warning. I have told you already, but you are sixteen and you will forget. The Seed in you is asleep. Your stars are gray because they are shuttered—you’ve got nine lanterns in your Star Sea with their shutters closed. That’s why your little mirror couldn’t see them. That’s why your father’s stone has stayed hidden ten years. But a Seed doesn’t stay asleep. You’ve been lighting your nodes one by one, and each one you light opens the shutters a crack.”

He pointed upward.

“When you climb out of here, you will pass through my ring again. It’s a lock, boy. It was built in the Violet Dusk to keep the Observatory’s instruments safe, and its key is the Lattice. When the key goes through, the lock turns. And when a lock turns, the key turns with it.” He let that sit. “Your shutters will open. All nine. And if the Lattice hears nine Seed-stars waking at once, somewhere above the Thirty-Three Heavens, a Warden is going to lift its head.”

Gu Yan’s mouth went dry. “Then I can’t leave.”

“Then you learn to close the curtains before you open the shutters. That’s the lesson.” Yu Xingzhou leaned forward. “Open your Lattice. All the way. Look at your Star Sea through it. Don’t argue.”

Gu Yan obeyed.

The nine gray stars hung in their frame, the four lit nodes glowing among them. Through the Lattice he could see them as structures now—small, tight weavings of light, each built on one node of the grid.

“In the Violet Dusk,” the Remnant said, “Star-Recorders had to observe things that didn’t want to be observed. Comets that burned out the eye. Stars that drew attention to whoever was looking at them. So we learned to look through veils. A veil is a structure like any other. It has lines. Watch.”

He raised a hand, and between his fingers a weave of grey light took shape: a fine, loose mesh, nine-sided, its threads crossing at angles so precise they hurt to look at. As Gu Yan watched, the Remnant laid it over a fragment of his own frayed light—and the fragment vanished. Not darkened; vanished, as if it had never been. Yet Gu Yan, looking with the Lattice, could see that it was still there, behind the weave, burning.

“Dusk-Veil Seal,” said Yu Xingzhou. “It doesn’t hide the light. It hides the shape of the light. The Lattice listens for the shape of Seed-stars. Cover the shape, and your stars can burn as bright as you like, and everything that looks for them—your mirrors, your compasses, the Wardens, all of it—will see what it expects to see.” A sniff. “Mostly. A Warden standing close enough to touch you would know. Try not to let one stand close enough to touch you.”

“How do I make it?”

“You’re a carpenter’s son, aren’t you? You’ve seen me make it. Now copy the joints.”

It took him three hours.

Three hours of weaving lines of his own mental strength into a mesh that would not hold, that slipped, that tangled, that came apart in his hands like wet paper. His nose bled. His head pounded so hard he vomited once, bitterly, onto the stone. The Remnant sat beside him and said things like “No,” and “That’s a knot, not a joint,” and “Your father did this in two hours, but he was older and less stupid.”

And then, at the end of the third hour, it held.

A fine silver-grey mesh, nine-sided, settled over his Star Sea like a fisherman’s net laid on still water. His nine gray stars were still there. He could still feel them, every one. But when he looked at them through the Lattice, their shape was blurred, softened, ordinary, nine stars that might be anyone’s.

“Adequate,” said Yu Xingzhou. Coming from him, it sounded like a eulogy.

The Remnant was fading.

Gu Yan had not noticed it begin. But the grey light of his form was thinner now, and the frayed edges had spread inward, and when he moved his hands they left faint trails in the air like smoke. Beneath them, along the vast body of the dead Immortal, the gold in the open chest had begun to dull.

“It’s starting,” Yu Xingzhou said. He did not sound afraid. He sounded tired, and a little curious. “Interesting. I always wondered what it would feel like. It feels like falling asleep in a library.”

“Is there anything—” Gu Yan began.

“No. Don’t be sentimental, it’s embarrassing.” The Remnant turned his thin face upward, toward the false sky full of lanterns. “One thing. When you climb—and you will, you’ve got his stubbornness—if you ever reach the Violet Dusk, if anything of it is still hanging there, find the Ninth Observatory of Shuangque. Or where it used to be. Tell them their Star-Recorder kept his post.” A thin, crooked smile. “Tell them he kept it for a hundred thousand years, and nobody relieved him, and he’s lodging a formal complaint.”

The light of him was almost gone now. Gu Yan could see the false sky clearly through his chest.

“And Gu Yan,” said Yu Xingzhou, and for the first time he used the name. “The Lattice was built as a ladder. Everyone who has ever tried to climb it has been treated as a thief. Ask yourself why the ladder would be guarded by the people at the top.”

Then the grey light came apart like mist in the sun, and the pit was dark, and the gold blood in the dead Immortal’s chest had become stone.

Far above, at the rim of the spire ring, someone screamed in fury.

And along the thick red line of the drill, which still pierced the ring and ran down the funnel wall toward the stone heart it would never reach, Gu Yan felt a pulse go back the other way—out across the mist, toward the plaza—hard and vicious, like a hand closing into a fist. A moment later the line pulled, and through it, faint and unmistakable, came the dense, earthy taste of Mo Ji’s qi being torn loose much faster than before.

She had lost her prize. Now she was going to take what she could get.
