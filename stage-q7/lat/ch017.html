When the other disciples had gone, Elder Han Qiu sat down on the stump of an old plum tree at the edge of the yard and looked at Gu Yan for so long that the shadows of the branches moved across the earth between them.

“Show me Flowing Cloud,” he said at last. “The first six forms.”

Gu Yan showed him.

He had practised them so often that his body moved through them without thought: the rising parry, the drifting step, the turning cut that followed an opponent’s force and bent it away. He did them well. He knew he did them well. When he finished, he stood with the iron sword lowered and his breathing even, and waited.

“Flowing Cloud,” said Han Qiu, “is a Mortal-grade technique of the high level. It was written about three hundred years ago by a sword master of the southern lakes who had studied water for most of his life. It is a good technique. Its principle is to yield. To become like cloud: soft, formless, so that the enemy’s force passes through you and is lost.” He paused. “You do it exactly as written. And every movement you make is wrong.”

Gu Yan said nothing.

“You do not yield,” said Han Qiu. “You place. When you parry, you are not letting the blade pass. You are setting your sword at the one angle where the enemy’s force will be split away from you, like a wedge set into a log. When you step, you are not drifting. You are moving to the exact spot from which the next cut will have the most leverage. You are doing Flowing Cloud with the mind of a carpenter, and a carpenter does not think like a cloud. A carpenter thinks like a chisel. Find the grain. Set the edge. Strike once.” He looked at Gu Yan’s hands. “You saw the Lu boy’s ankle during the duel. I watched. You were not looking at his spear.”

It was not a question, so Gu Yan did not answer it.

“How you see what you see,” said Han Qiu, “is your own affair. I am not going to ask. Every swordsman of any worth has something he does not explain.” He stood. “Keep practising Flowing Cloud. It will teach your feet. But stop trying to be water. You are not water. Come to the morning lessons. Watch. Don’t ask me questions yet.” He turned to go, and then said, over his shoulder, “And go and get yourself some merit points. That sword is good iron, but it was poorly tempered and someone has sharpened it badly at least twice since. It will break at the worst moment. A disciple of the outer court cannot afford a better one. Find a way to afford it.”

He walked out of the yard, between the plum trees, and the sense of an edge in the air went with him.

The Merit Hall was crowded in the late afternoons. Gu Yan spent a long time in front of the boards, reading tablets, with Mo Ji beside him groaning at every one.

“Collect two hundred Mirrorwing moths from the eastern ravine. Alive. Who wants two hundred live moths?”

“The alchemy hall.”

“Escort an Academy cart to the salt prefecture. Thirty days. Thirty days of carts, Gu Yan.” Mo Ji groaned. “Clear the Archive cellar. Rats. Forty merit.” He brightened. “That’s not bad. I can hit rats.”

“The deacon said they were not ordinary rats.”

“I am not an ordinary man.”

Gu Yan’s eye stopped on a tablet near the end of the board, half hidden behind others. Its paint was faded. It had been there a long time.

West Wall, Third Section. Formation Pillar Seventeen damaged by subsidence. Pillar’s protective array has lost coherence. Requires: inspection, structural repair of the pillar body, and resetting of the array housing. Formation Hall will supply materials. Applicants must have knowledge of stonework or carpentry. Reward: 300 merit, plus 20 low-grade spirit stones.

Below, in a different hand, someone had added: Third posting. Previous two applicants failed.

“Three hundred,” breathed Mo Ji, reading over his shoulder. “That’s seven and a half piles of rats.”

“Knowledge of carpentry.”

“You are carpentry.” Mo Ji paused. “Why did the last two fail?”

The Formation Hall master who met him at the west wall the next morning was a small, round, bald man of indeterminate age, in a robe so covered with ink stains and chalk dust that its original colour was a matter of speculation. He introduced himself as Master Fang Ziyou, examined Gu Yan’s disciple token with a magnifying lens as though it might be a forgery, and then said, “Jade Ridge. Carpenter’s grandson. You’re the one who’s been fixing the stools in the fourteenth building.”

“Yes, Master.”

“The boy in room eight is my nephew. He says you mended a chair-back without a single nail.” Master Fang put the lens away. “Well. Let’s see if you can mend a wall.”

The west wall of the Academy was not as tall as the city’s great walls, but it was old, older than the city’s, and it ran along the crest of a ridge on the mountain’s western flank with a long drop on its outer side. At intervals along it stood pillars of grey stone, each three zhang high and as thick as a large tree, each crowned with a bronze cap engraved with characters. Gu Yan could see, without even opening the sight, that they were part of something: the faint warmth in the air around them, the way the wind changed as it passed between them.

“The Academy’s outer barrier,” said Master Fang. “Thirty-six pillars. Each one anchors a portion of the array. When they are all sound, the barrier holds off anything short of a Mandate cultivator for long enough to send for the principal. Pillar Seventeen”—he walked to it and slapped its side—“is not sound.”

Gu Yan could see that too. The ground on the pillar’s outer side had slumped, perhaps a hand’s breadth, after some heavy rain. The pillar was leaning very slightly. Not enough to see by eye. Enough that the bronze cap was out of true with the caps on the pillars on either side.

“The first man,” said Master Fang, “was a stonemason. He shored up the ground, reset the pillar plumb, and called it done. The array did not wake. The second was one of my own disciples. He spent a month recarving the characters on the cap, on the theory that subsidence had cracked the engraving. The array did not wake. I,” said Master Fang, “have been busy with other things, and also I suspect I am too old to crawl about inside a pillar, and also I am curious. Do as you like. Don’t break anything.”

He sat down on a boulder, took out a flask, and watched.

Gu Yan walked around the pillar three times. Then he laid both hands flat on its stone and opened the sight.

The pillar bloomed with lines.

It was the most beautiful structure he had ever seen. More beautiful even than the lantern boat. Inside the grey stone, threads of qi ran from the bronze cap down through the pillar’s heart in a slow spiral, nine turns of it, gathering strength as they went, until they reached the base and spread out into the earth like roots, and through the roots joined the threads of the pillars on either side. A single great pattern, a woven thing, thirty-six trunks joined underground into one tree.

And there, about a third of the way up, where the pillar’s body had been built in sections and the sections joined, was the fault.

The stonemason had made the pillar plumb. But when the ground had slumped, the pillar had not merely leaned; it had twisted. Very slightly. A tenth of a turn, no more. And the old builders had cut the joint between the second and third sections in a particular way, with an interlocking pattern like a carpenter’s dovetail but in stone, so that the spiral of qi could pass smoothly from one section to the next. Twisted out of alignment by that tenth of a turn, the joint no longer matched. The spiral came down the upper section and hit the joint and simply stopped, like a river meeting a dam, and pooled there, and leaked out sideways through the stone in a hundred thin, wasted threads.

It was so obvious, once seen, that he almost laughed.

“Master Fang,” he said. “Do you have the original drawings for this pillar?”

Master Fang lowered his flask. “Why?”

“The joint between the second and third sections has turned. The qi can’t pass. I’d need to know how the joint is cut inside, to turn it back without breaking the interlock.”

There was a long pause.

“There are no original drawings,” said Master Fang slowly. “The west wall was built before the Academy kept records. Nobody alive knows how those joints are cut.” He was looking at Gu Yan very differently now. “How do you know there is a joint?”

Gu Yan had been afraid of that question. He had an answer ready, and it was not a lie.

“I’ve been listening to it,” he said, and rapped the stone with his knuckle, once above the joint and once below. The sound changed: very slightly, but it changed. “And my grandfather taught me that if a thing leans, look for where it bends, and if a thing twists, look for where it’s joined.”

Master Fang got up from his boulder and came over and rapped the pillar himself, above and below. He listened. He rapped again.

“Huh,” he said.

It took six days.

He could not see the shape of the joint’s interlock directly, not with his eyes. But with the sight, he could see where the qi pooled and where it leaked, and the leaking threads traced the hidden shape of the stone’s contact faces the way water traces the cracks in a floor. He drew it on paper, painstakingly, one line at a time, his head aching worse each day, waking each morning with dried blood crusted under his nose. Master Fang watched him draw and said nothing, but on the third day he brought a second flask, and on the fourth day he brought his nephew from room eight to hold the lamp.

On the fifth day, with timber braces and iron wedges and a set of jacks borrowed from the Academy’s carpentry shop, they lifted the upper two-thirds of the pillar a hair’s breadth off its joint. The stone groaned. Master Fang held the bracing with his own qi, a dense, steady, ink-dark pressure that kept the vast weight perfectly still. And Gu Yan, lying on his back in the gap with a lantern in his teeth, turned the joint back that tenth of a turn using nothing but a pry-bar, a mallet, and the pattern in his head.

On the sixth morning, they lowered it.

The stone sections met with a sound like a great door closing. For a moment, nothing happened.

Then the spiral of qi came down from the bronze cap, smooth and bright, reached the joint, and passed through it as if it had never been interrupted. It ran on down into the base and out into the roots. And all along the west wall, from pillar to pillar, Gu Yan felt rather than saw a faint, warm pulse go out through the earth, like a sleeping animal shifting and settling into a deeper sleep.

The air around Pillar Seventeen grew warm.

Master Fang Ziyou stood for a long moment with his hand flat on the stone. Then he took his hand away, looked at it, and looked at Gu Yan.

“Six days,” he said. “My disciple spent a month. The stonemason spent two.” He rubbed his bald scalp, leaving a streak of chalk. “Come and see me at the Formation Hall. Not now. When you have time. There are things in the Formation Hall that no one has been able to make sense of for a very long time, and I should like to know what a carpenter makes of them.”

“Yes, Master.”

“And go and wash. You look as if someone hit you in the face with a brick.”

Three hundred merit points. Twenty low-grade spirit stones.

He had never held twenty spirit stones at once. They sat in a little cloth bag on the bed in room nine, each one the size of a thumb-joint, translucent and faintly milky, and the qi coming off them was so dense he could feel it on his face like the breath of an oven.

Mo Ji sat on his own bed and looked at them with naked reverence.

“I got forty merit for the rats,” he said. “I fought the rats for three days. One of them bit me through my boot.”

Gu Yan took the bag, went to the Merit Hall, and came back an hour later with two small porcelain bottles and a much thinner bag. He set one bottle on Mo Ji’s bed.

Mo Ji stared at it. Then he picked it up and read the label, and the colour drained out of his broad face.

“Vein-Opening Pill,” he said. “Mortal grade, high. This is—Gu Yan, this is a hundred and twenty merit. Each. I looked. I looked every day.”

“I know. You look at it every day. You stand in front of the pill counter with your hands behind your back like a child at a sweet-stall.”

“I can’t take this.”

“You can. You’re going to need it.” Gu Yan sat down on his own bed with the other bottle. “Lu clan stewards don’t break anvils, you told me. They write things down. If someone’s going to keep writing things down about your father’s forge, you’d better be strong enough that they think about it first.”

Mo Ji did not say anything for a long time. He turned the bottle over in his enormous hands.

“Mo family doesn’t forget,” he said at last, roughly.

“I know. You told me. Take the pill.”

They took them together that night, sitting cross-legged on their beds with the window open to the river and the lanterns. The pill dissolved on Gu Yan’s tongue in a rush of heat that went straight down into his belly and spread out, not gently but in a single hot flood, like water released from a sluice-gate. He guided it with the sight, the way he had guided the stair’s pressure, toward the fourth meridian, which ran from his spine down his left leg, where it waited sealed and silted like the others.

He found the seam. He set the flood against it at an angle, along the grain.

It opened more easily than the others, and the qi wheel in his chest gained a fourth spoke and spun faster, and the cool night air came in through the window full of qi, and the Lattice in his mind glowed steadily, two nodes lit out of nine.

4th Vein.

Across the room, Mo Ji gave a sudden, deep grunt, like a man setting down something very heavy. The bed creaked. When Gu Yan opened his eyes, his friend’s skin had a faint brown sheen, like earth after rain, and he was grinning like a fool.

“Fourth,” Mo Ji said. “Fourth Vein. I can feel it. I could hit a wall.” He paused. “I should not hit a wall. The Formation Hall would make you fix it.”

Gu Yan laughed.

There was a knock at the door.

They looked at each other. It was past the second watch. Mo Ji got up and opened it, and there was no one in the corridor; but on the threshold, neatly placed, lay a folded card of heavy cream paper sealed with a dab of wax.

Mo Ji picked it up, turned it over, and went very still.

Pressed into the wax, small and precise, was a silver deer.
