The tide came at his section of the wall as if a dam had broken.

Every beast below the north wall turned at once, and the whole seething mass of them surged sideways toward the stretch of walkway where Gu Yan stood, a wave of red eyes and claws and tusks, and struck the wall beneath him so hard that the timbers jumped under his feet. A boar came over the palisade beside him, then a wolf on its back, then two more. Gu Feng’s spear took the boar through the eye. Gu Yan cut the wolf’s foreleg from under it.

“Captain Zhao,” said Gu Yan, ducking a mountain cat’s claws, “I need Captain Zhao.”

Zhao Rui reached them a dozen breaths later, his hatchet face streaked with blood and soot, his long saber notched to the hilt. Gu Yan did not waste words. He pointed at Crow Hill. “There’s a man by the dead pine with a bell. He’s driving them. Every time he rings it, they come harder. He just turned them all on me.”

Zhao Rui looked at the hill. He looked at the beasts, which were now crowding to this one stretch of wall and nowhere else, as if every one of them had been told exactly where to go. He did not ask how Gu Yan knew.

He turned to Gu Tieshan, who had come pounding along the walkway with his iron staff. “Hold here. Hold it with your teeth. The patriarch’s still down with the rhino, and if this boy’s right, we don’t have time to wait for him.” Then, to the two youths: “You. With me.”

They went out through the sally port in the west wall, a low iron door that let onto the steep ground above the Greenwash Stream, where the beasts were thinnest. Ahead, the tea-pickers’ village burned, and beyond it Crow Hill rose black against the glow.

Zhao Rui was at the 7th Vein. He ran in front, silent as a cat, his saber held low; once, when a lone fox came at them out of the dark, the blade moved and the fox was in two pieces before Gu Yan had seen the cut. Gu Feng ran behind, his spear trailing. Gu Yan ran in the middle and watched the crimson threads, which streamed over their heads toward the hill like a thousand strands of a spider’s web in the firelight, thicker and denser with every step.

Halfway up the slope, the hill was ringed with beasts.

Not a tide. A guard. A hundred, perhaps, standing motionless in a wide circle around the summit: wolves and bears and two great Rock Lizards, all perfectly still, all facing outward. Their red eyes caught the firelight.

The three of them dropped flat behind an outcrop of stone.

“Can’t fight through that,” Zhao Rui breathed. “Not quickly.”

Gu Yan was looking at the bell.

From here, forty zhang below the summit, he could see it clearly for the first time. The robed figure stood beside the dead pine with its back half to them, watching the town. The bell hung from its outstretched hand on a short chain. It was the size of a man’s fist, dark red and gleaming, cast of some metal Gu Yan had never seen, and its surface was covered in tiny engraved characters.

He looked at it the way he looked at timber.

The lines leapt out at him so fiercely that he flinched. The bell was not a bell. It was a knot. All the thousands of crimson threads from the whole tide came together into it, and inside it they did not simply end. They were woven. He could see the weaving, a pattern of dark lines running through the engraved characters, circling, crossing, folding back on themselves, so complex that he could not follow it for more than a moment before his eyes swam. It was a formation, a structure of qi built into the metal, and every thread in the tide passed through it.

A structure. And every structure had a place where it was joined.

He saw it. On the rim of the bell, where the two halves of the casting mould had met, there was a seam. But the lines of the formation crossed it, every one, and at the seam they had to pass from one half of the metal to the other, and there they were thin. Stretched. One point on that seam, no wider than a grain of rice, carried more than any other: a place where nine lines crossed at once. If that point broke, the weaving would come apart like a basket when its rim is cut.

Something warm was running from his nose. He wiped it away.

“There’s a flaw in the bell,” he said. “On the rim. If I can hit it with the point of my sword, I think it will break. And I think if it breaks, they’ll stop.”

Zhao Rui looked at him for a long moment.

“How close do you need to be?”

“Close enough to touch it.”

“Then Feng and I make a hole,” said the captain, “and you go through it, and you don’t stop for anything. Not for us. Understand?”

They came over the outcrop together.

Zhao Rui went first, and for a moment Gu Yan saw what the 7th Vein meant: the captain’s body blurred with qi, and his saber traced a pale arc through the air that went through the first wolf and the second and the third, and he was among the beasts like a scythe through barley. Gu Feng was a pace behind him, the spear’s tides rolling one, two, three, punching a lane through the circle. The beasts turned, roaring. The hole opened.

Gu Yan went through it.

He did not look left or right. He ran, uphill, over the corpses, with his father’s sword held low. The robed figure by the dead pine was turning, the hood coming round, the hand with the bell lifting.

Ten paces. Five.

The bell swung. He saw the pulse begin to run down the threads, dark and swift.

He lunged. The tip of the iron jian struck the rim of the bell exactly on the seam, on the point where nine lines crossed. He felt it go in. Not far. A hair’s breadth. Enough.

The bell screamed.

It was a sound like no bell ever made, a shriek of tearing metal and something else, a thousand voices together, and the crimson thing burst apart in a spray of red shards. And down the hill, in a great widening ring through the whole valley, the tide of beasts stopped.

Gu Yan felt it more than saw it: the threads snapping, all at once, whipping back like cut bowstrings and dissolving into nothing. Below the hill, the circle of guard beasts staggered. A bear fell over. A wolf shook its head and yelped and ran, tail down, toward the forest. Far off at the walls, the roar of the tide broke up into a thousand separate sounds of confusion, howls and squeals and bellows, beasts that had suddenly woken up in the middle of a battle and did not know why they were there.

The robed figure looked at its empty hand.

Then it looked at him.

The hood had fallen back. Under it was a man of perhaps forty, with a long, pale, ordinary face, a scholar’s face, the kind of face one might see behind the counter of a pawnshop in any prefecture city. His eyes were not red. They were brown, and mild, and faintly puzzled.

“That bell,” he said, “took three hundred lives to forge.”

He said it quite calmly, as one might mention the price of a horse.

“Three hundred.” He shook out his hand; there was blood on it, from the shards. “I’ve been feeding it for a year. One more month in that forest, and it would have been ready to send to the mountains. And a boy from a tea town puts a sword through it.” He tilted his head, studying Gu Yan. “How did you do that?”

Gu Yan did not answer. Behind him he could hear Zhao Rui and Gu Feng coming up the hill, fast, their breathing harsh.

“Well,” said the man. “No matter. You’ll do for a start on the next one.”

He moved.

Gu Yan had faced a boar and a spear and a tide of beasts. He had never faced a Star Sea cultivator who wanted him dead. The man did not seem to cross the space between them so much as arrive. Around his body, three points of light flared, dim and crimson, his stars, and his palm, open and soft and pale, struck Gu Yan in the chest.

It was like being hit by a falling roof.

He flew. He hit the trunk of the dead pine, and heard his ribs break, and slid down it to the ground with the world spinning and his mouth full of blood.

Zhao Rui reached the summit. The captain did not hesitate; he came in with his saber blurring, a cut that would have taken a bear’s head off. The robed man raised one hand and caught the blade between finger and thumb.

The saber stopped. Zhao Rui’s eyes went wide. Dark red light ran down the steel from the man’s fingers to the captain’s hand, and wherever it touched, the captain’s skin went grey and shrank against the bone, and Zhao Rui screamed, and tore himself free, and fell back clutching his arm, the hand withered and blackened like a leaf in winter.

Gu Feng’s spear came in from the other side. The third tide, the drowning one, with everything in it. The man turned his body a fraction and let the spearhead slide past him and struck the ash-wood shaft with the edge of his hand, and the shaft broke in two like a dry twig. His other hand closed around Gu Feng’s throat.

“Strong blood,” the man murmured, lifting him off his feet. Gu Feng kicked, choking, his hands clawing at the man’s wrist. “Good. Nine Forgings, and pure. I’ll take you first.”

Crimson light gathered at his fingertips. Seven points of it, in a ring around Gu Feng’s throat.

Gu Yan got up.

His chest was a cage of broken sticks and every breath was a knife. But he had seen what seven points of light on a throat meant, in a dead wolf in the lantern light at the north gate. He got his feet under him and he stood against the dead pine, and the world tilted and steadied, and he raised his father’s sword.

It was not enough. He knew it. He had nothing left. A 9th Forging body, however much starlight was in its bones, could not touch a Star Sea cultivator. He could see the lines in the man’s body, see the joints, see exactly where to cut, and he did not have the strength to get there.

He reached for it anyway.

Somewhere inside him, deep under the broken ribs, below the dantian where the Nine Furnace Art had told him for eight years to sink his breath, something gave.

He felt it like a door bursting open in a high wind. A passage inside his body that had always been closed, a narrow channel running from the dantian up through his chest and out along the inside of his right arm to the palm. The first of the nine primary meridians. It tore open all at once, and through it, from the air, from the night, from the dark around him, something came pouring in.

Qi.

It came into him cold and bright and alive, like a mountain stream into a dry ditch, and it filled the meridian and rushed down his arm into his hand, into the hilt of the iron sword, and the starlight in his bones rang with it, every silver point at once, like a bell answering a bell.

The 1st Vein.

He moved. Not toward the man’s heart or his head. Toward the wrist that held Gu Feng. He had seen its lines, the long bright bundle of tendon running from the elbow to the fingers. He cut along it.

The iron jian, filled with qi for the first time in ten years, went through the man’s forearm from wrist to elbow, a long straight line, as clean as a plane stroke through elm.

The man screamed and dropped Gu Feng.

He spun, his face no longer mild, his ruined arm hanging, and his stars blazed crimson around him, all three, and his good hand came up with dark red light gathering in it like blood welling from a wound. Gu Yan, empty again, falling, knew he could not stop it.

“Enough.”

It was not loud. It did not need to be. The voice came from the slope below, and the man froze with his hand raised.

Gu Shouyi came up the last of the hill slowly, leaning on a stick, in his old workshop apron, with his grey hair loose in the wind. He was breathing hard. In his bad hand he held something Gu Yan had seen a thousand times and never thought twice about: his grandfather’s ink-line box, the little wooden reel of cord and the pot of ink that every carpenter in the kingdom used to snap a straight line onto timber.

The robed man looked at him and laughed. “Old man,” he said, “go home.”

Gu Shouyi did not answer him. He looked past the man at Gu Yan, lying against the pine with blood on his mouth, and something passed over his old face, and then it was gone, and what was left was very quiet.

He hooked the end of the ink-line to the head of his stick and drove the stick into the ground. He pulled the cord out from the reel, a long black thread, taut. He set his thumb on it.

And Gu Yan, who had seen his grandfather’s lines every day since the first node lit, saw them change.

All the broken lines inside the old man’s body, the dark, severed meridians of a crippled Heaven Seal, flared at once. Not healed. Burned. Something in him that had been buried for ten years woke and gathered and ran, along roads that were no longer roads, tearing them further as it went, into his arm and his thumb and the black thread of the ink-line. And in the old man’s dantian, for a single heartbeat, Gu Yan saw a seal: a shape of light, square and straight and absolutely true, like a carpenter’s square drawn in fire.

Gu Shouyi lifted the cord, and let it go.

Snap.

A line of black went across the night.

It was perfectly straight. It ran from the head of the old man’s stick across the summit of Crow Hill and through the robed man and through the dead pine and on, out over the valley, out toward the burning village and beyond, a single stroke of ink drawn on the dark. It lasted less than a breath. Then it was gone.

The robed man stood very still. He opened his mouth. A thin, perfectly straight line of red appeared on his robe, from his left shoulder to his right hip. Then the two halves of him slid apart and fell.

Behind him, with a long, slow groan, the dead pine split down the same line and toppled.

In the silence that followed, Gu Shouyi coughed.

It was a small sound. Then he coughed again, harder, and bent over his stick, and blood came out of his mouth, a great deal of it, black in the firelight, and his knees gave way.

Gu Yan was there before he hit the ground. He did not remember crossing the summit. He caught the old man and held him, and looked at him with the sight, and saw that the lines inside his grandfather’s body, which had been broken, were now very nearly gone. Faint grey threads. Guttering, like a lamp at the end of its oil.

“Grandfather.”

The old man’s eyes opened. He looked up at his grandson. He smiled, with blood on his teeth.

“Ten years,” he whispered. “I’ve been wanting to do that for ten years.” His good hand found Gu Yan’s wrist and gripped it, weakly. “Did you see it? The line?”

“I saw it.”

“Good.” The eyes closed. “Remember it.”
