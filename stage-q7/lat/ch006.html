Old Liu’s dog had to be killed. It took three guards and a net, and one of the guards came back with his forearm torn open to the bone, and afterward, when Captain Zhao Rui’s men brought the body in, it was as dry and shrunken as the wolf had been, though nobody had touched it with anything but a spear.

After that, Jade Ridge stopped pretending.

The patriarch doubled the watch on the walls. The outer farms were told to bring their herds in to the fields below the town every night, and the town gates were closed at sunset, which they had not been in Gu Yan’s lifetime. The clan sent a rider to the prefecture city with a letter sealed in the patriarch’s own wax. The trial, Gu Hong announced, would still be held in seven days. Nobody argued. The trial had become the one ordinary thing in town, and people held on to it the way a man in a flood holds on to a fence post.

Gu Yan trained until his hands bled and then went on training. It did not seem to make any difference. The 7th Forging was settled in him now, his organs dense and steady, his breath deep, but the step to the 8th was a wall, and he could feel it. The blood. The next layer was the blood itself, and the Nine Furnace Art said that to temper the blood a cultivator must “heat it in the furnace of the organs until it runs like molten bronze,” which sounded very well in a manual and in practice meant sitting for hours waiting for something that did not come.

Seven days. Gu Feng was at the 8th Forging and pushing at the 9th. Gu Yan was at the 7th, and Gu Feng would not be the only one in the arena who was stronger than him.

On the night after the Liu farmstead, unable to sleep, he climbed out through the loft shutter and up onto the roof of the workshop.

He had done it often as a boy. The workshop roof was low-pitched, tiled in grey, and from the ridge of it, sitting with his back against the chimney, he could see over the compound wall and down the whole length of the valley: the dark terraces, the pale thread of the Greenwash Stream, the lights of the watchtowers on the town walls, each one a tiny restless spark. Beyond the walls, the Black Pine Forest was a blacker darkness against the dark of the mountains.

And above it all, the sky.

It was a moonless night and very cold, and the stars were out in their uncountable heaps. The Heavenly Stream ran overhead, so bright and so thick with light that it seemed to have texture, like spilled salt. He lay back against the tiles and looked at it, and let himself, for a while, not think about the trial or the forest or dead things with rust-coloured eyes.

It’s a ceiling, the old man had said. Painted very well.

He closed his eyes and looked at the frame. The first node glowed in its corner, silver and steady, as it had every night since the bracket. The other eight waited.

Then, without his doing anything at all, the frame moved.

Not shifted. Turned, very slightly, the way a sunflower turns. He felt it: the whole silver structure in his mind tilted, as if something had drawn it, and settled facing upward. Toward the sky.

And the starlight came down.

At first he thought he was imagining it. A faint coolness on his face and on the backs of his hands, like the touch of mist. But there was no mist. He opened his eyes and looked at his hand, lying on the tile beside him, and saw that it was very faintly silver. Not reflecting the stars. Glowing with them. A thin, almost invisible sheen of light lay over his skin, and as he watched, it sank into him, the way water sinks into a sponge.

He closed his eyes again, and saw what was happening.

The light was coming down from the stars in lines. Countless lines, finer than hairs, silver as the frame, so many and so faint that individually they were nothing at all. They came down out of the sky and met the frame in his mind and passed through it, and the frame did something to them. Gathered them. Sorted them, the way a carpenter’s hand sorts a bundle of dowels, and then sent them on, down, out of his mind and into his body.

Into his bones.

The cold reached his bones and he understood, a moment too late, that he should have been afraid of it.

It hurt.

It hurt the way nothing had ever hurt him: not the boar’s hoof, not the marrow decoction, not eight years of iron stakes. It was cold, a cold so intense that it burned, and it went into the bones of his arms and legs and spine like needles of ice being driven into the marrow one by one, thousands of them, each one finding a precise place and lodging there and refusing to melt. His body arched off the tiles. His jaw locked. He could not breathe. He could not have screamed if he had wanted to, and some distant part of him was grateful for that, because the old man was asleep below and the whole compound was within earshot.

He could not stop it. He did not know how. The frame in his mind was drinking the starlight and pouring it into him, and the needles kept coming, and he lay rigid on the roof with his eyes streaming and thought, very clearly, this is how I die.

He did not die.

The pain did not lessen. But after a time that might have been a few breaths or most of an hour, he found that he could look at it. He looked, with the sight that the first node had sharpened, down into his own body, into the bones, and he saw what the starlight was doing.

The needles were not random. Each one went into the bone and lodged at a joining, at a place where the fibres of the bone met and locked, and at each one, the line grew stronger. Thicker. Denser. Brighter. The bones of his arms were full of silver points like a sky full of stars, and between the points, faint as threads of frost, new lines were forming, binding each point to the next.

His bones were being rebuilt. Not tempered, as the Nine Furnace Art tempered them with heat and blows and slow years. Rebuilt, joint by joint, with something that came from the sky.

It was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen.

In the frame, the second node, the one beside the first along the top row, flickered. Not lit. Flickered: a stutter of silver light, as if a lamp were trying to catch and failing, again, again.

Then the eastern sky began to pale, and the stars dimmed, and the lines of light thinned and broke, and it was over.

He lay on the roof until the sun was up. When he could move, he moved, slowly, testing each limb as if it were a new tool. He climbed down. He went to the millstone in the yard and bent and took the iron ring in both hands.

It came up off the ground so easily that he nearly threw it over his shoulder.

He set it down, and stood looking at it, and then at his hands. He felt something that took him a moment to recognise. The heat of the furnace of the organs, the thing he had been waiting weeks to feel. It was there. His blood was moving through him heavy and hot, the way the Nine Furnace Art said it should, and he could feel it pressing outward against the walls of his veins, pulsing with a thick, bronze weight. The bones of his body were so cold, so dense with starlight, that the blood was heating against them like water poured over a stone that has been left in the snow.

The 8th Forging. In one night.

He went back up onto the roof the next night, and the night after that.

It did not get easier. If anything it was worse, because now he knew it was coming. Each night he lay down against the chimney and closed his eyes and let the frame turn to the sky, and each night the needles came, and he lay rigid and wordless with his jaw locked and his eyes streaming, and each dawn he climbed down with his body thrumming like a struck bell. But each dawn there was more of it. His bones grew so dense that when he flicked one fingernail against his forearm, it gave back a faint, clear note. His blood ran hotter. On the second night, it began to move by itself, without his breath to guide it, round and round in one great slow circuit through his whole body, as if his heart had become a mill wheel in a river.

On the fifth night, two days before the trial, it all came together.

He felt it begin a little after midnight. The starlight was pouring into his bones as it always did, and his blood was circling as it always did, and then the two met. The cold of the bones and the heat of the blood rushed into each other, and instead of hurting, for the first time, it was sweet. A deep, ringing sweetness that went through skin and flesh, tendon and bone, marrow, organ and blood, all of it at once, as if a hundred notes struck separately had suddenly all been struck together and become a single chord. He felt his whole body become one thing. Not a collection of layers. One thing, every part of it joined to every other part, the way a bracket set is one thing.

The 9th Forging.

He lay on the tiles and laughed, very quietly, without any sound, with tears running down into his ears.

When he opened his eyes, his grandfather was standing on the ladder at the edge of the roof, looking at him.

Gu Yan did not know how long the old man had been there. Long enough. Gu Shouyi’s face, in the last starlight, was the colour of ash. He was staring at Gu Yan’s hands, at the thin silver light that still lay over the skin and was still, slowly, sinking into him.

“Grandfather,” Gu Yan said.

The old man did not answer. He came up the last rungs of the ladder, slowly, carefully, and lowered himself onto the tiles, and sat there beside his grandson in the dark with his bad hand in his lap. He was breathing as if he had run a long way.

“Starlight,” he said at last. His voice was very rough. “He told me once. About starlight. I thought he was raving.” He shut his eyes. “Heaven help us. I thought he was raving.”

“Who did?”

But the old man only shook his head. After a while he got up again and climbed down the ladder without a word, and Gu Yan, after a moment, followed.

Gu Shouyi went to the back of the workshop, to the big cedar chest that stood under the tool racks, the one that had always been locked. He knelt in front of it and took a key from a cord around his neck that Gu Yan had never noticed, and unlocked it, and lifted out a long bundle wrapped in oiled cloth.

He unwrapped it on the workbench. It was a sword.

A plain one. A straight double-edged jian, three chi long, its scabbard of black lacquered wood worn grey at the mouth, its hilt wrapped in dark cord. When the old man drew it, it came out with a soft whisper and lay on the bench in the lamplight: grey iron, unornamented, with a faint watered pattern in the steel and a small nick near the tip. It was not a spirit weapon. It was not even a fine one. It was the kind of sword a man might buy in any market in the Cang Kingdom for a few silver taels.

“Your father’s,” Gu Shouyi said. “The first sword he ever owned. He carried it until he was Heaven Seal and the clan gave him a better one, and then he carried this one anyway.” He reached into the chest again and took out a thin, battered book, its paper yellowed and soft as cloth, its cover stained with something that might have been tea or might have been something else. “And this.”

On the cover, in brushstrokes worn almost to nothing, were the words: Flowing Cloud Sword. Mortal-grade, high.

“It’s not a great art,” the old man said. “Any decent sect in the kingdom would laugh at it. But it’s what he learned on. And he said…” He hesitated. “He said once that it was enough, for a man who could see how it was put together.”

He pushed sword and book across the bench.

“Two days,” he said. “Learn what you can.”

Gu Yan picked up the book. Its pages fell open in his hands to the middle, where it had been opened most often, and he saw that the margins were covered in small, neat, closely written characters in a hand he had last seen on the wooden tablet of his mother’s grave.

His father’s hand.

Most of the notes were about the forms: a foot moved here, a wrist turned there. But at the bottom of the page, by itself, set apart from the rest, was a single line, written more heavily than the others, as if the brush had been pressed hard into the paper.

The clouds are not the sky. Look past them.
