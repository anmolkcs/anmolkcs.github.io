For six nights after the boar, Gu Yan tried to break into the frame, and for six nights it refused him.

He was more careful than he had been the first time. He did not lean on the nodes any more. He sat on the loft floor with the shutters closed and sank into the black sea of his mind and simply looked, the way one looks at a lock before choosing a pick. He looked at the lines until his eyes, which were not his eyes, ached. He counted their length in a measure that had no units. He tried thinking at the nodes, and then not thinking at them, and then feeling at them, which made him feel foolish. He tried holding in his mind the image of the boar’s tendon, the bright knot of lines he had seen and cut, as if he could offer it up to one of the empty points like a key.

Nothing. The nodes hung grey and patient, as they had for ten years.

On the fourth night he tried pushing, just a little, and woke with his face on the floorboards and a nosebleed that took half an hour to stop. On the fifth night he did not try anything at all and simply sat with the frame, and it was almost restful. On the sixth he gave up before midnight and went down the ladder to get water, and found his grandfather asleep in the chair by the stove with a half-finished bracket in his lap.

Gu Yan stood looking at the bracket for a while. Then he put a blanket over the old man and went back up, and lay awake, thinking about what his father had said. It will only show you what is already there.

What was already there?

The commission came from the clan’s ancestral hall, and it was the largest job the workshop had had in two years.

The roof over the hall’s eastern gallery was sagging. The steward had sent for builders from the prefecture city, and the builders had come and looked and named a price that had made the steward go briefly the colour of old rice, and then the patriarch himself had said, at a council meeting, that he did not see why the clan should pay prefecture prices when it had a man who had built half the roofs in Jade Ridge sitting on the edge of the compound making drawers.

So the old man went to look, with Gu Yan carrying the tools. He spent an hour on a ladder under the gallery eaves, saying nothing. Then he came down and said the trouble was not the roof. It was the brackets.

The gallery roof was carried on dougong: the interlocking wooden bracket sets that sat atop each pillar in every proper building in the Cang Kingdom, block on arm on block, spreading outward and upward like the branches of a tree to catch the weight of the eaves. There were twelve sets in the eastern gallery, each made of more than thirty pieces, and not one nail or peg in any of them. The pieces held each other. Two of the sets, a century old, had rotted at the core of their lowest blocks, and the whole gallery had been leaning on the remaining ten for longer than anyone knew.

“I’ll make two new sets,” Gu Shouyi told the steward. “Twenty days. Prefecture price for the timber. My price for the work.”

“What is your price for the work, elder?”

“Lunch,” said Gu Shouyi, and the steward, who had been bracing for something else entirely, said nothing at all for long enough that Gu Yan had to look away to keep from smiling.

They cut the pieces in the workshop over the next ten days, from a seasoned trunk of camphor pine the colour of honey. Gu Yan cut the long arms, the gong, and shaped their curved undersides; the old man cut the blocks, the dou, with their deep notched seats, and every joint between them. It was the joints that mattered. Gu Yan had seen his grandfather make a hundred joints and had made a few hundred himself, simple ones, mortise and tenon, half-laps, dovetails for drawers. He had never watched the old man make a full bracket set.

He watched now.

Gu Shouyi worked slowly. His bad hand could hold a piece steady but not grip a chisel, so he worked one-handed and with his whole body, bracing a block against his hip, setting the chisel’s edge on the line with infinite care, tapping it with a mallet held loosely between the fingers of his left hand. Each notch he cut was a little different from the last. When Gu Yan asked why, he said, “Because this one sits under that one, and that one carries the arm that reaches farthest, so it gets pushed from that side more. Look.” And he set two pieces together, and they slid into each other with a soft wooden sigh and locked, and when he let go they stayed locked, as if they had been carved from one block.

On the eleventh day, the old man assembled the first set on the workshop floor.

He began with the great block that would sit on the pillar’s head. Into its notches he set the first arms, crossing at right angles, and on those he set smaller blocks, and into those the next arms, longer, reaching outward, and on those more blocks, and so it climbed, layer on layer, each piece seated in the one below and holding down the one below it, until the set stood waist-high on the floor, spreading outward from its single base like a pine tree in wood. There was no glue. No peg. No nail.

When it was done, Gu Shouyi put both hands on the top, the good one and the bad, and leaned on it with all his weight.

It did not creak.

Gu Yan was not looking at it the way he looked at a finished piece of work. Without meaning to, he had let the other sight come.

The silver lines rose out of the bracket set, and he caught his breath.

In the boar, the lines had been a living tangle. In a beam of timber, they were simple: long and parallel, stretched or bunched by flaws. Here they were neither. They ran up through the great block and divided at the first joint, half into one arm, half into the other, and at every joint after that they divided again and crossed and met, flowing from piece to piece, from arm to block to arm. The old man’s weight pressed down on the top, and Gu Yan watched the force of it come down through the lines like water through a branching river, splitting and splitting, spreading out through thirty pieces of wood until each piece carried only a thirtieth of it, and all of it gathered again in the single great block at the base and passed down into the floor.

No single piece was strong enough to hold that weight. He could see that. Each arm, on its own, would have snapped. But the joints made them not thirty pieces. The joints made them one thing.

Everything holds because of how it is joined.

It was not a new thought. Any apprentice could have told him that; he could have told himself. But it came to him now not as a thought but as a sight, the lines of force flowing through the joints, and behind the sight he understood something larger, all at once and without words: that it was true not only of a bracket. It was true of the boar’s leg, bones held by tendons, the tendon a joint through which a charge was carried. It was true of his own body, which the Nine Furnace Art had been tempering layer by layer, skin to flesh to tendon to bone to marrow to organ, each layer joined to the next. It was true of a stone, the grains of it locked together, and of the ironwood stake, which had held for years on a single frayed line. It was true of a house, a clan, a town. Things were not strong because of what they were made of. They were strong because of how what they were made of was joined. And wherever something was joined, there was a line. And wherever there was a line, it could be followed, or it could be cut.

That was what he had been seeing, all his life. Not grain. Not cracks. The joins.

In the black sea behind his eyes, in the upper-left corner of the frame, the first node lit.

It did not flicker as the nodes had flickered on the night after Gu Lan’s news. It caught, the way a lamp wick catches from a taper, and held: a small, steady, silver light, cool and clear. Warmth flowed out from it along the two silver lines that ran from that corner, a little way along each, as if the frame had been cold for ten years and was only now beginning to remember what warmth was.

And the world changed.

He was still standing on the workshop floor with a handful of wood shavings in his hand. But the lines were there without his reaching for them, soft and clear as a reflection on still water: in the bracket set, in the workbench, in the beams of the roof overhead, in the iron of the stove. In his grandfather’s body, which was full of lines, though many of them were broken, dark, cut off. It did not hurt to see them. There was no needle behind his eyes. He could look away, and they faded; look back, and they returned, as easily as focusing on a near thing and then a far one.

“Yan’er.”

He blinked. His grandfather had straightened up and was looking at him.

“You look like a man who’s just been hit with a plank,” the old man said.

“I’m all right.” His voice came out hoarse. He swallowed. “It’s a good bracket.”

That night the old man closed the shutters and barred the door, which he never did, and put the lamp on the floor between them, and poured two cups of wine, which he did even less.

“Tell me,” he said.

So Gu Yan told him. The frame, the nine nodes, the boar, the bracket. The node in the corner, lit. He spoke quietly, and his grandfather listened without interrupting, turning the wine cup slowly in his good hand. When it was finished he was silent for a long time.

“Is it from Father?” Gu Yan asked. “The stone?”

“Yes.”

“What is it?”

“I don’t know.” The old man looked at him. “That’s the truth. I don’t know what it is, and I don’t know where he got it. He never told me. I think he thought it was safer for me not to know.”

“Safer from what?”

Gu Shouyi drank. He set the cup down on the floorboards very precisely, as if it were a piece of a joint.

“Your father,” he said, “was the best cultivator this clan has produced in three hundred years. You don’t know that, because after he left nobody talked about him, and I didn’t make them. He was Heaven Seal at twenty-six. At twenty-six. I was forty-four when I engraved my first seal, and they called me a talent.” He smiled faintly, without much humour. “He could have been patriarch. He could have gone to the capital and served the King. He could have gone to Taixu. They’d have taken him.”

“But he didn’t.”

“No. Because he was a man who saw too far.” The old man’s eyes were on the lamp flame. “Always. Even as a boy, he’d look at a thing and see something behind it. He’d ask questions that didn’t make sense. Once, when he was about your age, he asked me how a house knows which way to fall. I told him a house doesn’t know anything, it’s a house. He looked at me as if I’d disappointed him.” A pause. “I think you would have understood the question.”

Gu Yan thought of the bracket set, the lines of the old man’s weight flowing down through it. He did.

“When he was thirty, he started going away. Months at a time. To the capital, to the west, I don’t know where. He’d come back with that look on him, like he’d been staring into the sun. He stopped talking to the patriarch. He stopped talking to me, mostly. He talked to you. He’d take you up on the ridge at night and show you the stars.” The old man’s mouth tightened. “And then one night he came home soaked to the skin, and gave you that stone, and the next morning he was gone. He left me one sentence. He said he was going to look for the way up.”

“The way up,” Gu Yan repeated. “Up where?”

His grandfather lifted his head and looked, not at Gu Yan, but at the ceiling of the workshop, at the dark beams and the tiles above them and, beyond them, unseen, the heaped and endless stars.

“That,” said Gu Shouyi, “is the question that cost me my son.” He reached out and gripped Gu Yan’s shoulder with his ruined hand, so hard it hurt. “And I’ll tell you this once. Whatever is up there, whatever he went looking for, he was afraid of it. I never saw that man afraid of anything in his life. But the night he left, he was afraid. So you go to the Academy, if you win your place. You learn. You get strong. But you keep that frame of yours behind a closed door. Because if your father was afraid of whatever is looking for it…” He let go. “Then I don’t want it to find you.”

Outside, a wind had risen. It moved through the pines on the ridge with a long, low sound like the sea, and up in the loft a shutter Gu Yan had not fastened properly began, very softly, to knock.
