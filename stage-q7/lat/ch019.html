The Observatory Archive stood on its own small terrace on the Academy’s northern slope, a squat round building of dark brick with a copper dome turned green by centuries of rain. It belonged, strictly speaking, not to the Academy but to the Royal Star Observatory, which kept a branch of its records here for the use of the Observatory-line disciples who studied at Starfall. Outer-court disciples were not forbidden to enter. They were simply never let in.

The old attendant at the door looked at Gu Yan’s token, looked at Gu Yan, and opened his mouth to say no.

“He’s with me,” said Su Lingxi’s voice from inside. “He’s a specimen.”

The attendant closed his mouth, considered this, and stood aside.

The west wing was a long curved gallery following the line of the dome, lit by narrow windows of thick green glass that made the air look like the bottom of a pond. Shelves rose from floor to ceiling, and on the shelves were not books but scrolls, thousands upon thousands of them, in lacquered cases with labels of bone. The smell was of old paper, camphor, and a faint sharp coolness that Gu Yan could not place until he saw the bowl of crushed ice-crystal on every table, keeping the damp away.

Su Lingxi sat at the far end, at a table covered in sheets of paper filled with tiny, crowded characters. The jade compass lay in front of her. Beside it lay a row of silver needles of different lengths, a small brass balance, an inkstone, and a lacquered box with its lid open.

“Sit,” she said, without looking up. “Give me your hand.”

He sat. “Good morning to you too.”

“Good morning. Your hand.”

He gave it to her. She turned it palm up and laid two fingertips on his wrist. They were cold, colder than the room, and he felt a fine, precise thread of qi slip into his pulse: not the blunt probe Elder Ruan had used, but something thin and exact, like a needle being threaded. Ice, and something else under it, something silvery that reminded him, oddly, of starlight on the carriage roof.

“5th Vein,” she said. “You were 3rd at the examination. That was twenty-three days ago.”

“I took a pill.”

“One pill does not open two meridians.” She let go of his wrist and wrote something. “You also had a nosebleed in the last day. The vessels in the left nostril are freshly healed.” She looked up at last, her dark eyes very direct. “You get them when you look at things. On the stair. At the stele. At the Lu banquet, Qu Hai says, you were so pale he thought you’d been poisoned.”

“You talked to Qu Hai?”

“I talk to everyone. People simply don’t notice.” She set down her brush. “I’ve spent twenty-three days observing you. Here is what I know. One: my compass, which reads the thread of fate in every living thing, reads nothing from you. Not weak. Not severed. Nothing. Two: you comprehend structures, of any kind, faster than is reasonable. Master Fang Ziyou of the Formation Hall told my uncle you repaired a barrier pillar no one has been able to repair in two years. Three: when you do this, you pay for it in blood. Four: you draw starlight into your body at night, on the roof of the fourteenth building, and the Observatory’s own instruments on the dome above us register a very slight disturbance when you do.”

Gu Yan sat very still.

“I’m not going to tell anyone,” Su Lingxi said. “If I had wanted to tell anyone, I would have told my uncle on the first day, and you would be in a room in the Observatory having needles put in you by people much less polite than me.” She folded her hands on the table. “I want to understand it. That’s all. You are the most interesting thing I have ever measured.”

“That’s a strange compliment.”

“It isn’t a compliment. It’s a fact.” But the corner of her mouth moved, very slightly. “So. A proposal.”

The proposal, it turned out, had been written down. She slid a sheet of paper across the table to him. It was laid out in numbered clauses, like a contract.

One. Gu Yan will present himself at the Observatory Archive, west wing, on the first and fifteenth day of each month at the hour of the snake, for observation.

Two. Observation will be non-invasive. Needles will be used only on the surface of the skin, and only with consent.

Three. Su Lingxi will record all observations privately in a cipher of her own devising and will not share them with any person, including members of her own family, without Gu Yan’s consent.

Four. In exchange, Gu Yan will be granted access to the west wing as Su Lingxi’s research assistant, with permission to read any star chart, almanac or cosmological record kept therein.

Five. Either party may end the arrangement at any time.

He read it twice. Then he looked up. “You wrote a contract.”

“I thought you’d appreciate it. Mo Ji says you think like a merchant.”

“Mo Ji says a lot of things.” He looked at the paper again. “Why the star charts?”

“Because you want to read them,” she said simply. “On the day of the duels, when the Third Prince used his stars, every other person in that arena looked at him. You looked up.” She tilted her head. “And because the Archive is the one thing I have that you don’t, and a fair trade should be fair.”

He thought of Grandpa: Never speak of lines to anyone. He thought of the illusion, the grid going out and out into the dark. He thought of the deacon by the caravan fire. A frog who knows it is in a well.

The Observatory had been watching the sky for nine hundred years.

“I won’t tell you how I see things,” he said slowly. “Not yet. Maybe not ever. You can measure whatever you like. But I won’t explain it.”

“That’s acceptable,” said Su Lingxi immediately. “I prefer to work it out myself. Explanations are usually wrong anyway.” She picked up her brush and added a sixth clause in her small, precise hand: Six. Gu Yan is not required to explain anything. She turned the paper around and held out the brush.

He signed.

The observations, that first morning, were stranger than he had expected and less painful than he had feared.

She had him sit with the compass on his open palm while she watched its needle, which stayed perfectly still. She had him look at a candle flame through a lens of polished crystal, and asked what colour its edges were, and wrote down his answer. She laid three of her silver needles on the skin of his forearm, not piercing it, just resting there, and each one slowly turned until it lay along the line of a meridian; that pleased her very much. Then she asked him to look at the grain of the table, really look, and when he did, with the sight, at the barest edge of his strength, she watched his face so closely that he felt like a chart himself.

“Your pupils change,” she said. “Very slightly. They don’t dilate. They—” She frowned, searching for the word. “Sharpen. As if the eye were focusing on something closer than the table.” She wrote. “Thank you. That’s enough. Your nose is about to bleed.”

It was. He pinched it.

She produced a clean handkerchief from her sleeve and handed it to him without comment, and while he held it to his face, she went to the shelves and came back with a long lacquered case, very old, its black surface crazed with fine cracks. She opened it and drew out a scroll and unrolled it across the table, weighing its corners with the inkstone and the compass and two bowls of ice.

“Payment,” she said.

It was a chart of the sky.

Not a sky Gu Yan knew. It was drawn in faded ink on silk gone the colour of weak tea, and it showed the heavens as a great dome, a curved shell covering the land, with the stars set on its inner surface in their constellations. He recognised the Azure Dragon in the east, the Vermilion Bird in the south. Below the dome lay the world: a flat disc with mountains and rivers and seas drawn in miniature around its edges, and, far to the east, very small, a cluster of tiny marks that might have been kingdoms.

“Three thousand two hundred years old,” said Su Lingxi quietly. “Copied from an older copy. The original was made by an astronomer of a sect that doesn’t exist any more. It’s the oldest chart we have of the shape of the world.”

Gu Yan leaned closer.

At the edge of the dome, where the shell of the sky met the edges of the world-disc, the astronomer had drawn a line. Not a mountain range; a line, very fine, running all the way round. And alongside it, in archaic characters he could barely read:

界膜. World Membrane.

“The sky is not the sky,” Su Lingxi said. “The old astronomers knew it. Every cultivator who reaches the Mandate realm and flies high enough finds the same thing. The air thins, the qi grows strange, and then there is something. Not a wall. A skin. You can press against it. You can’t go through. The stars we see are on the other side of it, or what we see of them is.”

It’s a ceiling, Yan’er.

He heard himself say, “My grandfather calls it a ceiling.”

“Your grandfather,” said Su Lingxi, “is more correct than most of the Observatory.” She touched the chart, very lightly, at the centre of the world-disc. “Look here.”

At the very middle of the world, a single vertical stroke rose from the land to touch the inner surface of the dome. It was drawn with more care than anything else on the chart: a column, its sides ruled perfectly straight, so tall that it rose past every mountain, past the clouds the astronomer had sketched in curling lines, all the way up to meet the World Membrane. Where it touched the membrane, the ink had been worked into a small, dense knot.

Beside it, the astronomer had written two characters: 天柱.

“The Sky Pillar,” said Su Lingxi. “At the centre of Azure Dust World. No one from the Cang Kingdom has ever seen it. It’s half a world away, past the Eastern Wastes, past the central region, past seas no ship has crossed. But every chart in this archive older than a thousand years shows it.” Her finger moved up the column. “The old records say it is the only place where the World Membrane can be passed. Only a Sovereign may climb it. At the top, the heavens send down a tribulation. Survive it, and you ascend.” She took her hand away. “Where to, the records disagree.”

Gu Yan did not answer.

He was looking at the chart with the sight now. He could not have stopped himself. The ink lines were only ink; but the astronomer who had drawn the original had been a cultivator, and had put something of his understanding into the drawing, and the copyist had been skilled enough to carry some of it across. The sky-dome had a faint shape of intention to it, curved and closed. The Pillar had a shape too, a single rising line, very strong.

And behind everything, behind the dome and the disc and the pillar, so faint he thought at first it was the weave of the silk, ran other lines.

Ruled lines. Thin, straight, evenly spaced. Crossing each other at right angles.

“What are these?” he asked. His voice sounded odd to him.

Su Lingxi bent to look. “The guide-lines? Copyists rule them so their characters stay straight. You’ll find them on half the scrolls in this room.”

“No.” He traced one with his fingertip, not quite touching the silk. It ran beneath the World Membrane and through it, without a break, and out the other side, and off the edge of the chart. And there, past the dome, where the astronomer had drawn nothing at all, two of the ruled lines crossed, and at their crossing was a tiny dot of ink so faint it was nearly invisible.

A node.

He looked further. There was another, past the dome’s other side. And another, very high, at the top of the scroll, where the ruled lines met beyond the sky. Each one a tiny dot. Each one, he saw now with the sight, carrying the faintest knot of that same intention that the astronomer had put into the World Membrane and the Pillar. As though each of those dots were, itself, a world.

Like lanterns, hanging on a frame.

“They don’t keep the characters straight,” he said. “There aren’t any characters out there.”

Su Lingxi was silent for a long moment. Then she bent closer, until her face was almost touching the silk, and looked where his finger pointed. He heard her breath catch.

“I’ve looked at this chart a hundred times,” she said softly.

“You were looking at the sky.”

“And you weren’t?”

“I was looking at what holds it up.”

She straightened slowly and looked at him, and there was something in her face he had not seen before: not the impersonal interest of a scholar with a specimen, but something closer and more uncertain, as if the specimen had turned its head and looked back.

In his mind, the Lattice stirred. The nine nodes in their small square frame, two of them lit. And for an instant, so brief he might have imagined it, he felt the silver lines at the frame’s edge pull outward, very slightly, like threads drawn taut by a hand somewhere far away.

Then it was still.

“First and fifteenth,” said Su Lingxi, after a moment. Her voice was perfectly level again. She began, carefully, to roll up the chart. “The hour of the snake. Don’t be late.” She paused, the scroll half-furled. “And Gu Yan. Elder Han Qiu is holding a trial for his sword students in three days. In the plum yard, at dawn. My uncle says he holds it every year and has not taken a single disciple from it in eleven.”

“Why are you telling me?”

“Because I’d like to observe what happens,” said Su Lingxi, “and I’d prefer that you not embarrass my research.” She slid the scroll into its case and closed the lid. “Your nose is bleeding again.”
