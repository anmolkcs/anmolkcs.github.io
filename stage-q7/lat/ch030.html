By noon, the whole Academy knew.

By evening, it had a name for him.

“Zero Star,” Mo Ji said, dropping onto his bunk so hard that the frame groaned. “That’s what they’re calling you. In the dining hall. In the library. A third-year in the inner court said it to my face, and I very nearly put him through a wall.” He glared at the ceiling. “I didn’t, because I’m civilised now. But I thought about it in great detail.”

“Thank you for not putting him through a wall.”

“It’s not funny, Yan.”

“It’s a little funny.”

Mo Ji rolled over and stared at him. “You opened a Star Sea, and the Mirror said you had nothing in it, and now four thousand people think you’re a cripple with good footwork, and you think that’s funny?”

Gu Yan sat by the window with his father’s iron sword across his knees, running a whetstone along the new notch near the hilt. In his Star Sea, nine grey stars turned slowly in their silver frame, heavy as iron. He could feel each of them, the way you can feel your own teeth with your tongue.

“I think,” he said, “that the Mirror is wrong.”

Mo Ji was quiet for a while.

“Su Lingxi said the same thing,” he admitted finally. “Not to me. To Gu Lan. She said her compass broke. It spun so fast the needle came off its pin.” He paused. “Is it wrong?”

“Yes.”

“How do you know?”

Gu Yan put down the whetstone. “Come to the training ground tomorrow at dawn,” he said. “Bring something heavy.”

Most of it was not cruel. That surprised him a little.

He had expected mockery. There was some: a laugh in the dining hall behind his back, the nickname written in chalk on the dormitory gate one morning and scrubbed off by Gu Lan before breakfast with a fury that frightened the gate-keeper. A pair of outer-court disciples who had cheered for him at the exhibition passed him on the path and looked away, embarrassed, as if they had backed a horse that turned out to be lame.

But mostly, what he got was pity, and pity was harder to bear than scorn.

A deacon at the merit office lowered his voice when he spoke to Gu Yan, as if to an invalid. An elder of the Formation Hall, who had praised his repair of the west wall pillar, stopped him on the Stele Forest path and said kindly that zero-star cultivators often made excellent formation masters, since formation work relied on comprehension rather than raw qi, and that there would always be a place for him in the Hall. A girl he had never spoken to left a packet of Foundation-Mending Pills outside his door with a note that said only I’m sorry. He did not know who she was. He gave the pills to the infirmary.

Gu Feng found him on the third day, at the edge of the herb terraces, and stood beside him for a long time without speaking. The main-family heir had reached Ninth Vein himself a few days before. They had never been friends. Gu Feng had never, in sixteen years, gone out of his way to speak to the carpenter’s grandson unless there was a trial or a clan duty to discuss.

“The Mirror’s never been wrong,” Gu Feng said at last, looking at the terraces.

“So they say.”

“It’s wrong.” Gu Feng’s jaw tightened. “I fought you at the clan trial. I lost. I’ve thought about that fight every day since. You read every step I took before I took it.” He turned and looked at Gu Yan directly, for perhaps the first time in their lives. “Nobody who fights like that has a hollow sea. Whatever the Mirror says.” He looked away again. “That’s all. I wanted to say it.”

He walked off before Gu Yan could answer, back straight, arms swinging, as if he had just completed an unpleasant clan duty.

Gu Yan watched him go and found, to his surprise, that his throat was tight.

The training ground at dawn was empty except for the dew, the deacon who swept it, and the Strength Pillar.

It stood at the north end of the ground, a column of black iron five zhang high, its surface engraved with characters that measured force in jin. Every disciple of the Academy struck it at least once a season. The marks of the strongest were inscribed on a bronze board beside it.

Gu Yan had struck it a month ago, at Seventh Vein, the day after the Star Tower. The characters had flared to twenty-two thousand jin, which was respectable. Mo Ji, at Sixth Vein with an Earth body built for exactly this, had struck nineteen thousand and sulked for an afternoon.

“I brought something heavy,” Mo Ji said. He had brought an anvil. He set it down on the dew-wet ground with a thud that shook the dirt. “My father’s old one. Two thousand jin. I don’t know what you want it for.”

“Throw it at me.”

“What?”

“Not now. After.” Gu Yan stepped up to the Strength Pillar and set his feet.

He did not use a technique. He did not draw his sword. He simply closed his right hand into a fist, breathed, and let the Star Sea rise.

It came up out of the dark of his dantian like the tide over a mudflat: slow, then fast, then irresistible. The nine grey stars turned, and as they turned they pushed, and the qi that flowed out of them into his meridians was not the bright, quick qi of his Vein days. It was dense, heavy, compressed, like water a thousand zhang deep. It filled his channels to the brim and kept pressing, and the starbone in his arm drank it and hardened, and he felt, for the first time, the full weight of what he had built in the closed room.

He struck.

The sound was not a clang. It was a boom, low and deep, that rolled across the training ground and set the dew trembling on the grass. The iron pillar shuddered from base to crown. The engraved characters blazed up its length, one after another, up past the mark where his fist had stopped a month ago, up past the marks on the bronze board, and stopped.

Forty-eight thousand jin.

Mo Ji stared at the characters. The deacon dropped his broom.

“That’s—” Mo Ji began, and stopped. “The Third Prince struck fifty-one when he opened his Star Sea. With seven stars.” He turned to Gu Yan, and his face went through several expressions very quickly before settling on outrage. “You hit it like you were knocking on a door!”

“I wanted to see,” Gu Yan said. His hand did not hurt. “Throw the anvil.”

Mo Ji looked at him, then at the anvil. Then, with the expression of a man who has decided that the world is mad and he might as well join it, he braced his legs, gripped the two-thousand-jin block of iron, and heaved it overhand with all the strength of his Earth body.

Gu Yan caught it.

He did not brace. He did not step back. He put up his left hand and the anvil hit his palm and stopped, as though it had flown into a wall, and he held it there at arm’s length, two thousand jin of iron, and felt the nine grey stars turn slowly in his Star Sea, entirely unbothered.

He put it down gently on the grass.

“Double,” he said. “More than double. Everything. Strength, qi, how long I can keep going. My body drinks from the Star Sea and the Star Sea doesn’t run dry.” He looked at his hand. “The Mirror says I have nothing to amplify my qi. But something is amplifying it.”

Mo Ji opened his mouth, shut it, and sat down on the anvil.

“I’m not going to ask,” he said at last. “You’ll tell me when you tell me. But I’m going to say this once, so listen.” He jabbed a thick finger toward the Academy, the white halls, the Mirror Hall at the far end. “When you’re ready to show them, I want to be standing at the front. I want to see their faces.”

“I’ll make sure of it.”

“Good.” Mo Ji folded his arms. “Zero Star.” He snorted. “Zero Star, my backside.”

“Mirrors measure what they were built to measure,” said Han Qiu.

It was evening in the courtyard. The plum tree had begun to drop its leaves, and they lay on the stone table in small brown curls. Gu Yan had come to report the Strength Pillar. The Elder, it turned out, already knew; the deacon who swept the training ground was apparently a man of very few words and very fast feet.

“The second principal forged that Mirror from a fallen star,” Han Qiu went on, “and tuned it to the stars of this world. Bright stars. White, blue, gold, red. Stars that burn. It can count a fire. It cannot count a stone.” He looked at Gu Yan. “Whatever you have in there, it does not burn. So the Mirror does not see it.”

“Then what is it, Elder?”

“I don’t know.” Han Qiu said it simply, without embarrassment. “I’m a swordsman, not a star-reader. But I know a sword when I feel one, and I felt the weight of your sea through forty steps of rock and a stone door. It was not hollow.” He paused. “I have known exactly one other man whose sea felt like that.”

Gu Yan waited. The Elder did not say the name. He did not need to.

“Let them call you Zero Star,” Han Qiu said. “It’s the best disguise you could have asked for. A Nine-Star genius would have every eye in the kingdom on him, and every ducal house trying to buy him or kill him, and the Holy Land’s envoys sniffing at his door. A zero-star disciple is nothing. No one watches nothing.” His voice dropped. “And there are things in this world, I think, that watch for something very like what you have. Be nothing, for as long as you can.”

Gu Yan thought of the Principal’s sharp gaze sliding over him in the Hall of Ten Thousand Stars, and the endless grid in the dead stone, and the cold, enormous thing moving in the wound beyond it.

“Yes, Elder.”

The announcement came four days later, pasted on the great board in front of the Star Tower in characters a hand high, under the seal of the Principal himself.

By order of Principal Wen Taiyuan and with the consent of His Majesty: the outer boundary of Fallen Star Valley will weaken on the first day of the Eleventh Month. Starfall Academy will send an expedition of twelve disciples under the command of Elder Han Qiu, to accompany the Royal Guard and such ducal parties as His Majesty permits. Selection is by realm, merit and the judgement of the Elder. The list follows.

Eleven names stood on the main list. The twelfth line had been left blank, marked only at the Elder’s discretion.

The crowd before the board was so thick that Gu Yan could not get within thirty paces. He did not need to. He could hear the names being read aloud by the people at the front, rippling backward through the crowd like stones skipped across a pond.

Third Prince Cang Yi. Of course. Lu Mingyuan, heir of the Lu clan. Several inner-court disciples at Mid and Late Star Sea whose names he knew only by reputation. Su Lingxi, of the Royal Star Observatory, for the reading of the Valley’s stars. Gu Feng, at Ninth Vein, the youngest on the list. And then, to a roar of laughter and disbelief from the outer-court disciples at the back, Mo Ji, whose Earth body is suited to the shifting weight of the Valley’s ground.

Mo Ji, beside him, went completely white.

“Me?” he said. “Me?”

Gu Yan grinned and thumped him on the back. Then he listened for the rest.

There was a second list beneath the first, in smaller characters: the reserves. Six names, to be called on only if one of the twelve fell ill or was injured before the Eleventh Month. The voices at the front read them out with less interest.

The last of them was his.

Gu Yan. Early Star Sea. Zero stars.

He stood still in the crowd. Around him, people who had heard his name glanced at him, and away, and at each other. A few looked sorry. One or two looked relieved, as if an uncomfortable question had been answered. The reserve list was a kindness, everyone understood that. It was the Academy’s way of saying we remember you fought the Prince well without having to send a zero-star cultivator into a valley that killed Heaven Seal elders.

He had expected it. He had told himself, walking down from the courtyard that morning, that he would expect it. It still landed in his chest like a fist.

Six weeks. A valley that had swallowed his father. And he would watch from the gate.

The crowd parted.

It did not part because anyone asked it to. It parted the way grass parts before wind, disciples stepping aside without knowing why, conversations dying, until there was a clear path from the edge of the plaza to the great board. Han Qiu walked down it in his plain grey robe with his hands behind his back.

He did not look at the crowd. He did not look at Gu Yan. He stopped in front of the board, read the main list once, slowly, from top to bottom, and then held out his hand to the deacon who stood beside it.

The deacon, trembling slightly, gave him a brush.

Han Qiu reached up and drew a single, clean line through the last name on the reserve list. Then, on the blank twelfth line of the main list, in a hand as sharp and certain as a cut through bamboo, he wrote a name.

Gu Yan.

He handed back the brush. He turned around. For the first time, he looked across the silent plaza and found Gu Yan in the crowd, and held his eyes.

“The expedition is under my command,” said Elder Han Qiu, to no one and to everyone. “I’m taking him.”
