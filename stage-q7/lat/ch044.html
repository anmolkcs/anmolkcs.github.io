“You are certain,” said Han Qiu.

“I’m certain of what I saw. I’m not certain what it was.”

They sat in the elder’s quarters, a bare room with a sword rack, a tea table, and a window facing the cliff. Han Qiu had listened to the whole account without interrupting. Now he turned his cup slowly on the table, a quarter-turn at a time, which Gu Yan had learned meant he was thinking hard.

“If you accuse him and you are wrong,” the elder said, “the Lu clan will say Starfall Academy is persecuting them out of spite for the Valley. The King will be embarrassed. The envoy will watch the whole kingdom quarrel like fishwives. And Lu Kang will be a martyr.” Another quarter-turn. “If you accuse him and you are right, but he hides it well enough that no judge can see it, the result is the same.”

“Then I shouldn’t accuse him.”

“No. You should fight him.” Han Qiu set down the cup. “It is your bracket. He must strike you to win. If he uses that art on you, you will be the only person in the arena who can see it at work, and you will be standing closer to it than anyone. What you do then is your affair.” He looked at Gu Yan levelly. “Do not kill him. Do not use the step. And do not let your seal slip. Whatever is in his chest, it is not worth what would happen if the envoy saw what is in yours.”

They fought on the central platform, the one directly below the royal pavilion, at the second bell.

Lu Kang was taller than he had seemed from the rail, with a long face and heavy-lidded eyes and the Lu clan’s sky-blue silk tied back at the sleeves. He bowed correctly. He did not sneer at Gu Yan’s board, or say anything at all. When the judge — a Heaven Seal elder from the Ministry of Rites, grey-bearded and bored — raised his hand, Lu Kang settled into a low, wide stance with both palms forward, and the air around his hands began to shimmer with heat.

“Begin.”

Lu Kang came in like a wave breaking.

It was a good art. Gu Yan had to admit that within three exchanges. Crimson Tide Palm did not strike once and withdraw; it struck in surges, palm after palm after palm, each blow carrying the momentum of the one before, so that defending against it was like standing in surf. The first surge drove Gu Yan back four steps. The second drove him back three more. He parried with the flat of the iron sword, and each parry rang up his arm and left his fingers numb.

And each time a palm came near him — even when it missed — he felt it. A faint, sickly tug, as though something had brushed the inside of his skin.

He let the third surge come. He read its lines as it formed, saw the eight blows it would contain and the order they would come in, and he slipped six of them and took the seventh on the sword and let the eighth, deliberately, land on his left shoulder.

It burned. It was a fire art; that was to be expected. Heat went into his shoulder, deep into the muscle, and his skin blistered under the silk. But that was not what made him clench his teeth.

When Lu Kang’s palm withdrew, something came with it.

Gu Yan felt it go. A thread of his own qi, a hair’s breadth thick, drawn out through the place where the palm had touched him — and wound around it, darker and wetter, a thread of his blood essence, the deep vital stuff that was not blood itself but the life inside the blood. It was so small it was almost nothing. A drop from a well. But it went out of him, and he felt the lack of it, and in his sea of consciousness the Lattice lit up with alarm as though someone had cut one of its lines.

He jumped back three zhang and looked.

Now that the thread had come from him, he could follow it. It ran from his shoulder across the gap to Lu Kang’s palm — already fading, already almost invisible — and up through Lu Kang’s arm along a path no ordinary meridian took, and into Lu Kang’s chest. There, under his breastbone, was the knot Gu Yan had seen from the rail: a tight, folded tangle of lines, layered over and over like a bandage wound around a wound. The thread went into it and vanished.

Inside the knot, something drank.

“You’re slow for a Mid Star Sea,” said Lu Kang. His voice was soft and reasonable. “Perhaps you should surrender before you’re hurt.”

“Perhaps,” said Gu Yan, and came forward.

He could not get close enough.

That was the problem, and it took him the next twenty exchanges to understand how large a problem it was. Lu Kang was a full sub-level above him and had been fighting with the Crimson Tide Palm since he was a child. Every time Gu Yan pressed in toward his chest, a surge met him and drove him back. Every time he tried to circle, Lu Kang turned with him, smooth as a door on oiled hinges. And every palm that touched him — on the forearm, the hip, the back of the hand — took another thread.

It was not much. A drop, and a drop, and a drop.

But by the thirtieth exchange Gu Yan’s legs felt heavy, and his breath came short, and when he looked down at his sword hand he saw that his knuckles had gone grey. In the stands, people had begun to murmur. He could hear it as a change in the sound of the crowd, a rising note of interest. The zero-star boy is losing. He’s lasting longer than anyone thought, but he’s losing.

He could have ended it. He knew that. One step through space, and he would be behind Lu Kang with the iron sword at his neck before the man could turn. Or one real sword strike, with real intent — the cut Han Qiu had been drilling into him on the cliff for two months, the cut that left a mark the world had to accommodate — and Lu Kang’s surge would part like water.

But the step was a door Han Qiu had told him to keep shut, and the intent would show. And neither would prove anything about the knot.

So he went inward.

He had done it before, on the cliff, in the dark of the dormitory roof, in the silence of the Valley’s inner zone. He let the arena fade. He let the next surge come at him and gave it only enough of his attention to survive it. And the rest of him, all the rest, he turned toward his Star Sea.

Nine stars hung in the dark, grey under the Remnant’s seal. Beneath them, around them, holding them, the frame of the Lattice: five nodes lit, four dark. And everywhere in his body, in the marrow of his bones, layered there night after night for two months, starlight — refined through the second node, squeezed thin through the seal, stored away more patiently than he had realised, waiting.

He had been treating the ceiling above Mid Star Sea as a thing to be reached someday.

Lu Kang’s next palm took a thread from his ribs, and Gu Yan thought, No. Not someday.

He opened his bones.

The starlight came up out of him like water out of a struck spring.

It did not go outward. It went inward, into the Star Sea, flooding it from beneath — and the nine grey stars, which had been lamps behind frosted paper for two months, drank it all at once. He felt them swell. He felt the space between them stretch, the whole sea widening as if the walls of a room were being pushed outward by hands he could not see. There was a pressure in his dantian like a held breath, then like a mountain resting on a single point, and then the ceiling he had been crouching under for two months simply was not there anymore.

He stood up into the space where it had been.

It was not violent. That surprised him. Breaking into the Vein Awakening realm had felt like being struck by lightning; igniting his Star Sea in Han Qiu’s closed room had felt like dying and being born at once. This felt like coming out of a low doorway into a tall hall. Everything in him straightened. His qi, which had been thinning under Lu Kang’s assault, came roaring back up his meridians, doubled, then more than doubled, dense and cool and faintly silver in a way that only he could see.

And the seal held. He felt it strain — felt the silver of his stars press against the grey like sunlight against shutters — and he held it down by main force, the way the Remnant had shown him, and the grey stayed grey.

Late Star Sea.

He opened his eyes.

Lu Kang’s surge was arriving. Eight palms, as before. But Gu Yan’s sight had sharpened with his realm, and now the knot in Lu Kang’s chest was no longer a tangle. He could see its structure. It was a formation — a tiny one, grown rather than carved, anchored to Lu Kang’s heart by four lines that ran out to his shoulders and hips. Four lines, holding a bandage closed over a mouth.

He did not step back this time. He stepped in, into the surge, and let the first palm hit his chest full on — and his new qi met it and his starbone body took it, and he did not move. The second palm landed on his arm and he did not move. Lu Kang’s eyes widened.

The iron sword went past his guard, light as a brush stroke, and touched him four times. Right shoulder. Left shoulder. Right hip. Left hip.

Gu Yan did not cut flesh. He barely cut silk. What he cut, with the thinnest thread of intent he could make and still call a cut, was the four lines.

The knot came undone.

Lu Kang screamed.

A mist burst out of his chest — out through his skin and his silk alike, as though it had been under pressure — a thick, dark-red mist that smelled of iron and rot. It billowed across the platform. Where it touched the formation wall at the platform’s edge, the wall hissed and darkened. And in the middle of the mist, for a moment, Gu Yan saw a shape: a pale crescent, red-rimmed, hanging in the air above Lu Kang’s head like a moon seen through smoke.

On the tiers, ten thousand people went silent all at once. Then the screaming started there, too.

Lu Kang had fallen to his knees. He was clawing at his chest. The mist was still pouring from him, thinner now, and as it went his face sank, his cheeks hollowing, as though it had been holding him up from inside. “No,” he was saying. “No, no, they said it couldn’t be seen, they said—”

The Ministry judge was no longer bored. He was on the platform with his hand raised and a Heaven Seal domain clamped over Lu Kang like a lid on a pot, and his face was white with fury. “Blood art,” he said, loud enough to carry. “On the King’s platform. Before the envoy of Taixu.”

From the royal pavilion, a single voice spoke. It did not seem loud. But it filled the bowl of the Arena of Ten Thousand Steps the way water fills a cup, and every person in it heard it as though it were spoken beside their ear.

“Seize him.”

Royal guards in black lacquer were already on the platform. They hauled Lu Kang up between them. His heels dragged on the jade. As they took him past Gu Yan, Lu Kang turned his ruined face and looked at him with an expression Gu Yan would remember for a long time: not hate, but bewilderment, the look of a man who had been promised something by people who lied.

The judge turned to Gu Yan. “Lu Kang is disqualified. The match is yours.” He hesitated. “Are you injured, disciple?”

Gu Yan looked down at himself. His shoulder was blistered and his knuckles were still grey, but the colour was returning, and the qi in him was humming like a struck bell. “Not badly.”

“Then go and see a physician anyway. And — well done.”

By noon the whole city knew. By evening, so did the envoy.

The Lu clan’s Third Elder knelt before the royal pavilion in sackcloth and declared that his grandson had been seduced by demonic cultivators without the knowledge of his family, and that the Lu clan, which had already lost one traitorous steward to the Blood Moon Sect, would give up another if the King commanded it. The King did not command it. He did not say anything. He simply let the old man kneel there in the sun for an hour while the crowds watched, and then had him helped to his feet and taken home.

Gu Yan heard all of this from Mo Ji, who heard it from everyone. He was sitting on the steps outside the physicians’ pavilion with a salve on his shoulder, feeling the new depth of his Star Sea with a kind of wonder, when he looked up and found the Lu clan’s box across the arena — high on the western tiers, its silk awnings drawn half-closed.

Lu Mingyuan was standing at its rail, alone, looking down.

Their eyes met across four hundred zhang of stone and crowd and evening air. Lu Mingyuan’s face was calm. Then his lips moved, shaping a single word, slow and deliberate, so that there could be no mistake.

Silver.
