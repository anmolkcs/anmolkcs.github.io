The first semifinal was his.

Gu Yan walked out of the tunnel into a silence he had not heard in the arena before. Ten thousand steps of people, and none of them shouting. They had all heard by now — about the third pillar, about the King’s domain, about the patriarch of the Lu clan sitting in his box all night between two royal guards with his cane across his knees. And they had all, Gu Yan thought, done the same arithmetic. A Lu clan grandson exposed by the zero-star boy. The zero-star boy nearly crushed by a Lu clan “accident.” And now the zero-star boy against the Lu clan heir himself.

Lu Mingyuan was already on the platform.

He stood with his hands clasped behind his back, in sky-blue silk embroidered with silver cranes, his long hair bound up in a jade crown. He looked rested. He looked, in fact, better than Gu Yan had ever seen him — the pallor he had carried since the Valley was gone, and there was colour in his cheeks and a brightness in his eyes, as though he had slept well and woken to good news. A slender sword hung at his hip.

His board read: Lu Mingyuan. Starfall Academy, inner court; Lu clan. Peak Star Sea. Stars: 7.

Gu Yan climbed the steps and bowed. Lu Mingyuan returned it with perfect correctness.

“You got my note,” he said.

“I did.”

“You didn’t answer it.”

“I didn’t think it was a question you wanted answered with words.”

Lu Mingyuan smiled. It was a real smile, and that was the worst thing about it. “No,” he said. “I suppose it wasn’t.”

The judge — the same grey-bearded Ministry elder from the Lu Kang match, who had evidently asked for this one — looked at both of them for a long moment, then stepped back and raised his hand.

“Begin.”

For the first hundred exchanges, Lu Mingyuan fought clean.

It frightened Gu Yan more than anything that came after. He had faced Lu Mingyuan in the Valley, in the red light of the blood formation, with Mo Ji screaming in chains and Han Qiu and Crimson Moth tearing the sky apart above them. He had won that fight by the narrowest margin, with nine silver stars blazing out of him, and he had thought he understood how Lu Mingyuan fought.

He had not understood. In the Valley, Lu Mingyuan had been Late Star Sea and fighting to escape. Here he was Peak Star Sea, a full stage above him, and fighting to win, and he had brought a sword. In the Valley he had fought with his palms. Gu Yan had not known he could do this. His swordsmanship was beautiful.

There was no other word. Lu Mingyuan’s slender blade moved like ink on silk, in long, flowing strokes that never stopped and never seemed to hurry. Every cut led into the next. Every parry became a thrust. He used an art the board did not name, which Gu Yan guessed was something the Lu clan kept for its heirs: Earth-grade high at least, perhaps more, and it had been taught to him from childhood by the best masters a ducal clan could buy. Gu Yan read its lines and found them long and graceful and almost seamless, a river with no rocks.

He was driven back across the platform. He took a cut on the left arm, then another along the jaw. He gave ground, and gave ground, and found that the ground he gave was being chosen for him: Lu Mingyuan was herding him, gently, toward the north-east corner, where the platform’s edge would leave him no room to retreat.

“You’re very good,” Gu Yan said, between breaths.

“I’ve always been very good.” Lu Mingyuan’s voice did not strain. “That was never the problem.”

He turned a parry into a thrust that grazed Gu Yan’s ribs, through the bandage, into Ye Han’s half-healed cut. Gu Yan bit back a sound.

“Do you know what the problem was?” Lu Mingyuan said. “The Lu clan was a ducal clan when the Cang Kingdom was founded. There were nine of us then. Now there are five, and in a hundred years there will be three, and the Lu clan will not be one of them. My grandfather is Heaven Seal. His grandfather was Mandate. Every generation, a little less. Every generation, a little more land sold, a little more pride swallowed, a little more bowing to a royal house that was once our equal.” The slender sword flickered; Gu Yan blocked by instinct. “And above us all, the Holy Land. And above the Holy Land, the Pillar. And above the Pillar — who knows? You know. You’ve seen it. That’s what I said in the note. You’ve seen it, and you’re hiding it.”

“And the Blood Moon Sect offered you a way up.”

“They offered me a way out of the well,” said Lu Mingyuan. “What would you have done?”

He pressed in. Gu Yan’s back foot found the edge of the platform.

“I wouldn’t have fed people to it,” Gu Yan said.

And he stopped retreating.

He went in through the river.

There were no rocks in Lu Mingyuan’s art; Gu Yan could not find a flaw to cut along. So he did what he had done against Ye Han. He chose a line of his own — straight through the centre of the flowing strokes — and cut along it with intent, and Lu Mingyuan’s beautiful sword met the plain iron one and stopped, for the first time in a hundred exchanges.

The jade between their feet cracked.

Lu Mingyuan’s eyes widened. Then they narrowed. Then, slowly, he stepped back, and lowered his sword, and smiled again.

“There it is,” he said. “Very well.”

He stopped hiding.

It came out of him like a tide coming in. Not mist, as with Lu Kang. Lu Mingyuan’s blood art did not leak; it rose, a deep and steady crimson welling up behind his skin, so that for a moment his face was flushed like a man drunk on wine. His seven stars, which the arena’s formation projected faintly above his head as it did for every fighter, changed colour one by one — from clear to rose, from rose to red, from red to the dark, clotted crimson of old blood. The air on the platform grew warm and wet and smelled of iron. On the tiers, people began to stand.

And under all of it, as before, Gu Yan felt the concealment.

It was layered over Lu Mingyuan’s true nature like lacquer over wood: coat after coat after coat, each one thin and perfect, sealing the blood art in and the world’s eyes out. Even now, with the art in full flood, the judge only frowned, and raised his hand as though to test the air, and hesitated. What showed was a fire-attribute surge, an aggressive burst of qi from a desperate Peak Star Sea heir. What did not show — what could not be seen without eyes that saw structure — was where it came from.

Lu Mingyuan came at him, and he was twice as fast.

The next thirty exchanges were the worst of Gu Yan’s life.

Lu Mingyuan’s sword was still beautiful. Now it was beautiful and hungry. Every cut, even the ones Gu Yan blocked, pulled at him — not the faint hair-thin threads Lu Kang had drawn, but ropes, cables, drawing out his blood essence in long, sickening drags that left his limbs cold and his vision grey at the edges. He felt his Late Star Sea qi, so new and so hard-won, draining away like water through a cracked jar. He took cuts on both thighs. He took the flat of the sword across the temple and saw stars that were not his own.

But he was looking, the whole time.

He was looking past the sword, past the flushed face and the red stars, through the coats of lacquer, into Lu Mingyuan’s chest. It took every scrap of perception he had. Blood ran from his nose and down over his lips and he did not wipe it. Somewhere very far away, his skull was splitting.

And there, at last, it was.

It was not a knot like Lu Kang’s. It was a seed. It sat in the very centre of Lu Mingyuan’s Star Sea, among his seven red stars, like a pit in a fruit: small, black-red, pulsing slowly, and from it ran seven fine lines, one to each of his stars, feeding them. It was not a formation that had been grown. It had been planted. And Gu Yan, who carried a seed of his own in the depths of his mind, knew with absolute certainty, the way one knows one’s own handwriting, whose hand had planted it.

Crimson Moth.

The Blood Moon Sect had not just made a bargain with the Lu clan’s heir. They had put something inside him.

Lu Mingyuan saw his face change and knew, in that instant, that Gu Yan had seen it. For the first time in the fight, real fear came into his eyes.

He attacked with everything.

Gu Yan did not dodge it. He could not have; there was nowhere to go and nothing left in his legs. He stepped forward instead, into the hungry red sword, and let it pass along his left side — it opened him from hip to ribs, and he felt the blood essence pour out of the wound toward it in a torrent — and as it passed he put the point of the iron sword against Lu Mingyuan’s breastbone.

He did not push. He did not need to. He had read seven lines, and now, with the thinnest, most precise intent he had ever made, he cut them.

Not the stars. Not the man. The lines between.

One. Two. Three. He felt each one part like a taut string. Four. Five. Lu Mingyuan’s mouth opened. Six.

Seven.

The seed came loose from its roots, and the lacquer cracked.

What poured out of Lu Mingyuan then was no mist. It was a moon. A vast crimson crescent rose up out of his chest and above the platform and above the arena, swelling as it rose, until it hung over the Arena of Ten Thousand Steps like a second sky — red as a wound, pocked and veined like a living thing, and from its lower rim there dripped, slowly, long threads of something that was not quite blood. Every person in the bowl could see it. Every person in the bowl could feel it, a damp, appalling hunger pressing down on them from above.

It was the emblem of the Blood Moon Sect, ten thousand zhang across, and it had been living inside the heir of the Lu clan.

Lu Mingyuan stood under it with his sword hanging from his hand, and looked up at it, and his face was the face of a man watching his house burn.

“Take him.”

This time the King did stand.

His domain came down over the arena like a lid, and the crimson moon, which had been swelling, stopped swelling. Its dripping threads hung still in the air. Royal guards in black lacquer poured onto the platform from all four sides — and Lu Mingyuan, with a sound like a sob, reversed his sword and drove its pommel into his own chest.

Blood Escape. Gu Yan had read of it in the Observatory archives. A demonic art of last resort: burn one’s own blood essence all at once, years of life in a single breath, for a burst of speed no one could match.

Lu Mingyuan became a streak of red light, and went straight up.

He did not get far. Han Qiu was already in the air — leaping, not flying, from the Starfall box in a single bound that cracked the stone behind him — and his sword met the red streak a hundred zhang above the platform with a sound like a gong. The streak faltered. And then the commander of the royal guard, a Ninth Seal Heaven Seal veteran Gu Yan had only ever seen standing behind the throne, was beside it, and his open hand closed around it the way a man closes his hand around a moth.

The red light went out. Lu Mingyuan fell, limp, into the commander’s grip, and the two of them came down together onto the platform beside Gu Yan, and the crimson moon above the arena unravelled into nothing and was gone.

In the Lu box, Lu Wenhe had half-risen from his seat. The two royal guards beside him did not touch him. They did not need to. The King’s domain held him exactly where he was, an old man in a scholar’s robe with his hand outstretched toward his grandson, and his face was wet.

“The heir of the Lu clan has practised demonic arts before the throne,” said the King, into the silence, “and harboured a seed of the Blood Moon Sect within his body. His patriarch has attempted murder on my Tournament ground.” The voice was quite level. “From this day, the Lu clan is no longer a ducal house. Its lands and treasury are sealed. Its patriarch and its heir will be held for judgement. Its people will be questioned, and those found innocent will go free.” A pause. “Let the record of this Tournament show it.”

Gu Yan was carried off the platform. He was told later that he had won the semifinal, and that he had insisted on walking, and had managed four steps.

He woke in the physicians’ pavilion with his side stitched and bound, and Mo Ji asleep in a chair beside him, and the sound of the crowd outside like distant surf.

“The other semifinal,” he said. His voice came out a croak.

Mo Ji woke with a snort. “Prince won,” he said. “Lingxi made him work for it. Ninety exchanges. She pinned three of his meridians, Gu Yan, three, nobody’s ever done that to him. Then he broke through her needles and she conceded. She’s bruised but she’s fine. The King has put the final back three days, so the physicians can stitch you both together. She fights for third the same morning.” He paused. “You fight for first.”

Gu Yan lay back and closed his eyes, and turned his attention inward.

His Star Sea was still there. His nine stars were still there, grey under the seal. But the seal itself — the Remnant’s grey lid over the silver coals — had changed. He had pushed his perception through Lu Mingyuan’s lacquer with everything he had, and the strain of it had gone somewhere. He could feel it now, running across the seal from one edge almost to the other.

A crack. Thin as a hair. And through it, very faintly, silver light.

From the doorway of the pavilion, someone cleared his throat. It was Cang Yi, in plain clothes, with a fresh bandage wound around his right wrist where a needle had gone in.

“In three days,” the prince said, “don’t hold anything back.” He looked at Gu Yan steadily. “I’ll know if you do.”
