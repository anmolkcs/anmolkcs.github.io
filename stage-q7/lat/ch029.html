The stair beneath the plum tree went down forty steps, turned once, and ended in a room no larger than his grandfather’s workshop.

It was carved from the living rock of the mountain, smooth-walled and perfectly round. There was no furniture except a single cushion of faded grey silk in the centre of the floor. The walls were bare. But the moment Gu Yan stepped inside, he felt the formation, and when he opened his eyes the other way he saw it: lines carved into the stone so finely that they were invisible to ordinary sight, spiralling inward from the walls toward the cushion in a great slow vortex. A spirit-gathering array, old and patient, drawing qi from the bones of the mountain and concentrating it here, in this one room, until the air was thick as honey.

Above the cushion, the ceiling opened into a narrow shaft, perfectly straight, perhaps a hand’s width across. Far up at the top of it, very small, a single star shone.

“The shaft goes to the summit,” said Han Qiu, from the bottom step. “When I had it cut, I was told that at this hour of this season, the star Tianshu stands directly above it. I don’t know whether that matters. The man who designed this room thought it did.”

Tianshu. The star above Fallen Star Valley. The one that had dimmed for a quarter of an hour on the night his father went in.

Gu Yan did not say anything. He sat down on the cushion.

“I’ll be at the top of the stair,” said Han Qiu. “If your qi turns back on you, I’ll come down and try to stop it. I may not be fast enough. Do you understand?”

“Yes, Elder.”

“Do you want to wait three years?”

“No, Elder.”

Han Qiu looked at him for a moment longer in the faint light from the shaft. Then he turned and climbed the stairs, and the stone door ground shut above, and Gu Yan was alone.

He did not begin at once.

He sat and breathed and let the room’s qi settle into him, slow and heavy, the way his grandfather used to let a new board sit in the workshop for a month before cutting it, so that it learned the air. He ran his qi through all nine meridians, one full circuit, then another, then another, until the great wheel of it turned smoothly and evenly, every channel brimming.

He thought about the gap between rivers and a sea.

Then he closed his eyes and went down into the Lattice.

It hung in the dark as always: nine nodes in a three-by-three frame, joined by silver lines. The top row blazed: grain, starbone, the cut, one complete line of resonance, ringing faintly. The middle row and the bottom row were dark. Nine nodes. Three lit. Six empty.

He had wondered, ever since he had first understood what a Star Sea was, what would happen to the Lattice when he opened one. Whether it would be swept aside, or buried, or simply ignored. He had wondered whether he could open a Star Sea at all, with this strange frame already living in the space where the stars should be.

Now, sitting in Han Qiu’s closed room with the star of the Valley shining far above him, he understood that he had been asking the wrong question. The frame was not in the way. The frame was the joinery. A Star Sea was a space that did not exist until you made it. And he already had a structure, waiting, nine nodes in a perfect lattice, built to hold something.

He opened his dantian.

It was not like breaking a gate. He had broken nine gates and each had been a knot, a resistance, a wall. This was the opposite. This was taking everything he had and letting it go.

All nine meridians emptied at once.

The qi came rushing out of them like water out of nine broken dams, roaring inward from his arms and legs and spine toward the centre of his body, a flood of cold silver and warm green and bright white converging on a single point below his navel. His dantian, which had held it all these months like a cup, filled in a heartbeat and overflowed in the next. Pressure built behind his ribs. It went on building. His whole body went rigid. His jaw locked. He could not breathe.

Now. Han Qiu had said it would feel like this: like the qi had nowhere to go. Like the cup was about to burst.

The cup burst.

His dantian tore open, not outward into his body, but inward, into a space that had not existed a moment before. It was like falling. It was like the floor of a room giving way to reveal that beneath it there had always been a cavern, black and silent and enormous. All his qi poured into it, every drop, and it was not enough. The cavern was too large. The qi spread across its floor like a puddle on a plain, thin and directionless, and began to sink away.

It’s failing.

He felt it. The space he had made was too big for what he had to fill it. The qi was dissolving into the dark, and when it was gone, there would be nothing left to hold the cavern open, and it would collapse, and the collapse would come rushing back up through his empty meridians and tear them to pieces.

He reached for the Lattice.

It came.

The nine nodes descended into the dark of the cavern like a frame lowered into a foundation pit. The silver lines spread across the void and locked into place, each one anchored, each one load-bearing. The empty dark was no longer formless. It had a structure. It had joints. The dissolving qi, finding something to hold on to, rushed toward the lines and ran along them and gathered, thickening, at the nodes.

Nine nodes. Nine points where the qi pooled and spun and grew dense.

Nine stars.

They ignited one by one, from the top left to the bottom right, in the order of the frame. Grain. Starbone. The cut. Then the six empty nodes of the middle and bottom rows, which held no comprehension yet, only space, and which lit anyway, because the frame demanded it.

They did not ignite bright.

He had seen Cang Yi’s stars, white and fierce. He had seen the Star Tower’s falling stars, white and blue and gold. His were none of those colours. They were grey. Dull grey, like iron cooling on an anvil, like ash, like the cold stone his father had pressed into his chest ten years ago. They hung in the void of his new Star Sea in a perfect three-by-three grid, joined by silver lines, nine dim grey lights.

But they were heavy.

He could feel them. Each one sat in his Star Sea like a stone sunk in deep water, utterly still, utterly dense. They did not flare or flicker. They did not orbit. They did not need to. The qi in the cavern flowed along the silver lines between them in a slow, endless circuit, from star to star to star, and each time it passed through one, it came out thicker.

A sea. He had a sea.

He breathed out, and the breath shook.

Qi rose up out of the Star Sea and back into his meridians, not like water poured from a cup, but like a tide coming in. It filled his nine channels and kept coming, and the channels, which had seemed so broad that morning, felt suddenly like streams beside a river mouth. His body grew warm, then hot, then cold, as the starbone drank from the new sea and the new sea refilled itself from the gathering array and the slow cold light at the top of the shaft.

He opened his eyes.

The faint star at the top of the shaft was still shining. He could see the formation lines in the walls more clearly than ever, every carved spiral, every node. He could see the grain in the stone of the floor. He could see the faint lines of his own heartbeat in the veins of his wrists.

Early Star Sea.

He did not know how long he sat there. When at last he stood, his legs were steady. He climbed the forty steps and pushed at the stone door, and it swung open onto a grey dawn and a plum tree, and Han Qiu sitting on the bottom step with his sword across his knees, exactly where he had promised to be.

The Elder looked up at him.

For a long moment, he did not speak. His dark eyes moved over Gu Yan slowly, from head to foot, the way the Principal’s had in the Hall of Ten Thousand Stars, searching for a joint.

“Hm,” said Han Qiu at last. He rose. “You’re alive.”

“Yes, Elder.”

“You opened it.”

“Yes, Elder.”

“How many?”

Gu Yan hesitated. “Nine.”

Han Qiu’s face did not change. But he was silent for a very long time, and when he spoke again his voice was very quiet.

“Don’t say that number to anyone,” he said. “Not until the Mirror has spoken. Come. The Star Mirror Hall opens at the second bell. Every disciple who reaches Star Sea is registered within a day. If you don’t go, people will ask why.”

The Star Mirror stood at the far end of a long hall of white marble, on a dais of nine steps.

It was a disc of polished silver-grey metal three zhang across, set in a frame of carved jade. Its surface was so perfectly smooth that standing before it was like standing at the edge of a still pond at night. According to the Academy’s records, it had been forged by the second principal from a meteorite that fell into the Cang Kingdom two thousand years ago, and in all that time, it had never once been wrong. When a cultivator who had opened a Star Sea stood before it and released their qi, the Mirror showed their stars: their number, their colour, their brightness. The Academy built its rankings on it. Families arranged marriages on it. The Mirror had once shown seven stars for a fifteen-year-old prince, and the kingdom had celebrated for a month.

Word travelled fast in the Academy. By the time Gu Yan climbed the nine steps, there were perhaps two hundred disciples in the hall, and more coming. Mo Ji stood near the front, practically vibrating. Gu Lan was beside him, and Gu Feng behind her, arms crossed, frowning. Su Lingxi stood alone by a pillar, her compass in her hand, the lid open.

He saw, too, Lu Mingyuan, standing among a small group of inner-court disciples near the door, talking quietly. And at the very back of the hall, where the light from the windows did not reach, Han Qiu.

The deacon of the Mirror, an old woman with a face like folded paper, nodded to him. “Release your Star Sea,” she said. “Do not hold back. The Mirror sees what is there.”

Gu Yan faced the silver-grey disc and saw himself in it: a tall, lean youth in a blue robe, slightly pale, with dark eyes and a carpenter’s hands.

He released his qi.

It rose from the Star Sea in a slow, heavy tide. He felt the nine grey stars turn, all at once, in their silver frame, and their weight pressed outward through his body like the pressure of deep water. The air around him thickened. A faint chill spread across the dais.

The Mirror’s surface rippled.

The hall went quiet. Two hundred disciples leaned forward.

The ripples spread across the disc, reached its jade frame, and faded. The surface went still again. In it, Gu Yan saw himself. He saw the hall behind him. He saw the window light and the white marble and the crowd.

He saw no stars.

Not one.

The silence in the hall stretched. Then it broke, not into laughter, but into something worse: a murmur, low and rising, full of pity. Zero. Did you see? Nothing at all. He opened a Star Sea and there’s nothing in it. I’ve heard of it happening. A broken foundation. A hollow sea. Poor fellow. He fought the Third Prince last week. He was so good.

The old deacon looked at the Mirror, then at him. Her paper face softened.

“I’m sorry, child,” she said quietly. “It happens, once in a great while. A Star Sea opens and nothing ignites. Your qi is there, I can feel it. But without stars, there is nothing to amplify it.” She dipped her brush in ink and wrote in the great register beside the dais, and he saw the characters form: Gu Yan. Early Star Sea. Stars: 0. “You may still cultivate. Some zero-star cultivators reach Heaven Seal, eventually.”

“Thank you, Deacon,” Gu Yan said.

He turned and walked down the nine steps.

The murmur followed him like wind through dry grass. He saw Mo Ji’s face, stricken and furious, and Gu Lan’s, close to tears. He saw Gu Feng staring at the Mirror as if he wanted to punch it. Near the door, he saw Lu Mingyuan, who had turned to look at him, and whose handsome, open face held an expression of perfect, courteous sympathy, and beneath it, for the space of one breath, at the very corner of the mouth, something small and satisfied, like a man who has watched a rival’s horse go lame.

At the back of the hall, Han Qiu stood with his arms folded, looking not at Gu Yan, but at the Mirror. He was frowning. It was not a disappointed frown. It was the frown of a craftsman looking at a measuring rule that had just given him a number he knew to be wrong.

And by the pillar, Su Lingxi stood very still, staring at the compass in her hand.

Its needle was spinning.

Not wavering, not shivering. Spinning, around and around and around, faster than he had ever seen it move, so fast that it blurred into a disc of silver, as if it had been asked to point at something and could not find a direction large enough.

She looked up, and her eyes met his across the hall. Her lips moved, without sound.

Not zero.

Gu Yan smiled at her, very slightly, and walked out into the morning.

He was not angry. He was surprised to find that he was not even disappointed. In his Star Sea, nine grey stars turned slowly in their silver frame, heavy as iron and patient as stone, and he could feel every one of them. Let the Mirror say what it liked. His grandfather had spent fifty years making joints that held without a single nail, and no one in Jade Ridge had ever been able to see how.

He was halfway down the white steps outside when he heard, behind him in the hall, a faint high sound, like a fingernail drawn across glass.

He looked back. Through the open doors, the deacon was bent over the Mirror’s frame, frowning, running her thumb along the edge of the silver-grey disc. She shook her head and straightened, and the crowd’s noise rose again and covered whatever she said.

Gu Yan let a thread of lines into his eyes.

On the perfect surface of the Star Mirror, which had never in two thousand years been wrong, a crack ran from the jade frame toward the centre. It was thinner than a hair. No one but him could have seen it.

It ran perfectly straight, the way no crack in nature ever runs, like one line of a grid.
