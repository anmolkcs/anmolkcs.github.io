The deacon who had brought them from Jade Ridge was waiting at the gate on the first morning, and Gu Yan was absurdly glad to see him.

“You passed,” the deacon said, looking over the three Gu cousins, and then at Mo Ji, whom he had evidently heard about. “Good. Now forget that you passed. You were the best of your towns. Here, you are five hundred new pebbles in a very old riverbed.” He turned. “Follow me. I will show you the Academy once. After today, if you get lost, you are on your own.”

They followed, with forty other new disciples who had been assigned to him.

Starfall Academy was not a building. It was a mountain, or the long southern shoulder of one, with the city lapping against its foot. Halls and pavilions climbed its slopes on terraces of grey stone, connected by stairs and covered walkways and little arched bridges over streams that came down from somewhere higher up. The trees were very old: pines with bark like dragon scales, gingkoes whose leaves were just beginning to turn gold. Everywhere there was the sound of water, and of distant shouts from training yards, and once, from a high pavilion, the long thin note of a qin.

“The outer court,” said the deacon, gesturing at the lower terraces. “Where you will live, train, and, if you are unlucky, die of boredom. Lecture halls, training yards, the Merit Hall, the refectories. Above it”—his hand rose to the upper slopes, where the buildings were fewer and larger, half hidden in mist—“the inner court. Inner disciples. You cannot go there. You will be told when you can. Most of you will never be told.”

“How do you become an inner disciple?” asked someone.

“Reach Star Sea,” said the deacon. “Or do something so remarkable that an elder takes you as a disciple first.” He did not slow down. “The first happens to perhaps one in five of you, eventually. The second, to perhaps one in a hundred.”

The Merit Hall was a long, low building near the main gate, its walls lined with wooden boards on which hung hundreds of little tablets of painted bamboo.

“Missions,” said the deacon. “The Academy does not feed you for nothing. Every month you receive a small allowance of low-grade spirit stones. It is enough to keep you alive. It is not enough to make you strong.” He tapped a tablet. “Repair this wall. Escort this caravan. Collect thirty Frostleaf herbs from the north slope. Clear the rats from the Archive cellar, which, I warn you, are not ordinary rats. Each mission pays in merit points. Merit points buy everything worth having. Pills. Techniques from the Scripture Pavilion. Time in the Star Tower. Spirit stones. A better room.” He looked at Mo Ji. “Food.”

Mo Ji looked stricken. “Food costs merit?”

“Good food costs merit.”

“That’s inhumane.”

“That is cultivation,” said the deacon, and moved on.

He took them past the Scripture Pavilion, a tall, dark building of eight sides guarded by two stone lions whose eyes followed people; past the alchemy halls, where the air smelled of burnt honey and sulphur; past training yards where older disciples were sparring in pairs, their weapons flashing with qi, so fast that Gu Yan’s eye could barely follow. One pair, a man with twin sabres and a woman with a chain-whip, were fighting on top of a row of wooden posts no wider than a hand, leaping from one to the next as if the ground were lava. Neither of them seemed to notice the new disciples staring.

Then the path turned a corner of the hillside, and they came out into the forest of stones.

Gu Yan stopped walking.

It was a wide, gently sloping meadow of short grass, perhaps half a li across, and it was full of steles. Hundreds of them. Thousands. They stood at no regular interval, some alone, some in clusters, like a forest that had grown from a scattering of seeds. Some were taller than the Academy’s halls; some barely reached a man’s knee. Some were polished black stone with characters cut so deep you could put your fist in them. Some were rough boulders with a single line scratched on them. Some were cracked, or broken, or half sunk into the earth. And from all of them, faintly, came a pressure. Not like the stair. Gentler. Like walking into a room full of people who have all stopped talking at once to look at you.

“The Dao Stele Forest,” said the deacon quietly. “Every stele here was carved by a master. Some of them were Academy elders. Some came from elsewhere. Ruins, battlefields, the tombs of sects that no longer exist. Each one carries a comprehension: a piece of the carver’s understanding of the Dao, set in stone. Sit before the right stele with the right mind, and you may understand in an afternoon what took its carver a lifetime.” He paused. “Or you may sit before it for thirty years and understand nothing. Or you may understand too much and lose your mind. The Forest is open to all disciples. It is not supervised. Please bear that in mind.”

Gu Yan, without quite meaning to, had let the sight open.

He closed it again at once, his eyes stinging. It was like looking into the sun. Every stele was a knot of lines, dense and brilliant and utterly different from every other, a thousand voices all speaking at once in a thousand languages. His head ached sharply behind the eyes.

Later, he thought. Slowly. One at a time.

He felt someone watching him and turned. Gu Feng was looking at him with a frown. “You’ve gone pale,” his cousin said.

“Just the pressure.”

“Hm.” Gu Feng looked out over the stones. “I could stay here forever,” he said, with a kind of hunger Gu Yan had not heard in his voice before, and then seemed embarrassed, and walked on.

And beyond the Forest, at the top of the slope, stood the Tower.

Up close, the Nine-Story Star Tower was both less and more than it had seemed from the lantern boat. Less, because it was only stone; plain, dark grey stone, without carving or colour, rising in nine tiers that each narrowed slightly from the one below. More, because it was so tall that standing at its foot and looking up made Gu Yan dizzy. The nine silver bands that had glowed at night were not decoration. They were the outer edges of nine great formations, one per floor, each laid into the stone in a ring.

“The Star Tower,” said the deacon. “Each floor is a trial ground. Different gravity. Different qi. Different dangers. The first floor is open to any disciple who pays its merit cost. Each floor above requires that you have cleared the one below. To my knowledge,” he added, “no one now living has cleared the ninth.”

“What’s on the ninth?” asked Gu Lan.

The deacon looked up at the tower’s top, where it vanished into the pale morning sky.

“Nobody knows,” he said. “That is rather the point.”

The outer court dormitories were long buildings of grey brick on the lowest terrace, each divided into rooms for two. Gu Yan was assigned to the fourteenth building, room nine. Mo Ji, by some stroke of fortune or because he had gone to the dormitory deacon and stood very close to him until the deacon found a way, was assigned to the same room.

It was small. Two narrow beds, two cabinets, a single window looking out over the rooftops of the Lantern Ward toward the river. A meditation mat. A shelf. The plaster on one wall was cracked, and through the crack came a draught that smelled of pine.

Gu Yan looked at the cabinet assigned to him. Its door hung crooked. One of its hinges had been nailed back on by someone who did not understand hinges, and the frame had been forced so many times that the mortise had split.

“Don’t,” said Mo Ji, dropping his bundle on the bed. “Don’t you dare start fixing furniture on the first day. Do you know what’s going to happen? You’ll fix your cabinet, and then the boy in room eight will see, and then he’ll ask you to fix his, and then the whole fourteenth building will be lined up outside our door with their broken stools, and I’ll never get any sleep.”

“I’ll do it quietly.”

“You don’t know how to do anything quietly. You sit still loudly.”

Gu Yan looked at him. “Gu Lan told you that.”

“Your cousin is a fountain of useful information.” Mo Ji flopped back on his bed, which creaked alarmingly. “Also she told me that you once spent six days carving a single wooden bird because the first five times the wings didn’t sit right.”

“They didn’t sit right.”

“Six days, Gu Yan.”

He fixed the cabinet that evening, quietly, with a borrowed chisel and a sliver of hardwood from the woodpile behind the refectory. It took less than an hour. The boy from room eight saw. By the end of the week, Gu Yan had fixed four stools, two cabinets, a window-shutter, and a bed that had been sagging in the middle for so long that its occupant had developed a permanent crick in his neck.

Mo Ji complained bitterly about every one of them. He also made a point of telling everyone who came to the door that the work was free, which nobody had asked him to do, and once, when an older disciple from building twelve tried to bring a broken sword-rack and pay with an insult instead of a thank-you, Mo Ji simply stood up from his bed and filled the doorway until the disciple went away.

The first sword lesson was on the fourth day.

Of the five hundred new disciples, perhaps a hundred and fifty used swords. They gathered in a bare yard of beaten earth on the third terrace, ringed with old plum trees, and waited, and nobody came.

They waited for the length of an incense stick. Then for another. People began to murmur. Some sat down. A tall boy from one of the noble families said loudly that he had been told the Academy’s sword instructor was Elder Han Qiu, and that Elder Han Qiu was a Heaven Seal cultivator of the 6th Seal, the greatest sword master in the kingdom after the principal, and that if this was how he taught, then he, the tall boy, had been misinformed.

Gu Yan had not sat down. He had been standing in his place in the third row with his father’s sword at his side for the entire wait. It had not occurred to him to do anything else. In the workshop, when Grandpa said wait, one waited.

He was therefore one of the few disciples still on their feet when he noticed that one of the plum trees at the edge of the yard had a man standing under it who had not been there a moment ago.

Or had been there the whole time.

He was perhaps fifty. Lean, grey at the temples, dressed in a plain dark robe without insignia. His face was long and severe, and very still, and he carried a sword in a black scabbard across his back, and as Gu Yan looked at him, he felt something he had never felt from a person before: an edge. A thin, cold sense of sharpness in the air, as if the man were not standing in the yard so much as cutting a man-shaped hole in it.

“Those of you who are sitting,” said Elder Han Qiu, without raising his voice, “may leave. Come back next year.”

There was a frozen silence. Then the sitters scrambled to their feet in a rush of cloth, the tall noble boy among them, red to the ears.

Han Qiu walked into the yard. He did not walk like a man; he walked like a blade being drawn, without any unnecessary movement whatever.

“I am not going to teach you techniques today,” he said. “You all have techniques. Your families bought them, or your teachers gave them, or you found them in a book. Most of them are bad. Some of them are good. It does not matter, because none of you can use them.” He began to walk slowly along the first row. “Today I am going to look at how you hold your swords. Draw.”

A hundred and fifty swords came out of their scabbards.

Han Qiu walked the rows. He said very little. To one disciple: “Too tight.” To another: “You are holding a stick. Hold a sword.” To a third, the tall noble boy, he said nothing at all, but simply reached out, flicked the flat of the boy’s blade with one fingernail, and the sword leapt out of the boy’s hand and stuck quivering in the earth two zhang away. The boy stared at his empty hand.

“Again,” said Han Qiu, and walked on.

He came to the third row. He came to Gu Yan.

Gu Yan held his father’s iron sword in the stance the Flowing Cloud manual taught, the stance he had practised ten thousand times in the clan yard. His grip was steady. He met the elder’s eyes.

Han Qiu looked at the sword. Then at his hand. Then at his feet, and his shoulders, and back to his hand. For a long time.

The cold edge in the air pressed against Gu Yan’s skin like the flat of a knife.

“Where did you learn to hold a blade?” said Han Qiu.

“Jade Ridge, Elder. In my clan’s yard. And—” He hesitated. “In my grandfather’s workshop.”

“Your grandfather is a swordsman?”

“A carpenter.”

Something moved in the long, severe face. It was too slight to be called a smile. It was the expression of a man who has just found the answer to a riddle that has been bothering him.

“Yes,” said Elder Han Qiu. “I thought so.” He reached out and, with one finger, touched the back of Gu Yan’s hand where it wrapped the hilt: the way his thumb lay along the grip, the slight forward cant of his wrist, the way his whole arm was arranged behind the blade, braced and precise, to drive force along a single line into a single point.

“You hold it like a chisel,” he said.

The yard was very quiet. Somewhere behind Gu Yan, someone smothered a laugh.

Han Qiu did not look at the laugher. He kept looking at Gu Yan, with those flat, steady eyes that seemed to see through skin and into the grain of the bone.

“That,” he said, “is not necessarily a criticism.” He took his finger away. “Stay after the lesson.”
