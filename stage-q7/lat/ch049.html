He mended the seal that night on the dormitory roof, with the stars coming out one by one over Cangdu and his whole body shaking.

It was like rejoining a shattered tabletop without glue. The Remnant had shown him the pattern once, in the inner zone of the Valley, with a contemptuous patience that had made it stick: the grey lid was not a wall but a weave, laid line over line across the mouth of his Star Sea, and every line had to sit in its place or none of them would hold. Gu Yan found the broken ends one by one and laid them back together. It took him until the fourth watch. When it was done, he could feel a scar running across the weave where the crack had been, a seam that would always be a little weaker than the rest — but the silver was hidden again, and the Lattice in his mind lay quiet and dim behind it, and when he looked up at the sky, nothing in it seemed to be looking back.

He did not know what Qin Shang had meant. He was not sure he wanted to.

Mo Ji was waiting at the foot of the ladder, as he had been the night before the breakthrough, two months ago. He held out a bowl of cold noodles without a word. Gu Yan ate them sitting on the steps.

“Nine,” Mo Ji said finally.

“Nine.”

“In a square.”

“Yes.”

Mo Ji was quiet for a long time. Then he said, “I want the tombstone changed,” and Gu Yan laughed so hard that his stitches pulled.

The Star Mirror was brought to the arena at noon the next day.

It had never left Starfall Academy before in its history. Eight royal guards carried it on poles through the streets of Cangdu, a disk of polished bronze two zhang across set in a frame of black stone carved with formations, and crowds lined the way to watch it pass. In the Arena of Ten Thousand Steps it was set up on the central platform, facing the royal pavilion, and the Principal himself checked every line of its frame.

“You do not have to do this,” Han Qiu said quietly, in the tunnel.

“Everyone saw,” said Gu Yan. “If I refuse, they’ll wonder what I’m afraid of.”

“They will wonder anyway.” But the elder did not argue further.

Gu Yan walked out onto the platform and stood before the Mirror. Ten thousand faces watched him. He kept the seal closed. He kept it closed as tightly as he had ever kept it, every mended line in its place, and he looked into the bronze.

For a long moment, nothing.

The Mirror showed what it had shown the day he first ignited: his own face, pale, a little thinner than he remembered, and above it, darkness. No stars. Somewhere on the tiers, someone began to laugh, uncertainly —

Then the bronze grew warm.

Gu Yan felt it reach for him. The Mirror had been made, centuries ago, by some master of the kingdom to find stars in a cultivator’s sea and count them. It had been made to look through things. And now it was looking, and finding nothing, and looking harder — he could feel its formations straining against the Remnant’s weave like fingers prying at a shutter. Its frame hummed. Then it groaned.

Nine points of silver flickered in the bronze, faint as stars through cloud, in a perfect square.

And the Mirror cracked.

The crack ran from rim to rim with a sound like river ice breaking in spring. A second followed it, then a third. The bronze clouded. The formation-lines in the black stone frame flared white, then failed, one after another, like lamps going out down a long corridor — and with a long, grinding sigh, the Star Mirror of Starfall Academy came apart in a slow rain of bronze shards on the white jade at Gu Yan’s feet.

Silence.

Then the whole arena was on its feet, and the noise was such that Gu Yan could not hear himself think.

“Mirrors,” said Han Qiu, beside him, into the uproar, “measure what they were built to measure.” He looked at the wreckage for a moment, and then at Gu Yan, and his mouth did something it rarely did. “That one was not built for you.”

The presentation took place at sunset, in the royal pavilion.

The King gave the prizes himself, as was the custom: a spirit sword of Earth grade for the champion, a set of formation plates for second, a cauldron of medicinal herbs for third. He spoke briefly and well. He said the Tournament had been the strangest in eight hundred years and had shown the kingdom both its rot and its strength. He did not mention the Lu clan by name. He did not need to.

Then he stepped aside, and the envoy of the Taixu Holy Land stood up.

Qin Shang looked at the three of them for some time: Cang Yi in the blue of the royal house, his collarbone bandaged; Su Lingxi in Observatory grey, perfectly composed; Gu Yan between them, in a borrowed robe because his own was too bloodstained to wear.

“I said I would decide,” the envoy said. “I have decided. If they wish it, all three will come with me to the Holy Land. My ship leaves Cangdu in twenty days.” He paused. “Do you wish it?”

“Yes,” said Cang Yi at once.

“Yes,” said Su Lingxi.

“Yes,” said Gu Yan.

The envoy nodded as if they had told him the time of day. “Good,” he said. And that was all.

Afterwards, while the court milled and the bells of Cangdu rang out across the evening, Qin Shang passed close to Gu Yan on his way down the pavilion steps. He did not stop. He did not even turn his head. But his voice came quietly at Gu Yan’s ear, as it had in the arena.

I will not ask what you carry. I have read the Holy Land’s oldest records, and I know enough to know I do not want to be the one who asks. Whatever made that seal is older than Taixu. Keep it mended, boy. Nine silver stars in a square will draw eyes even among thirty thousand.

Then he was gone into the crowd, a mild grey man in a linen robe, and Gu Yan stood on the steps with the bells ringing and his heart beating very hard.

He spent seven of the twenty days in Han Qiu’s closed room.

It was the same room in which he had first ignited his Star Sea, a stone chamber without windows, lined with formation plates and smelling faintly of incense and old sweat. He sat in its centre with the iron sword across his knees and went down into himself.

The Tournament had given him more than a title. He could feel it everywhere in his body. Ye Han’s frost, Lu Mingyuan’s hunger, Cang Yi’s mountain — every one of them had pressed on his Star Sea from outside, and it had grown under the pressure the way a tree grows thick against the wind. His nine stars were larger than they had been a month ago. The space between them was deeper. And the sixth node, newly lit, hummed beside the fourth and fifth, and the whole middle row of the Lattice resonated softly, like a held chord.

On the third day he stood up into the last ceiling of the Star Sea realm.

It was the gentlest breakthrough yet. There was a moment of pressure and then the sense of a great stillness, as though his Star Sea had reached the full size it would ever reach in this realm and settled into it, and its nine stars, silver under the grey, burned steadily in their square. Peak Star Sea. Beyond it, he could feel the next realm — Heaven Seal — not as a ceiling but as a blank page.

He began to write on it.

He did not mean to engrave a seal. He had thought he was months from it, perhaps years. But on the fourth day, turning the iron sword over in his hands, he found himself thinking of the pillar on the cliff and the groove he had cut into unbroken granite, and of the thread between his blade and Cang Yi’s spear — and his attention went, almost of its own accord, to the third of his nine stars. The one that had first lit with sword intent in a collapsed observatory in Fallen Star Valley.

He began to carve.

It was not like carving wood. It was like carving meaning. He took the intent of every cut he had ever made — the leaf cut along its own vein in Han Qiu’s courtyard, the four lines in Lu Kang’s chest, the seven in Lu Mingyuan’s, the thread in the final — and pressed it into the star, stroke by stroke, the way his grandfather pressed a chisel along a marked line. Each stroke was a decision. Each stroke had to sit exactly where the last had left room for it. It was the hardest work he had ever done, and the slowest. By the end of the seventh day he had laid down a little more than half of what he could feel the whole would be.

A half-formed seal: a single character, the strokes of its left side complete and blazing, its right side only faintly sketched, as if waiting.

Sword.

When he came out, Han Qiu was sitting by the door, as Gu Yan had once sat by Mo Ji’s. The elder looked at him for a long moment, his gaze resting somewhere around Gu Yan’s dantian.

“Earth grade,” he said at last. “Half of one.” He rose. “Most disciples’ first seal is Mortal, and whole, and they are proud of it for the rest of their lives.” He turned away, and Gu Yan thought, for an instant, that his voice was not quite steady. “Finish it in the Holy Land. And write to me when you do.”

The cloud-hawk carriage was Cang Yi’s idea. “You’re going home,” the prince said flatly, two days before Gu Yan could have asked him for anything. “Ten days each way by road is too long. The royal stables have a carriage that will do it in two. Take it, and bring it back with all its feathers.” He paused. “And give your grandfather my respects. Any man who taught you to hold a sword like a chisel deserves them.”

So Gu Yan went home to Jade Ridge, with Gu Lan beside him chattering the whole way and Gu Feng sitting opposite, quiet, watching the land fall away below.

The hawk was the size of a house, and flew higher than any bird had a right to, and from its back the Cang Kingdom unrolled beneath them like a map on a scholar’s table: rivers silver in the sun, the grey smudges of towns, the dark green fur of forests. They passed over the Black Pine Forest at noon on the second day. Gu Yan looked down at it and thought of an Iron-Back Boar, and a red bell on a hilltop, and could not believe how small it all was.

Jade Ridge Town turned out in the streets to meet them. Gu Hong stood at the clan gate with the elders behind him, and bowed to Gu Yan — to Gu Yan, the branch-family carpenter’s grandson — and said, with real feeling, “The Gu clan thanks you,” and then, with less formality and more honesty, “and the Gu clan is a little afraid of you, which I suppose it will have to get used to.”

But Gu Yan did not stay long at the gate. He went down the lane to the edge of the compound, to the workshop.

The roof had been mended. He could see the new tiles. The yard smelled of cedar shavings, as it always had. And on the bench under the eaves, in the late sun, with a half-planed board across his knees and a cup of warm water at his elbow, sat Gu Shouyi.

He looked older. He looked thinner. But when he coughed, it was a short dry cough, not the long tearing one Gu Yan had heard in the brushstrokes of the letter, and his hands on the plane were steady.

“I told you not to come back,” the old man said.

“You told me not to come back before I was sent for.” Gu Yan sat down on the bench beside him. “The Holy Land sent for me.”

Grandpa Shouyi was quiet for a long time. He set down the plane. Then he put one heavy, calloused hand on the back of Gu Yan’s neck, the way Gu Yan had held Mo Ji the night of his breakthrough, and left it there.

“The pill was too expensive,” he said gruffly. “And that girl’s star-water tastes of nothing. Tell her it works.”

They sat in the yard until the sun went down. Gu Yan told him about the Valley, about the Remnant, about his father’s carved marks on the stone and the broken jade tablet. He survived. He found a crack in the Lattice. He went up.

His grandfather listened to all of it without a word. When it was done, he picked up the plane again and ran it once along the board, slow and even, and a long curl of cedar fell away.

“Then go up after him,” he said. “And when you find him, drag him back down here by the ear, so I can tell him what I think of him.”

“I will.”

The old man glanced at him sideways. In the dusk his eyes were very bright. “The letter. Have you opened it?”

“Not yet.” Gu Yan looked up, past the eaves, at the darkening sky with its first few stars. “You said to wait until I could see the sky’s edge. I haven’t seen it.”

His grandfather followed his gaze, up into the deepening blue — the ceiling he had muttered about on a roof, a lifetime ago.

“You will,” said Gu Shouyi.
