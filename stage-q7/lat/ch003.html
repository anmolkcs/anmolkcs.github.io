“Iron-Back Boar,” said Gu Lan, and slapped a page of the clan’s bestiary down on the workbench between the chisels.

It was a woodblock print, slightly smudged: a squat, heavy-shouldered beast with tusks curving up past its snout and a ridge of overlapping plates running from its skull to its tail like the roof tiles of a temple. Underneath, in small characters, the bestiary said: Grade-1 spirit beast. Found in pine forests of the eastern prefectures. Temperament: foul. Hide: proof against ordinary blades. Marrow of the leg bones, decocted with blood-root and red ginseng, aids cultivators of the 6th and 7th Forging.

“Gu Xiu’s father saw tracks below the Split Rock, at the edge of the Black Pine Forest,” she said. “Fresh ones. A big male, by the size. One boar has four leg bones. Four leg bones is enough marrow for three people to push a level each, if we’re careful with the decoction. That’s me, you, and Gu Xiu. Gu Bao’s coming for the fun of it, and because he’s eighth Forging and can carry it home.”

“And because the clan rule says no one under 9th Forging goes into the Black Pine without a 9th Forging escort,” said Gu Yan, “so you’ll go only as far as the outskirts, where the rule doesn’t quite reach.”

“The outskirts,” Gu Lan agreed, without a trace of embarrassment, “is exactly where the boar is.”

His grandfather, sanding a drawer front at the other bench, did not look up. “Take the hatchet,” he said. “Not the good one.”

They went at first light, four of them in hunting leathers, with spears and bows and Gu Lan’s sword and, at Gu Yan’s belt, the second-best hatchet from the workshop: a short-hafted thing with a heavy head that he had sharpened the night before until it would shave the hair off his forearm.

Gu Xiu, a lanky, quiet boy of sixteen who was the best archer among the clan youths, led. Gu Bao, seventeen and built like a rain barrel, brought up the rear with a boar spear over his shoulder and a pack of rope and salt, cheerfully telling anyone who would listen about the last time he had hunted, which had been a deer, which had escaped.

The Black Pine Forest began two li beyond the last terraced field of Jade Ridge. It did not so much begin as rise: the land tilted up into the mountains, the soil grew thin and rocky, and the pines came down to meet it, dense and dark and very old. Their bark was nearly black, deeply furrowed, and wept a resin that smelled sharp enough to sting the nose. The canopy closed overhead within a hundred paces, and the morning light turned green and dim. Their feet went quiet on a floor of needles that had been falling for ten thousand years.

Somewhere far inside, the bestiary said, there were Grade-3 and Grade-4 beasts, things that could kill a Star Sea cultivator. Nobody from Jade Ridge went far inside.

They found the tracks below the Split Rock, a crag of grey granite cracked down the middle as if by an axe the size of a house. The prints were deep, cloven, as broad as Gu Yan’s spread hand. Beside them the bark of a pine had been gouged at knee height, long pale wounds that were still bleeding resin.

“Sharpening its tusks,” Gu Xiu murmured. “Today. This morning.”

Gu Bao had stopped talking.

They followed the trail upslope for half an hour, through a gully thick with fern, and came out at the edge of a clearing where a pine had fallen years ago and torn a hole in the canopy. Sunlight fell into it in a thick gold shaft. In the middle of the light, rooting in the soft earth around the fallen trunk, was the boar.

The woodcut had not done it justice.

It was the size of an ox. Its shoulders stood higher than Gu Yan’s chest, humped with muscle, and the plates along its back were not like roof tiles at all but like the iron scales of a war-horse’s barding, dull grey, each one overlapping the next, each one thick as a thumb. Its tusks were yellow and as long as daggers. It was snorting and grunting in a contented, rumbling way, turning over earth with its snout as easily as a man turns a page, and every so often it lifted its head and the small black eyes surveyed the trees around the clearing with an intelligence that made Gu Yan’s neck prickle.

“Plan,” breathed Gu Lan.

They had made one on the way. Gu Xiu would open from the trees, aiming for the eye; the eye and the soft underside were the only places an arrow would bite. When it charged, Gu Bao would set the boar spear against the ground and let it run onto the point, angled under the plates at the chest. Gu Lan and Gu Yan would come in from the flanks to cut the legs.

It was a good plan. Gu Xiu drew, held, breathed, and loosed.

The boar turned its head at the sound of the string.

The arrow, which would have gone into its eye, struck the bony ridge above it and skipped away into the ferns. The boar screamed. It was not a pig’s squeal. It was a sound like iron dragged across stone, so loud the pines seemed to shiver, and before the echoes had died it was moving.

It did not go for the archer. It went for Gu Bao, who was the biggest, as if it had picked him out from the start.

Gu Bao did what he was supposed to do. He planted the spear butt against a root and braced and lowered the point. The boar came on in a straight line, head down, and at the last moment, as the steel was about to take it in the chest, it dropped its head lower still and caught the spear shaft with one tusk and flicked it aside, as a man might brush away a reed.

Then it hit him.

Gu Yan heard the impact. A solid, flat sound, and a crack under it like a green stick breaking. Gu Bao went up and backward through the air, a full two zhang, and struck a pine trunk and slid down it and did not get up.

“Bao!” Gu Lan was already moving, sword out, coming in at the boar’s flank as it skidded and turned. Her blade struck its side. It rang off the plates with a sound like a temple bell and left a bright scratch and nothing more. The boar swung its head and she threw herself flat, and a tusk passed over her back close enough to slice through the leather of her coat.

Gu Xiu was shooting as fast as he could draw: one arrow broke on the plates, another stuck shallowly in a foreleg, a third missed.

Gu Yan stood still.

It cost him to do it. Every instinct he had was screaming to run in, to do something. But he had seen that charge, and he knew with complete certainty that running in would get him killed. So he stood at the edge of the clearing with the hatchet in his hand and he looked at the boar the way he looked at timber.

The lines came. They came harder than they ever had, as if they had been waiting for him to need them. The silver threads rose out of the boar’s body, and they were not like the lines in wood. They moved. They ran along its limbs in long braided bundles, tightening and loosening with each step, flaring bright where the great muscles of the shoulders gathered for a lunge, dimming where they rested. He saw how the whole weight of the beast was carried, how its power went down through the shoulders into the forelegs and into the ground. He saw the plates as a dead weight lying on top of that living structure, protecting it, but not part of it.

And he saw where they did not reach.

On the inside of each foreleg, just above the knee joint, where the leg bent, the plates stopped. Under the coarse grey hair there, every line in the leg came together into a single bright knot, a pulley over which the whole force of the charge was drawn. The tendon. If that line were cut, the leg would not carry the weight.

The pain started behind his eyes, a hot needle. Something warm trickled from his nostril.

The boar had turned toward Gu Lan again. She had rolled to her feet and was backing toward the trees, sword up, her face white. It lowered its head.

“Lan,” Gu Yan said, his own voice sounding strange and distant to him, “go left. Make it turn right. Xiu, the right eye, when it turns.”

He did not wait to see whether they heard. He ran.

He did not run straight at the boar. He ran at an angle, toward its left side, and as Gu Lan broke left the boar swung to follow her and its head came round and Gu Xiu’s bowstring thrummed. The arrow did not reach the eye; it took the beast in the cheek, a shallow cut, but it made the great head jerk aside, and for a single breath the left foreleg was planted with all the beast’s weight on it, and the bright knot of lines above the joint was exposed.

Gu Yan dropped to one knee in the churned earth beside the boar’s flank, close enough to smell the rank musk of it, and swung the hatchet up and inward at that point with every jin of strength in his body.

He felt the edge go through hair, through hide, into something that resisted like wet rope and then parted.

The boar’s leg folded. All that weight, all that momentum, came down on a limb that was no longer there to hold it, and the beast pitched forward onto its chest and tusks and ploughed a furrow in the earth, screaming. It thrashed. A hind hoof caught Gu Yan in the side and threw him three paces, and the world went white, and he rolled and came up gasping, his ribs on fire, still holding the hatchet.

The boar was trying to rise. It got its other three legs under it and lurched up, and the cut foreleg dangled and would not take weight, and it went down again on that side. Its head swung around, searching for him.

Gu Lan came in from its blind side and drove her sword, two-handed, into its right eye, all the way to the hilt.

The boar shuddered once along its whole length. Then it was still.

Gu Bao was alive. Two ribs were broken and his left arm was badly wrenched, and when Gu Xiu felt the bones of it he made a sound through his teeth that made Gu Yan wince, but the big youth opened his eyes after a while and said, “Did it escape?” in a tone of deep resignation, and when they told him it had not he laughed and then stopped laughing very quickly because of the ribs.

They made a litter of spear shafts and rope. It took all of them to drag the boar’s carcass out of the forest, and by the time they reached the terraced fields it was afternoon, and they had become something they had not been when they set out. Gu Yan could not have named it. A thing that happens between people who have stood in the same clearing.

“You told us where to go,” said Gu Xiu quietly, as they walked. “How did you know?”

“I watched how it moved,” Gu Yan said. It was true. “It turned toward whatever ran. And the leg… I’ve been cutting joints since I was eight. Everything has a place where it bends.”

Gu Xiu considered this with the seriousness of a person who was not fully convinced and was too polite to say so. Then he nodded. “Your nose is bleeding,” he said.

The old man made the decoction himself. He split the leg bones in the workshop yard with a maul, scooped out the marrow, which was dense and yellowish-pink and smelled of iron, and simmered it for six hours with blood-root and slices of red ginseng and a few other things from a drawer Gu Yan had never been allowed to open. He gave a third of it to Gu Lan and a third to Gu Xiu and kept back the last third, and when the moon was up he set the bowl in front of his grandson.

“Drink it hot,” he said. “Then sit. Don’t fight it.”

It tasted like rust and earth and something faintly sweet. It went down his throat like molten lead.

For a moment there was nothing. Then the heat in his bones, the banked coal he had carried for a year and a half, flared.

It was as if someone had opened the damper on a furnace. The heat roared up through his marrow, every bone in his body at once, his thighs and spine and ribs and the small bones of his hands, until he could have sworn he felt them glowing. He clenched his teeth. Sweat sprang out on his forehead and ran into his eyes. The heat built and built, pressing outward against the inside of the bones, against walls that had been tempered and tempered again until they were strong enough to hold it, and then, at the peak of it, when he thought he could not sit still for one more breath, it did not break out.

It went in.

It flowed inward from the marrow, deeper, into the soft places at the centre of him. He felt his heart grip it and beat, once, so hard the sound of it filled his skull. He felt his lungs fill with it, and his breath came out of him hot as a forge’s breath. His liver, his kidneys, his stomach: each one took the heat and clenched around it and grew, he could not have said how, denser, as if each organ were being packed tighter with something solid. It hurt. It hurt in a way that was close to pleasure, like stretching a muscle that has been cramped all day.

The 7th Forging. The organs.

When it ebbed, he was lying on his back on the floor of the workshop, soaked in sweat, the cool air of the night on his face. His heartbeat was slow and heavy, each beat a long deep drum. He could hear, he realised, the mice in the walls. He could hear the stream at the bottom of the valley.

His grandfather was sitting on the step with his back to him, looking at the boar’s leg bones, which lay in a heap beside the maul. He had picked up the left foreleg and was holding it to the moonlight, turning it to look at the place above the knee.

“Who made this cut?” Gu Shouyi said, without turning around.

Gu Yan lay very still.

“Me,” he said.

The old man looked at the bone for a long moment. Then he set it down gently on the heap, as if it were something fragile.

“It’s a good cut,” he said. “Too good.” And he got up, and went inside, and Gu Yan heard him sitting in the dark by the stove for a long time without lighting a lamp.
