The guards did not take him to the palace. They took him to a boathouse.

It stood at the far end of the Academy’s lake, a low wooden building with a curved roof and a jetty running out over the water, and it was so plain that Gu Yan would have walked past it a hundred times without a second look. Inside, there was a single room, a brazier, a table with a teapot, and a window that opened onto mist and the grey morning surface of the lake.

The Third Prince was making tea.

He did it badly. He poured the water too hot and too fast, and the leaves rose and swirled in a frothy mass. Then he looked at the result with a slight frown, as though it were a technique that had not worked as described in the manual.

“Sit,” Cang Yi said. “I’m told I’m supposed to have a servant do this, but every servant in the palace reports to someone, and I wanted this conversation to belong to the two of us.” He poured two cups. “It will taste terrible. I apologise in advance.”

Gu Yan sat. He tasted the tea. It was terrible.

“How is your cousin?” the Prince asked.

“Asleep. The infirmary says the needle drew off some of her qi but didn’t damage her meridians. She’ll be well in a week.”

“Good.” Cang Yi seemed to mean it. He sat across from Gu Yan and put his own cup down untouched. The seven stars that had orbited him in the Star Tower were invisible now, drawn back into his Star Sea, but Gu Yan could feel them all the same, a pressure at the edge of his lines like heat from a closed furnace. “The guard captain who found you on the granary roof sent her report to the magistrate. The magistrate sent it to my father’s ministers. One of my father’s ministers sent it to me, because I asked him a year ago to send me anything involving drained bodies.” He paused. “Your cousin is the fourth person in Cangdu this year to be attacked that way. The first three are dead. So is a disciple of this Academy, as you know, since you were seen walking along a culvert behind the outer-court dormitories at night with blood on your sleeve.”

Gu Yan did not react. Han Qiu’s warning came back to him: the Discipline Hall is not the only one who has eyes in that lane.

“Your Highness has many eyes.”

“My father has many eyes. I borrow a few.” Cang Yi leaned back. “I’m going to tell you some things that the court does not say aloud. I’m telling you because I watched you fight, and I watched you on the third floor, and I think you are someone who sees more than he says. If I’m wrong, you can drink your tea and go home and forget this boathouse exists.”

“And if you’re right?”

“Then I’ll ask you for something. And you can still say no.”

“You’ve seen the Principal’s lecture,” Cang Yi said. “So you know how small we are.”

He dipped a finger in his tea and drew on the table: a rough circle, then five dots around its edge and one in the centre.

“The Cang Kingdom. My family holds the centre: the capital, the river, the royal army, the treasury. My father is a Mandate cultivator of the middle stage. In a fight, there is no one in this kingdom who could stand against him for longer than it takes to drink a cup of tea.” He tapped the central dot. “But a king cannot fight everyone at once, and a king cannot be everywhere. The kingdom was founded by my ancestor and five companions. Each was given a duchy. Their descendants are the five ducal clans.”

He touched the dots around the edge, one by one.

“The Xiahou in the north, horse breeders and border guards. The Pei in the south, rice and river trade. The Ning in the east, by the coast, salt and shipping; your Jade Ridge is in their shadow. The Zhao in the south-west, scholars and officials; half the ministers at court are Zhao by blood or marriage.” His finger came to rest on the last dot, on the western edge of the circle. “And the Lu.”

“Mines,” Gu Yan said. “And forges.”

“Mines and forges. The western duchy runs right up to the Blood Moon Mountains. The richest iron and spirit-stone veins in the kingdom lie in its foothills. Every sword in the royal army was forged from Lu iron. Every spirit stone that pays the Academy’s elders passed through Lu hands first. The treasury depends on them. The army depends on them.” The Prince looked at his own drawing. “If my father moved against the Lu clan without proof, the other four dukes would ask which of them would be next, and the kingdom would split like a cracked bowl.”

“And the Taixu Holy Land would notice,” Gu Yan said quietly.

Cang Yi glanced up, and something sharpened in his face. “Yes. The Holy Land does not rule us. But every few centuries, a kingdom in the Eastern Wastes tears itself apart in civil war, and every time, the Holy Land sends an envoy to see whether the kingdom is still worth keeping. Sometimes they decide it isn’t. Then a neighbour is permitted to take its land, and its royal house is remembered only in histories.” He said this calmly, as a fact he had grown up with. “My family has ruled here for two thousand years by not being worth that envoy’s trouble.”

Gu Yan looked at the five dots. “You think one of the dukes is protecting the Blood Moon Sect.”

“I think the Blood Moon Sect has been harvesting blood in this kingdom for thirty years, and growing bolder, and that no sect hiding in mountains could do that without someone feeding it, warning it, hiding it.” Cang Yi’s voice stayed even. “The beast tides along the southern border this spring. The drained bodies. A bell-ringer killed by an old man at Jade Ridge.” He watched Gu Yan’s face. “Yes, I know about that too. That report came to me as well.”

“Is it the Lu?”

“I don’t know.” It was the most honest thing Gu Yan had heard anyone at court say. “The Lu clan’s lands run beside the Blood Moon Mountains. Their forge made the dagger on your granary roof. Their heir is the most capable young man in the kingdom after myself, and I am not certain about the after.” A faint, wry smile. “But the Zhao have wanted a war in the west for twenty years, because it would ruin the Lu and make the Zhao indispensable. The Xiahou breed the horses the Blood Moon Sect would need to cross the plains. Any of them could have put that dagger there. Or no one put it there, and a cheap criminal bought a cheap knife.”

“Lines that lead to a wall,” Gu Yan murmured.

“I’m sorry?”

“Something a friend said.” He turned his cup on the table. “What do you want from me, Your Highness?”

Cang Yi was silent for a moment, as if choosing his words with the same care he had failed to apply to the tea.

“Your eyes,” he said. “I don’t know how you see what you see. I won’t ask. I suspect you would lie to me if I did, and I’d rather you didn’t have to. But you saw something on that granary roof that a city guard captain didn’t. You saw something in that dormitory lane that the Discipline Hall missed. I want you to keep looking. And when you see something, I want you to tell me.” He met Gu Yan’s eyes. “Not my father’s ministers. Not the Discipline Hall. Me.”

“And in return?”

“In return, your cousin will have two royal guards near her whenever she leaves the Academy, and she will never see them. In return, if you need something from the capital, a door opened, a record found, you can ask me. And in return,” he added, “you will have my word that I will never order you to do anything. I’m not commanding you. I’m asking.”

“Why not command me? You could.”

“Because people who are commanded do what they’re told.” Cang Yi turned his own cold cup in his hands. “People who agree do what’s needed. I need the second kind.”

Gu Yan thought about it for a long time. Steam had stopped rising from the teapot. Outside, a heron stood in the shallows, still as a carved post.

“I have conditions,” he said.

“Name them.”

“I won’t spy inside anyone’s house. I won’t pretend friendship with anyone to get close to them. I’ll tell you what I see, when I see it, but I decide what I go looking for.” He paused. “And if I find something that would hurt my friends, I’ll tell them first.”

Cang Yi considered this with the same frank attention he had given Gu Yan on the third floor.

“Done,” he said. Then, after a moment, he smiled, and the smile changed his face entirely, made him look his age. “That’s more conditions than my generals give me.”

He walked back along the lake path in the thin morning sun with the taste of bad tea in his mouth and a great deal to think about.

He had not gone far when someone fell into step beside him.

“Disciple Gu,” said Lu Mingyuan pleasantly. “I hoped I might find you.”

The heir of the Lu clan was eighteen and dressed in pale grey brocade so fine that it seemed to shift colour in the light. He had the kind of handsome, open face that people trusted on sight, and the kind of voice that made a request sound like a favour he was doing you. Gu Yan had spoken to him at length only once before, at a banquet where he had politely declined the Lu clan’s patronage. He looked at him now with the lines and saw what he had seen then: Mid Star Sea, five stars, channels bright and orderly. Nothing red. Nothing stolen.

Which meant nothing at all. The steward at that banquet had been the one carrying blood.

“Young Master Lu.”

“I heard about your cousin,” Lu Mingyuan said, and his voice dropped into sincere concern. “A terrible thing. On the festival night, of all nights. I’ve sent a box of Spirit-Restoring Pills to the infirmary for her; the best my family’s alchemists make. Please don’t refuse them on her behalf. She shouldn’t suffer because of my clumsiness with banquets.”

“That’s generous of you.”

“It’s the least I can do.” Lu Mingyuan walked a few steps in silence, then sighed. “I understand there was a dagger. From the Northgate forge.” He said it before Gu Yan could, smoothly, as though setting down a stone he knew Gu Yan was about to pick up. “I’ve already written to my father. He’s furious. Not at you, of course. At the thought that some criminal is carrying our steel into the city to do such things. Our forge sells to half the kingdom. It’s a reputation we’ve spent three centuries building.” He shook his head ruefully. “Someone will try to use it against my family. Someone always does. The Zhao have been whispering about us for twenty years.”

Gu Yan said nothing. He thought of Cang Yi’s finger on the wet table, sliding from one dot to the next.

“Well.” Lu Mingyuan stopped at the fork where the path turned toward the inner court. “I won’t keep you. I only wanted you to know that if you need anything, anything at all, my family’s resources are at your disposal. A disciple who could do what you did on that roof at Eighth Vein deserves better than an outer court dormitory.” He smiled warmly. “Ninth Vein now, I hear. Congratulations.”

He bowed and walked away, grey brocade catching the sun.

Gu Yan stood at the fork for a long moment.

He had told no one he had reached Ninth Vein. Not the guard captain. Not the infirmary. Mo Ji knew. Gu Lan, perhaps, if she had felt it through the fog. Han Qiu would have sensed it the moment he climbed the hill.

Lu Mingyuan had looked at him once, in passing, and known.

Or someone had told him.

Han Qiu was waiting in the courtyard, sitting at the stone table under the plum tree with a cup of tea that was, Gu Yan could tell from the smell alone, considerably better than the Prince’s.

“You were in a boathouse,” the Elder said.

“Yes, Elder.”

“With a prince.”

“Yes, Elder.”

Han Qiu sipped his tea. “I won’t ask what he wanted. He’ll have told you that no one is commanding you, and he’ll have meant it, which makes him more dangerous than his father, not less. Good men make the hardest masters, because you never notice you’re serving them.” He set down his cup. “I’ve also received a note this morning from the office of the Academy’s exhibitions. The autumn exhibition matches are in six days. The Third Prince has requested an opponent.” He looked at Gu Yan. “He’s requested you.”

Gu Yan stared at him. The Prince had not said a word about it.

“He didn’t mention it,” Gu Yan said.

“Of course he didn’t. He’d have had to ask, and you might have said no.” Han Qiu picked up his cup again. “I accepted on your behalf. You’re my disciple. You’ll fight a seven-star Star Sea cultivator in front of the whole Academy, at Ninth Vein, and you’ll lose.”

He drank.

“See that you lose well.”
