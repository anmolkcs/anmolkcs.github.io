The plane bit into the elm and sang.

It was a thin, clean sound, like a breath drawn through the teeth, and a curl of pale wood rose from the blade and fell against Gu Yan’s wrist. The shaving was so fine the dawn showed through it. He let it drop with the others and set the plane back at the head of the beam.

Outside, Jade Ridge Town was not quite awake. A rooster on the Wang family’s wall had been arguing with the sun for a quarter of an hour and losing. Down the slope, the mill wheel on the Greenwash Stream turned with its old wet complaint, and somewhere a woman was beating a quilt over a railing, each blow a soft thump that carried a long way in the cold. The air in the workshop smelled of camphor pine, linseed oil and the iron of the stove, which had gone out in the night and needed lighting.

He pushed the plane again. Sing. Again.

The beam was ironheart elm, eleven chi long, meant to be the crossbar of the new granary doors for the clan’s eastern storehouse. Ironheart was a stubborn timber, dense enough that a careless chisel would bounce off it and a careless carpenter would bounce off the clan steward’s temper afterward. Gu Yan liked it. Ironheart did not pretend to be anything other than what it was.

He stopped, set the plane down, and looked at the wood.

There was the grain any man could see: brown lines running the length of the beam, darker where the tree had grown slow through hard winters, lighter where the summers had been kind. And there was the other grain.

It came when he looked a certain way, the way one looked at a far hill until the haze sharpened into trees. Faint lines, silver as frost on a spider’s thread, rose out of the wood and hung just under its surface. They did not follow the visible grain. They ran where the strength of the timber ran. Here they flowed straight and even. Here, two hand-spans from the left end, they bunched and twisted around something buried in the beam, like water parting around a stone in a stream.

A knot. Deep inside, invisible from every face. If he cut the mortise where the steward’s drawing put it, the chisel would open that knot and the beam would split under the first heavy load of grain.

Gu Yan took a stick of charcoal from behind his ear and marked the mortise a finger’s width further right, where the silver lines ran smooth again.

Then he blinked, and the lines went away, and it was just a beam.

He had seen them since he was small. For a long time he had thought everyone did, the way everyone saw colours, until he had said to his cousin, when he was seven, that the wall of the ancestral hall was “tired on the left side,” and she had laughed at him for a week. Three months later a summer storm had brought the left half of that wall down. Nobody laughed then. Nobody connected it to him either, and he had learned something useful from that: that a thing said plainly is easily forgotten, and a thing not said at all is never questioned.

When the beam was trued, he went out into the yard to train.

The workshop sat on the very edge of the Gu clan compound, where the tiled roofs of the main family gave way to plaster walls, then to vegetable plots, then to the pine slope that climbed toward the ridge. It was the place the clan put people who were useful but not important. Gu Yan’s grandfather had been important once. Now he made doors.

The yard was beaten earth, swept every morning. In one corner stood a granite millstone with an iron ring set in its side, which Gu Yan had been lifting since he was ten. He bent, took the ring in both hands, set his feet, and breathed in the way the Nine Furnace Body Art taught: low and slow, the breath sinking past the stomach until it seemed to settle somewhere in the pelvis like a coal.

He lifted.

The stone came off the ground. His back stayed straight. The weight went down through his arms, into his shoulders, down the long muscles of his back and into the bones of his legs, and in the bones it met something that pushed back. That was the work of the 6th Forging: the tempering of the marrow. For a year and a half his bones had ached deep inside, not the ache of a bruise but a slow, banked heat, as if each one were an iron bar left in the embers. Some mornings he could feel the heat pulse in time with his heart.

He held the millstone at his chest for a count of three hundred, set it down without a sound, and moved through the forms of the Iron Hide Fist until steam came off his shoulders.

Above him the sky was turning from grey to that hard, clear autumn blue the Cang Kingdom was proud of. Gu Yan paused between forms to look at it. He always did. A pair of white cranes were climbing out of the river valley, circling up on the morning warmth, higher and higher, until they were two specks and then nothing. Where did they go, when they were too high to see? His grandmother, when she had been alive, used to say that a crane that flew high enough would reach the courts of the immortals and come back with a pearl in its beak. He had asked her once why no crane ever came back with a pearl. She had said they liked it up there too much.

“You’ll get a crick in your neck,” said a voice from the gate. “And then you’ll lose the trial and blame the sky.”

Gu Lan came through the gate with a steamed bun in one hand and half a steamed bun in her mouth. She was fifteen, a year younger than him, a main-family daughter with a round face, a sword on her hip that she had been allowed to carry for exactly four months, and a habit of arriving places at a run.

“What trial?” Gu Yan said.

She swallowed, choked slightly, thumped her chest, and held up the uneaten bun like a herald’s token. “That’s what I came to tell you. You owe me this bun, by the way. I ran all the way from the main hall. Starfall Academy is sending a recruitment envoy. To Jade Ridge. Here.” She pointed at the ground, as if the envoy might arrive on that particular patch. “One month from now.”

Gu Yan took the bun because she was holding it out, and because she would be offended if he did not.

Starfall Academy. Everyone in the Cang Kingdom knew the name, in the way that everyone knew the name of the King and the colour of the royal banners. It stood in Cangdu, the capital, eight hundred li to the west. Its elders were Heaven Seal cultivators, men and women who could make the air bend around their intent. Its Principal was said to be a Mandate expert, a being of the same order as the King himself. Disciples who entered its gates came out, if they came out at all, as the kind of people small clans like the Gu bowed to.

Envoys did not come to Jade Ridge. Jade Ridge sent its hopefuls to the prefecture city twice a decade, and they generally came home again.

“Why here?” he said.

“Nobody knows. Uncle Hong thinks the Academy is sweeping the eastern prefectures because there’s some great tournament in a few years and they want more bodies. Second Aunt thinks it’s because of the Gu name, which is what Second Aunt thinks about everything.” Gu Lan waved the question away. “It doesn’t matter why. It matters how many. The envoy will examine three candidates from each clan. Uncle Hong is holding a clan trial to choose ours. Everyone under eighteen. Strength stone, then sparring. Twenty-eight days from today.”

She said this last part looking straight at him, and he understood that the bun had been a bribe.

“I’m sixth Forging,” he said.

“So is Gu Ruo, and Gu Ruo is entering.”

“Gu Feng is eighth.”

“Gu Feng,” said Gu Lan with great dignity, “is a pillar of the clan and a very handsome boy and he has never once in his life made anything with his hands that a person could sit on. You’re entering.”

He bit into the bun. Pork and cabbage, still warm. She had genuinely run.

Cangdu. The capital had the kingdom’s archives, the royal registers of cultivators, the records of every expert who had passed through the gates of Starfall Academy in the last thousand years. Ten years ago, a man named Gu Changfeng had walked out of this workshop one winter night and not come back, and nobody in Jade Ridge could tell his son where he had gone. Perhaps nobody in Cangdu could either. But Cangdu was a great deal bigger than Jade Ridge.

“I’ll think about it,” he said.

“You’ve already thought about it. Your ears go red when you’ve decided something.” She grinned. “I’ll tell Uncle Hong to put your name down.”

The old man was awake when Gu Yan went back inside, sitting by the cold stove with a cup of yesterday’s tea, looking at the beam.

Gu Shouyi was seventy and looked eighty. He had been a big man and his frame still was, broad across the shoulders, with hands like split cedar, but the flesh had gone thin on him over the last ten years, and his left hand did not close all the way any more. When he moved, he moved carefully, as if his body were a borrowed tool he did not want to break. Once, the whole of Jade Ridge had known his name as a Heaven Seal cultivator, the strongest of the Gu in three generations. Ten years ago, something had happened on the clan’s eastern road, something that people in town still did not talk about cleanly, and his cultivation had been crippled. The clan had given him this workshop and a stipend, and he had taken up the chisel he had used as a boy, and he had not, in Gu Yan’s hearing, ever complained about it.

“You moved the mortise,” the old man said.

“There’s a knot.”

His grandfather leaned forward and ran his thumb along the face of the beam, over and over the spot where the silver lines had twisted. There was nothing to see on the surface. There never was.

“Hm,” said Gu Shouyi. He sat back. “Lan was here.”

“There’s going to be a clan trial. For Starfall Academy.”

“I heard her. Half the ridge heard her.” The old man drank his tea, made a face at it, drank it anyway. “Finish the doors first.”

That was all. Gu Yan lit the stove.

They worked through the day. Gu Yan cut the mortises and the old man cut the tenons, because his eye was still better for tenons, even with one hand weak. At midday the clan steward came by in a fine grey robe to look at the work, frowned at the moved mortise, was told about the knot, looked at the unblemished face of the wood, was told again, and went away still frowning. Children ran past the gate chasing a hoop. Bells rang twice from the main compound for the evening training session of the main-family youths, and the shouts of their drills came faintly over the walls: ha, ha, ha, the sound of young men hitting things in unison.

By dusk the two granary doors leaned against the wall, braced and pegged, smelling of fresh oil.

They ate on the step, as they did when the weather was fine. Millet porridge, pickled radish, a little salted fish. The sun went down behind the ridge and the cold came up out of the valley, and one by one, then all at once, the stars came out.

The Cang Kingdom had famous skies. Travellers from the southern kingdoms, where the air was wet and hazy, came to the eastern prefectures in autumn just to see them. On a night like this the stars were not scattered but heaped, poured across the dark so thickly that the black between them looked like the exception. The great river of light that people called the Heavenly Stream ran from the north-eastern horizon to the south-west, so bright it threw faint shadows. Gu Yan had once tried to count the stars in a single square of sky framed by the workshop eaves. He had given up at four thousand.

The old man had stopped eating. He was looking up, his bowl held loosely in his good hand, with an expression Gu Yan did not see on him often. It was not wonder. It was closer to the look he gave a joint that had been cut by someone else and cut badly.

“People say it goes on forever,” Gu Shouyi said.

Gu Yan waited.

“It doesn’t.” The old man’s voice was low, almost to himself. “It’s a ceiling. A very high one. Painted very well.” He was silent for a moment. “But a ceiling.”

“Grandfather?”

Gu Shouyi looked at him, and for a heartbeat there was something in his face that Gu Yan could not read at all. Then it was gone, and he was only a tired old man who had worked a long day.

“Old men talk nonsense at night,” he said. “Wash the bowls.”

Gu Yan lay on his cot in the loft above the workshop, listening to the beams settle as the night cooled. Below, his grandfather’s breathing had gone slow and rough with sleep. Through the gap in the shutters he could see a narrow slice of the Heavenly Stream.

A ceiling.

He thought of the cranes climbing until they vanished. He thought of the archives in Cangdu. He thought of a winter night ten years ago: rain on the roof, lamplight, the smell of wet wool, a man with his father’s face kneeling in front of him with a stone in his hand. The stone had been grey and cold, colder than river ice, and it had gone into his chest like a key going into a lock, and there had been no pain at all. Don’t be afraid, his father had said. Gu Yan had been six. He remembered the words and the cold and not much else, and in the morning his father had been gone.

He closed his eyes.

Behind his eyelids was the thing that had been there since that night. He had grown so used to it that he hardly noticed it any more, the way one stops hearing a stream one lives beside: a faint pattern in the dark, like the afterimage of a window frame after staring at a bright sky. Two lines across, two lines down. A square divided into nine. Where the lines met, and at the corners, there were points, nine of them, dim and grey and empty, like lantern hooks on a frame with no lanterns hung.

It had never done anything. It had simply been there.

Tonight, as he lay looking at it, the nine points pulsed.

Once. Silver, sudden, bright as a struck spark, all nine together, and then dark again. The lines between them flickered with the same light, and for the briefest instant Gu Yan had the impression that they did not end at the edges of the square, that they ran on outward into the dark, farther than he could follow.

His eyes flew open. The loft was black. Through the shutter gap, the stars burned in their heaps, perfectly still.

He lay without moving for a long time, his heart thudding in his chest. Then, very carefully, he closed his eyes again.

The nine nodes waited in the dark. And though he could not have said how he knew, he was certain of one thing: they had not pulsed on their own.

Something had called to them. And it had called from above.
