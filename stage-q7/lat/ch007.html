On the morning of the trial the whole of Jade Ridge came to the clan arena, because for one day nobody wanted to think about the forest.

The arena lay below the ancestral hall, a sunken circle of packed earth thirty zhang across, ringed by stone terraces where the clan sat by rank: the patriarch and elders in the covered pavilion on the north side, the main family on the terraces below them, branch families further round, and the townsfolk, who had no right to be there at all and came every time anyway, crowded onto the eastern slope with baskets of pears and roasted chestnuts and children on their shoulders. Banners of Gu blue hung limp in the still air. The morning was cold and grey, the sky low, and the smell of chestnut smoke drifted over everything.

Gu Yan stood with the other entrants at the arena’s edge, twenty-six of them, and tried to keep his hand off his father’s sword.

He had slept for two hours. The rest of the last two nights he had spent in the workshop yard with the iron jian and the battered book, working through the Flowing Cloud Sword form by form while the old man sat on the step and said nothing. It was, as his grandfather had warned, not a great art. It was a sword style for a minor sect’s outer disciples: nine forms, all of them variations on a single idea, a blade that moved like cloud before wind, yielding, drifting, sliding off a stronger weapon and coming round to strike from where it was not expected. In two days he had learned the nine forms well enough not to embarrass himself. He did not know them. Not the way Gu Feng knew his spear.

But he had read his father’s notes in the margins until he could see them with his eyes shut, and he had looked at each form the way he looked at a bracket set, at the lines of it, at how one movement joined to the next. And he thought he understood, a little, what his father had meant. Enough, for a man who could see how it was put together.

“You look terrible,” said Gu Lan, beside him, cheerfully. She was bouncing very slightly on her toes. “Did you sleep?”

“Some.”

“I didn’t sleep at all. I keep thinking I’m going to be sick.” She did not look as if she was going to be sick. She looked as if she was going to fight a bear. “Gu Feng broke through to the 9th Forging three days ago. Did you hear?”

He had heard. The whole compound had heard. He nodded.

“He’s going to win,” she said, and then, looking at him sidelong, “probably.”

The strength stone was a slab of grey-green jade as tall as a man, set upright at the arena’s centre, its face polished smooth and inscribed with a column of numerals running from bottom to top. A cultivator struck it with a bare fist, and the force of the blow travelled up through the jade and lit the numerals one by one in a pale green glow. The number at the top of the light was the strength in jin. It was the oldest instrument the clan owned, and Instructor Gu Tieshan guarded it like a hen with one chick.

They went in order of age. The youngest entrants, fifteen-year-olds at the 6th Forging, lit the stone to five thousand, five and a half, and were cheered by their families. Gu Ruo, sixteen, 6th Forging, lit it to six thousand two hundred and grinned all the way back to the line. Gu Xiu, his arm still in a sling from the boar, had not entered. Gu Lan, at the 8th Forging, struck it so hard she hopped on one foot afterward, swearing under her breath, and the light climbed to seven thousand eight hundred, and her mother in the main-family terrace stood up and clapped.

Then Gu Feng.

He walked to the stone without hurry and stood before it, and the arena quieted. He breathed in. Gu Yan, watching, let the other sight come, faintly, and saw the lines of his cousin’s body draw together like the strings of a bow being drawn: from the ground through his heels, up through his legs and hips and spine and shoulder, every line of force in his body winding into one, all of it gathered into his right fist.

He struck. The jade rang like a bell.

The light raced up the column, past seven thousand, past eight, past nine, and stopped a breath short of the next mark. Nine thousand six hundred jin.

The terraces erupted. For a 9th Forging cultivator who had broken through only three days ago, it was a superb result, and everyone in the arena knew it. Gu Feng bowed to the patriarch’s pavilion, and turned, and his eyes went straight to Gu Yan, and he walked back to the line without a word.

“Gu Yan,” called Gu Tieshan.

He walked out to the stone. He was aware of the noise of the terraces dying away, of the townsfolk on the slope craning to see. Most of them knew him as Elder Shouyi’s grandson, the quiet boy from the workshop who had made their doors and mended their tables. Some of them had heard he had reached the 7th Forging. Nobody but the old man knew about the roof.

He stood before the jade and did what he had seen Gu Feng do: drew the lines of his body together, heel to fist. But his body was not Gu Feng’s body. When he drew on it, he felt the cold dense weight of the starlight in his bones answer him, and the hot bronze of his blood behind it, and every line of force came together not like the strings of a bow but like the joints of a bracket set, each one carrying its share, the whole of him one thing.

He struck.

The stone did not ring. It made a deep, short sound, like a great door slamming somewhere far away, and the light shot up the column so fast it seemed to leap: past nine thousand, past ten, past eleven. It stopped at eleven thousand eight hundred jin.

There was a long, total silence.

In the pavilion, Second Elder had half risen from his seat. Instructor Gu Tieshan was staring at the stone as if it had insulted him. Gu Yan lowered his fist, which ached all the way to the shoulder, and turned, and walked back to the line, and was aware of every eye in the arena following him. In the pavilion, the patriarch had not moved. He was simply looking at Gu Yan, steadily, with his chin resting on his hand.

Behind the patriarch, in the shadow at the back of the pavilion where the clan’s retired elders sat, Gu Shouyi had closed his eyes.

“Eleven thousand,” Gu Lan whispered, when he reached her. Her face had gone pink. “Eleven thousand. You said you were seventh!”

“I was.”

“When?”

“Last week.”

She stared at him. Then she started to laugh, helplessly, and had to put her hand over her mouth.

The sparring went on all afternoon.

It was a simple elimination, two at a time in the arena, until one person failed to rise or yielded or was stepped out of the chalk circle. Gu Yan’s first match was against a cousin of the second branch, a fifteen-year-old with a staff, who came at him with tremendous enthusiasm and very little else, and whom he stepped out of the circle in four exchanges without drawing his sword. His second was against Gu Ruo, who had a good broadsword and a bad habit of dropping his left shoulder before he swung; Gu Yan saw the habit in the lines on the second exchange and slid inside it on the third, and the flat of his iron jian came to rest against Gu Ruo’s ribs, and Gu Ruo laughed and yielded. His third was harder: a seventeen-year-old at the 8th Forging, Gu Wen, with twin short sabres and quick feet. That one cut his forearm, a shallow slice that ran blood into his palm, before Gu Yan caught the rhythm of the sabres and turned them both aside with a single drifting parry from the fourth form of the Flowing Cloud, and put the point of his sword at the base of the young man’s throat.

In the other half of the bracket, Gu Feng beat everyone, quickly and cleanly. In the semifinal he met Gu Lan, and she fought him harder than anyone had fought him in years, her light sword darting and flickering around the long shaft of his spear, and the townsfolk on the slope roared for her, and he beat her anyway in thirty exchanges, sweeping her feet from under her with the butt of the spear and halting the point a cun above her collarbone. Then he helped her up. She took the match for third place against Gu Wen half an hour later, and fought it in such a towering fury that it was over almost before it started.

Then it was the final, and the sun was going down.

Gu Feng’s spear was ash wood, eleven chi long, with a leaf-shaped steel head, and in his hands it was a living thing.

His style was the Rolling Tide Spear, a Mortal-grade high art the clan had bought from a coastal sect a hundred years ago, and the main family’s best fighters had been perfecting it ever since. It was built on waves: thrusts that came in sets of three, each one longer and heavier than the last, like breakers rolling up a beach. The first tide tested you. The second pushed you back. The third, the clan said, drowned you.

They bowed. Gu Tieshan dropped his hand.

The first set came at once. Thrust, thrust, thrust, each one so fast the spearhead blurred, and Gu Yan gave ground before them, sliding sideways, his iron sword drifting out to meet the shaft, turning it, turning it. He did not try to block. Against that reach and that weight, a block would have numbed his arm. The Flowing Cloud did not block; it yielded and redirected, and he let it, and the third thrust of the set skated off his blade with a scream of steel and went past his hip by a hand’s breadth.

Gu Feng recovered before Gu Yan could close the distance. The spear came round, the butt this time, a sweep at his legs, and Gu Yan jumped it and came down and the second set was already coming.

He lost the first ten exchanges. He knew he was losing them. He was stronger than Gu Feng; the strength stone had said so, and every time steel met wood he felt his cousin’s arms strain against his own. But strength did not matter if he could not get close, and he could not get close. The spear kept him three paces away, then four, then five, and every set of three drove him further back toward the chalk line, and on the eleventh exchange the third thrust of the set came faster than he had read it and the spearhead opened his shoulder through his tunic. Hot pain. Blood on his chest. A roar from the terraces.

He went back three paces and stood, breathing, and looked.

Not at the spear. He had been watching the spear. He looked at Gu Feng.

The lines of his cousin’s body rose out of him in the evening light. He watched them as Gu Feng came on again, the first thrust of the set, the second, and he saw it. Before each thrust, the lines gathered. The power came up from the ground, through the back foot. And before the third thrust of every set, the drowning tide, the lines in Gu Feng’s back foot did something they did not do before the first or second: they sank. A small thing. A shift of weight to the heel, a settling, as the leg loaded itself for the longest and heaviest blow. It lasted the length of a heartbeat. It happened every time.

Every time.

The first thrust came. He let it past. The second. He gave ground, one step, deliberately, letting it drive him. He watched the back foot. The lines sank into the heel.

He moved before the third thrust began.

It was the seventh form of the Flowing Cloud, Cloud Crosses the Peak, which in the manual was a leaping cut and which his father had annotated, in tiny characters, better low. He went low. He went forward and down and inside the line of the spear, as the spear was still being drawn back to strike, and the leaf-shaped head drove through the space where his chest had been and the shaft of it went over his shoulder, and he was inside its reach, inside the circle where a spear was only a long stick, and Gu Feng’s eyes widened, and the grey iron of the jian came round and stopped against the side of his neck.

The arena was absolutely silent.

They stood like that for a long breath, Gu Feng’s spear still extended, its point quivering, Gu Yan’s sword resting on his cousin’s skin. Gu Yan could feel his own heart pounding. His shoulder was soaked with blood.

Then he lowered the sword, and stepped back, and bowed.

“Your third tide,” he said quietly, so only Gu Feng could hear, “is the best thing I’ve seen anyone do with a spear. You load it in your back heel. Only on the third. I’d fix that, before the Academy.”

Gu Feng stared at him. Something moved in his long, serious face: surprise, and anger, and then, slowly, something that was neither. He grounded the spear.

“Yes,” he said. “I think I will.” And he bowed back, as deeply as Gu Yan had.

The patriarch rose in his pavilion, and the arena fell quiet to hear him.

“The Gu clan of Jade Ridge,” said Gu Hong, his dry voice carrying easily in the dusk, “will present three candidates to the envoy of Starfall Academy. Gu Feng. Gu Lan.” He paused, and looked down at the arena, at the bleeding youth in the plain tunic with an old iron sword in his hand. “Gu Yan.”

The noise that went up then was louder than any that day. Gu Lan hit him in his good shoulder so hard it nearly knocked him over. On the eastern slope, the Lin family’s children were chanting his name. In the shadows at the back of the pavilion, the old man had opened his eyes and was looking at him, and Gu Yan could not tell, at that distance, whether he was proud or afraid.

The horn sounded while they were still cheering.

It came from the north, from the outer farms, long and low and broken off in the middle, as if the man blowing it had been cut short. The noise in the arena faltered. Heads turned. Then it came again from another tower, closer. And then a third, from the north gate itself, not a signal now but a frantic, ragged braying, on and on.

In the pavilion, the patriarch was already on his feet, and Captain Zhao Rui was running.

From beyond the walls, from the dark line of the Black Pine Forest, came a sound that Gu Yan felt in his teeth before he heard it: a deep, rolling thunder, like a river in flood, growing louder. Hooves. Paws. Thousands of them.
