The cliff behind Starfall Academy had no name. The disciples called it Han Qiu’s Cliff, though never where he could hear them. It was a shelf of grey granite above a drop of three hundred zhang, with a single stone pillar at its edge like a finger raised in warning.

Gu Yan reached it every morning before the bell, while the lanterns of Cangdu still lay far below like coals in an unbanked hearth.

Han Qiu was already there. He was always already there.

“Again,” the elder said, without turning.

Gu Yan drew his father’s iron sword. The blade had three new notches from the Valley, and he had not let anyone grind them out. He faced the pillar, set his feet, and looked.

The lines came at once. Since the Valley he no longer had to reach for them. The grain of the granite rose through the pillar in long, slanting sheets, with faults where water had crept in and frozen and thawed for centuries. A hand’s breadth above his waist, three faults met.

He cut.

The sword went into the stone a finger’s depth and stopped. A thin rain of grit pattered on his boots.

“Again,” said Han Qiu.

He cut again, the same line. This time the blade went a finger and a half. His wrists ached.

“Do you know what you are doing wrong?”

“I’m cutting where the stone is weak,” Gu Yan said.

“And?”

“And that’s what any good mason would do.”

Han Qiu turned at last. “A mason looks for the weak place because he cannot make one,” he said. “In the Valley you found lines that were already there and followed them. Good. A man who cannot see the road will not walk it. But a sword is not a road. A sword is a decision.” He laid two fingers on the pillar, far from the faults, where the granite was whole and dense. “Here.”

“There’s no line there.”

“Then draw one.”

Gu Yan looked at the unbroken stone for a long moment. In his sea of consciousness, nine nodes hung in their silver frame. Five glowed: the grain of things, the starlight in his bones, the cut, what the stars remembered, and the narrow, treacherous sliver of space he had touched in the Valley. Four were dark. Beneath all of it, like a lid of ash laid over live coals, lay the seal the Remnant had taught him, which kept the silver of his nine stars hidden behind a dull and ordinary grey.

He raised the sword and cut at the whole stone.

It rang like a bell. The shock went up his arms and down his spine, and he staggered back two steps. On the pillar there was a scratch so faint he had to lean close to find it.

“Again,” said Han Qiu, and for the first time that morning, something in his voice sounded almost pleased.

By the end of the first week the scratch was a groove. By the end of the second it was a cut a thumb deep, clean-edged, and when Gu Yan ran his finger along it he could feel, very faintly, that the stone on either side had accepted it — that the granite’s own lines now bent around the place where his sword had been, as a river bends around a rock.

“Sword intent,” Han Qiu said, watching him do this, “is not a feeling. Fools think it is anger, or confidence, or the wish to win. It is the willingness to leave a mark the world must accommodate. When your intent is deep enough that it stays in the world after your blade has gone, you are ready to engrave a seal.” He paused. “You are not ready.”

“How far away am I?”

“Further than you want. Closer than you deserve.” The elder regarded him. “And stop using the step.”

Gu Yan said nothing.

“You used it twice this week against the training puppets. I saw the air fold.” Han Qiu’s tone did not rise. “That shift through space is the most precious thing you brought out of the Valley, and you spend it like a boy with his first silver tael. A trick used twice is a habit. A habit is a thing your enemy can count on. Keep it for the day it is the only door left.”

At night the work was different.

On the blue-glazed ridge of the inner-court dormitory there was one place where the eaves of the Star Tower did not block the eastern sky. Gu Yan lay there after the lamps went out, cold tiles against his shoulder blades, and opened himself to the stars.

Starbone Refining had never been gentle. Before the Valley it had felt like being slowly filled with molten silver through every pore at once. Now, with the Remnant’s seal sitting over his Star Sea, it felt like the same molten silver being forced through a sieve. The starlight came down. The second node pulled it inward and drove it into his bones. And the seal, which existed to make him look ordinary, squeezed every thread until it was thin enough to pass unnoticed.

It hurt. His ribs felt as though someone was tapping them, one by one, with a jeweller’s hammer. His spine burned in segments. Sweat ran down his temples into his hair and froze there in the night wind.

But underneath the pain he could feel it working. His bones had a density they had not had a month ago. When he pressed his thumb against the iron edge of the gutter, the iron dented before his skin did.

He counted his stars, nine of them, like lamps behind frosted paper. He was at Mid Star Sea, and he could feel the next threshold not as a wall but as a ceiling he had not yet stood up to his full height to touch.

On the fortieth night, when he climbed down at the fourth watch, Mo Ji was sitting on the steps of the dormitory in his undershirt, waiting for him, looking as though he had not slept and did not intend to.

“I’m going to do it,” Mo Ji said. “Tomorrow. Will you watch the door?”

The breakthrough chambers were small stone rooms cut into the root of the mountain, lined with formation plates that calmed the qi of the earth. Mo Ji sat cross-legged in one with his enormous hands on his knees, three bottles of hoarded pills beside him and an expression of such ferocious concentration that Gu Yan nearly laughed.

“Don’t,” Mo Ji said, without opening his eyes.

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You were going to. You have a face for it.”

Gu Yan sat by the door with the iron sword across his knees. A cultivator breaking through to Star Sea was open in a way he would never be again, and it was a custom older than the academy that a friend should sit by the door.

He watched in the way only he could. Mo Ji’s nine primary meridians were bright with earth-yellow qi, every one of them open and full, a thick and stubborn light like late sunshine on clay. All nine fed into his dantian. And the dantian was straining. Gu Yan could see the lines of it, the shell of it, pressing outward like the walls of a kiln when the fire is too hot.

After an hour, the shell cracked.

It did not shatter. It opened, the way a seed opens. Beyond it was darkness, and space, more space than could possibly fit inside a man’s belly: the beginning of a Star Sea. Mo Ji gasped aloud. His qi poured into the opening and swirled there, looking for somewhere to settle, and Gu Yan, watching, thought of his grandfather pouring molten lead into a mould.

The first star caught. It was a heavy, dull gold, like a nugget pulled from a riverbed. Then a second, beside it. A third. Mo Ji’s face was purple. Veins stood out on his neck. The qi kept circling, and for a long breath Gu Yan thought it would stop at three, which would have been no shame at all.

Then Mo Ji bared his teeth and pushed, and a fourth star lit, smaller than the others but fierce, and the whole small constellation settled into place with a sound Gu Yan felt rather than heard, like a door closing in a distant house.

Mo Ji opened his eyes. They were wet.

“Four,” he said hoarsely. “Was it four? I felt four.”

“It was four.”

“Four!” Mo Ji lurched up and seized Gu Yan by both shoulders. “My father ignited two. My grandfather never ignited at all, he died at Seventh Vein shoeing a war horse. Four!”

“Your grandfather sounds like he had a hard life.”

“He had a terrible life, he was kicked in the head by a horse,” said Mo Ji, and burst out laughing, and then for some reason he was crying, and Gu Yan held him by the back of the neck until he stopped.

That afternoon the Star Mirror in the outer-court hall showed four gold points of light above Mo Ji’s head, and he stood in front of it for longer than was strictly necessary.

“Four,” he said again, at dinner, to everyone in earshot. Then, turning to Gu Yan with great seriousness: “Which is four more than you, according to the Mirror. I want that remembered. When we are old men, I want it on my tombstone.”

“I’ll have it carved myself,” said Gu Yan.

The others were climbing too.

Gu Lan broke into the Eighth Vein in the middle of a footwork lesson and was so startled she fell off the training post. She found Gu Yan afterwards with a scraped elbow and an enormous grin. “Grandfather Shouyi would say I did it wrong.”

“He would say you did it in a stupid place,” Gu Yan agreed. “That isn’t the same thing.”

Gu Feng was quieter about it. He had seen disciples die in the middle zone, and something in his face had settled. He reached the Ninth Vein in the second month and spent his days in the Stele Forest. When Gu Yan passed him there one evening, Gu Feng looked up from a stele and said, without preamble, “When I ignite, I don’t think I’ll have many stars.”

“Stars aren’t everything.”

“Says the man the Mirror calls zero.” Gu Feng almost smiled. “I know. I was there, remember. I saw what came out of you at the blood formation.” He turned back to the stele. “I’m not going to say anything. I just wanted you to know I know.”

Gu Yan nodded and went on. After the Valley the academy had kept quiet, and so had the royal house, and so had everyone who’d seen the silver flare in the middle zone, each of them for their own reasons. When the Mirror was consulted afterwards it had read zero again. The Remnant’s seal was good. Most of Cangdu had decided whatever happened in the Valley was the work of Elder Han Qiu and the Third Prince, and a zero-star boy who had happened to be standing nearby.

That suited Gu Yan very well.

The letter from Jade Ridge came on the fifty-first day.

It came wrapped in oilcloth and tied with the coarse hemp twine his grandfather used for everything. Gu Yan took it up to the roof to open.

Yan’er,

The courier is impatient, so I will be short. The workshop is fine. The roof leaked in the autumn rain and I fixed it. The Lan girl’s mother brings me soup twice a week whether I want it or not. Gu Hong came by and sat in the yard and said the clan is proud of you, which I suppose means you have not embarrassed us yet. Do not start.

My cough is the same as always. Do not believe anything else you hear. Do not come back here for any reason. You have a Tournament, Gu Hong says. Train. If you come home before you are sent for, I will close the door on you.

Eat properly. You never eat properly when you are thinking.

— Shouyi

Gu Yan read it three times. Then he read it a fourth time, but not the words. He looked at the brushstrokes.

He had not meant to. The lines came without asking: the weight of each stroke, where the brush had been firm and where it had faltered. His grandfather had a carpenter’s hand, square and economical. In this letter the strokes trembled. Not much — no one else would have seen it. But at the ends of the long horizontals, where a steady hand lifts the brush cleanly, there were tiny feathers of ink, the marks of a wrist that could no longer hold still. And in two places, halfway down the page, the line of the column wandered, as though the writer had stopped to cough and lost his place.

My cough is the same as always.

He sat with it in his lap a long time, looking east, toward Jade Ridge ten days away.

Then he climbed down and went to the Academy Pharmacy.

The pill he wanted was called Nine-Turn Marrow-Nourishing Pill, Earth-grade low, and it was meant for exactly this: old cultivators whose meridians had been crippled, whose bodies were slowly consuming themselves for want of the qi they could no longer hold. It would not heal his grandfather. Nothing in the Cang Kingdom could do that. But it would make the nights easier and the coughing less, and perhaps buy a year, or two.

It cost two thousand merit points. Gu Yan had two thousand one hundred and forty, most of it from the Valley. The deacon raised his eyebrows; everyone else was buying talismans for the Tournament. Gu Yan paid.

He was wrapping the jade box in oilcloth at the courier station an hour later when someone set a small porcelain jar on the counter beside it. He looked up. Su Lingxi was standing there in her grey Observatory robe, looking not at him but at the courier’s ledger, as if it interested her enormously.

“Starlight Dew,” she said. “From the Observatory’s roof basins. It is collected over a hundred nights. It soothes the lungs. If your grandfather takes a drop of it in warm water before he sleeps, the pill will settle better.”

“Lingxi—”

“It is not a gift,” she said. “It is an experiment. I want to know whether starlight affects an old cultivator’s meridians the way it affects yours. You will write and ask him how he sleeps, and tell me.” She turned to go, then paused. “Also, eat something. You look like a man who has forgotten to.”

He watched her walk away through the crowd, straight as one of her own needles, and then he wrapped the jar beside the jade box and paid the courier double to ride fast.

On the morning of the sixtieth day, the registration board went up in the great square before the Academy gate.

It was a wall of polished black stone forty zhang long, and the names glowed on it in gold as the registrars wrote them: every academy, clan, frontier army and wandering school in the kingdom, all under twenty-five, all at Vein Awakening or above. Ten thousand names, more than any Tournament in living memory, because this year the prize was a place in the eyes of the Taixu Holy Land.

Gu Yan found his own entry near the bottom of the Starfall column.

Gu Yan. Starfall Academy, inner court. Mid Star Sea. Stars: 0.

Someone behind him snorted. A lower voice said: “The Lu clan has entered its whole young generation. Every one of them. Even the ones who never fight.”

Gu Yan did not turn. He was looking past the board, past the roofs of Cangdu, to the eastern sky.

There was a speck there, low on the horizon. At first he took it for a bird, or a cloud. But clouds drifted, and birds wheeled, and this did neither. It hung in the sky, perfectly steady, and very slowly it grew — and when he narrowed his eyes and let the lines come, he saw that it was laced through and through with them, a dense and glittering web of formation-lines so intricate that his head throbbed just to look.

Behind him, the square had begun to go quiet, one voice at a time, as other people saw it too.
