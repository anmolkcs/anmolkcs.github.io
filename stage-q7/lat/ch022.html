The Nine-Story Star Tower stood at the heart of Starfall Academy the way a nail stands in a board: driven in, immovable, holding everything else in place.

Gu Yan had walked past it every day for a month and had never stopped finding it strange. It was not built of stone, or not only of stone. Its lower floors were black basalt fitted so closely that he could not see the joints even with the lines; its upper floors faded through grey granite to something pale and translucent, like frozen milk, and the ninth floor was so high and so bright that it was hard to look at in daylight. At night, the disciples said, it drew starlight down from the sky in visible threads.

“They say the founder pulled it out of the ground with one hand,” Mo Ji said, craning his neck.

“Look at the fourth floor,” Gu Yan said. “The courses change. Someone with a heavier hand laid those.”

Mo Ji squinted, saw nothing, and sighed.

Around them, nearly four hundred new disciples of the season had gathered on the tower plaza in neat rows, outer court and inner court mixed together for once. Gu Lan waved from three rows ahead, bouncing on her toes. Gu Feng stood beside her with his arms folded, looking at the tower as if it had personally insulted him. Su Lingxi stood alone near the eastern edge, compass at her belt, eyes half closed.

A deacon with a voice like a temple gong stepped onto the tower’s lowest step.

“The Star Tower is a trial and a gift,” he announced. “Each floor holds a different environment. Each floor tests a different part of a cultivator. You may climb as high as you can endure. You may sit on any floor you reach and cultivate there for as long as your merit allows. You may leave at any time by touching the jade plate beside each stair. You will not be killed. You may wish you had been.” A ripple of nervous laughter. “First floor: Heavy Earth. Second floor: Dry Sea. Third floor: Falling Stars. Beyond the third floor, new disciples need not concern themselves. Last year, of three hundred and eighty new disciples, two hundred and ninety reached the second floor. Forty-one reached the third. Two reached the fourth.” He paused. “One of those two is now dead, because he believed reaching the fourth floor meant he was ready for the world. Do not be him. Enter.”

The first floor was a vast round hall with a floor of black stone, and the moment Gu Yan stepped onto it, his body tried to become part of the floor.

It was as though every particle of him had doubled, then doubled again. His knees buckled. His spine compressed. The blood in his feet throbbed, heavy as mercury. Around him, disciples dropped to one knee, gasping, and a few simply lay down flat on the stone and stared at the ceiling.

Gu Yan stayed standing.

He had spent months drawing starlight into his bones on rooftops, feeling each strand settle into the marrow like molten silver into a mould. His skeleton was not what a Fifth Vein cultivator’s skeleton should be. He felt the floor try to crush him and his bones simply said no, in a quiet, stubborn voice that sounded a little like his grandfather’s.

He let the lines come.

The hall was threaded with formation lines like the inside of an enormous drum. They ran outward from a central point beneath the floor, heavy and brown and slow, pulling down. But they were not uniform. Near the walls they bunched together and the pull was strongest; near the centre they spread apart, and in between there were shallow channels where the pull twisted rather than fell.

Most disciples were crawling along the wall toward the stair. Gu Yan walked toward the middle instead, and the weight eased by perhaps a fifth.

“How?” Mo Ji groaned from the floor behind him. He was on hands and knees, face red, veins standing out on his neck. Earth-attribute qi rose from his skin in a dull bronze haze; he was not crushed, but he was clearly fighting it.

“Don’t fight it,” Gu Yan said. “Lean into it. Your qi is Earth; you’re pushing against your own element. Let it pull you down and stand on top of the pull.”

Mo Ji glared at him. Then he closed his eyes, breathed, and stopped resisting. The bronze haze around him sank into the stone instead of pushing away from it. Slowly, shaking, he stood.

“That,” Mo Ji said, “is the most annoying thing anyone has ever told me, and it worked.”

They crossed the hall together. Gu Lan was already at the foot of the stair, sweat running down her face, grinning. Gu Feng was beside her, pale and furious and upright; he nodded once at Gu Yan, which from Gu Feng was practically an embrace.

The second floor was a desert.

Not of sand. Of absence. The moment Gu Yan crossed the threshold, he felt the qi in his meridians flinch, the way a hand flinches from a cold surface. The air here held no spiritual qi at all. Worse, it pulled: the formation lines of the Dry Sea ran outward like the threads of a sponge, drinking any qi that leaked from a cultivator’s body.

Disciples who had stormed through the first floor on raw power slowed and stumbled here. Some turned back at once. Others sat down and clenched every channel, trying to hold their qi in, and shook with the effort.

“It’s like being bled,” Gu Lan whispered, hugging herself. Her wood-attribute qi, usually a bright spring-green glow around her, had dimmed to a faint film.

Gu Yan could see the lines drinking her. Tiny threads of green ran from the pores of her skin into the air and vanished.

“Your circulation is too open,” he said. “You learned your technique in a clan full of qi. Here, close the outer loop. Run your qi only through the inner channels, from the dantian to the heart and back. Don’t let it touch the skin.”

She tried. The threads thinned. She looked at him with something between gratitude and suspicion. “Since when do you know my technique?”

“I’ve watched you practise for ten years.”

“You’ve watched me practise for ten years and only just decided to tell me I’ve been doing it wrong?”

“You weren’t doing it wrong at home,” he said. “You’re doing it wrong here.”

Gu Yan’s own qi was not leaking much; five open veins carried less than nine, and his body, steeped in starlight, was dense enough to hold what it had. But his mind ached. Keeping the lines visible in a place that had no qi to light them was like reading by moonlight on a cloudy night.

At the far end, the stair to the third floor glowed faintly. Mo Ji reached it, put one foot on the first step and stopped. His face was grey with fatigue.

“I’m done,” he said. “If I go up there and fall over, you’ll have to carry me, and you can’t carry me.”

“I can carry you.”

“You can carry me,” Mo Ji conceded, “but I’ll never live it down.” He clapped Gu Yan on the shoulder, hard enough to rattle teeth. “Go on. Go see what the stars look like. I’ll sit here and cultivate in a desert like a hermit.”

Gu Lan and Gu Feng stayed too, Gu Feng with a long look at the stair that said next season. Gu Yan climbed alone.

The third floor was full of falling stars.

He stopped on the top step and simply looked.

The hall was round, like the first, but its ceiling was not a ceiling. It was open, or seemed to be, onto a night sky crowded with more stars than he had ever seen, even on the clearest winter night in Jade Ridge. And from that sky, slowly, constantly, motes of light were falling. They drifted down like snow, each one a tiny point of white or blue or gold, and where they touched the floor they burst into puffs of dense, cold qi that rolled across the stone like mist.

The air was thick with it. Spiritual qi so concentrated that breathing it was like drinking cold water too fast. After the desert of the second floor, his meridians gasped.

He took one step forward, and a falling star struck his shoulder like a needle of ice. Qi flooded into him, wild and unrefined, and his fifth vein shuddered under the surge. He staggered.

So this is the test. The stars were food, and they were also stones being thrown at you.

There were perhaps thirty disciples on the third floor already, scattered across the hall. Most sat cross-legged near the walls, where the falling stars thinned, wrapped in protective qi, catching only the occasional mote. A few were lying flat with their eyes shut, faces twisted. Su Lingxi was there, near the stair, her compass open, its star-iron needle drinking motes before they reached her; she gave him a small nod and returned to her meditation.

And in the very centre of the hall, where the stars fell thickest, a young man sat alone.

He was seventeen, with a face that would have been handsome on a painting and was merely direct in life. He wore plain dark robes without a single royal emblem. Falling stars struck him constantly, shoulders, head, knees, and each one dissolved into his skin with a faint chime. Around him the air shimmered with seven points of light that orbited slowly, like moons.

Third Prince Cang Yi. Early Star Sea. Seven stars.

The seven stars around Cang Yi were not decoration. Each one pulled at the falling starlight, each one hummed with a different comprehension. He was not resisting the third floor. He was eating it.

Gu Yan looked at the lines.

The falling stars were not random. They fell along lines, long silver threads stretching from the false sky to the floor, dense in the centre and sparser toward the walls. And between the threads there were gaps, pockets where the falls missed. He could walk through them. He could, if he wanted, reach the centre and barely be touched.

He thought about that, and then he did something stupid.

He walked out into the falling stars on purpose, sat down two zhang from the Third Prince, and opened his body to them.

The first ten strikes were like being stabbed with icicles. The next twenty made him want to scream. Starlight poured into him, not the gentle, distant starlight of the rooftops but raw, concentrated, falling like hail. But his body knew this. His body had been built from this. In the Lattice, the second node blazed. Starbone Refining woke and took the hail and did what it had always done with starlight: it drew it down into the bones, through the marrow, and out along the meridians.

His fifth vein filled, then overflowed. Qi crashed against the sealed gate of the sixth.

He saw the gate in lines: a knot of fibres in the meridian, tight as a fist. He had opened five of them before by battering at them with patience. Now he did not batter. The third node, still new, still cold and white, shone, and he simply chose the line along which the knot would part.

It parted.

Qi surged into the sixth vein like water into a dry riverbed, and the sensation was so intense that for a moment he forgot the stars were hitting him. Warmth filled his left arm from shoulder to fingertip. Then the warmth became a rush, and the rush became pressure, and the falling stars kept falling, and he realised he was not finished.

The seventh gate was harder. It lay deep along the spine, a knot wrapped around a knot. He chose a line and the line failed. He chose another. His nose began to bleed; the drops froze into red beads on his lip in the starlit cold. A falling star hit him squarely on the crown of the head and he almost lost the thread entirely.

A cut is a line chosen. And then the world must agree.

He refused every other line. Every one. There was only this one, the one through the centre of the knot, and nothing else in the world existed.

The seventh gate opened.

The rush through his spine was like being struck by lightning that did not hurt. His back straightened on its own. His senses widened: he could hear the chime of each star dissolving into the Third Prince’s skin, smell the cold metal of the qi, feel the minute vibration in the floor where the tower’s formation hummed. Seven veins. Seven rivers of qi running through him, broader and faster than they had been an hour ago.

He opened his eyes.

Cang Yi was looking at him. The seven stars had stopped orbiting; they hung still in the air, as if they too were curious.

“You opened two veins in a quarter of an hour,” the Prince said. His voice was even and quiet, without any edge. “Sitting in the centre of the third floor. At Fifth Vein.”

“Seventh now,” Gu Yan said, and wiped the frozen blood from his lip.

“Seventh now,” Cang Yi agreed. He studied Gu Yan with the frank attention of someone looking at an interesting piece of craftsmanship. “I watched your duel at the entrance examination. Against the Lu clan’s man. You let him attack eleven times before you moved. I counted.”

“He kept telling me where his next attack would be.”

“He didn’t say a word.”

“Not with his mouth.”

Something flickered in the Prince’s face, perhaps amusement. “Most people who come to the centre of this floor do it to prove something. To me, usually. They want to sit next to the Third Prince and endure what he endures.” He tilted his head. “You didn’t look at me once while you were breaking through.”

“I was busy.”

This time it was unmistakably a smile, brief and genuine. “I’m Cang Yi.”

“I know. Gu Yan.”

“I know.” The Prince rose in one fluid movement. Falling stars struck him and he did not seem to notice. “I’m going to the fourth floor. I’d invite you, but you’d go, and then you’d be the second disciple the deacon warns about next year.” He hesitated, as if weighing something. “I’ll wait for you higher up, Gu Yan of Jade Ridge. Don’t take too long.”

He walked to the far stair and climbed out of sight, and the seven stars went with him like attendants.

Gu Yan sat in the falling starlight for another hour, settling his new veins, until his merit ran out and the jade plate by the stair chimed.

At the foot of the stair, Su Lingxi was waiting, compass closed.

“You sat beside the Third Prince,” she said, “and broke through twice, and talked to him as if he were a carpenter’s apprentice.”

“Is that bad?”

“It’s interesting.” She fell into step beside him. “My compass has never been able to read him either. Not because he has no fate. Because he has too much. It’s like trying to read a single thread in a rope.” She was silent for a few steps. “Next to him, for a moment, your nothing looked like a hole in the rope.”

Gu Yan did not know what to say to that, so he said nothing.

On the plaza outside, a notice had been pasted to the tower’s base while they climbed. Disciples crowded around it. Mo Ji read it aloud for them in a voice that carried across half the Academy.

“By order of Principal Wen Taiyuan: in three days’ time, all disciples of the new season will attend the Principal’s annual lecture in the Hall of Ten Thousand Stars. Subject: The Shape of the World.” He lowered the notice. “The shape of the world? It’s round. Everyone knows it’s round. It’s a whole lecture about that?”

Behind them, an old deacon who had been sweeping the steps stopped and leaned on his broom.

“Boy,” he said mildly, “I have heard that lecture forty times. Every time, I come out knowing less than I thought I did when I went in.” He went back to sweeping. “Wear something warm. The Principal likes to open the roof.”
