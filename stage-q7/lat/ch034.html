They went down the rubble slope in silence, keeping to the shadows of the fallen dome, and lay flat behind the base of a broken column three hundred paces from the edge of the plaza.

From here Gu Yan could see it clearly.

The circle was perhaps a hundred paces across, laid directly onto the gold-veined paving. Its lines were not painted or carved. They were grown, thin channels of something dark and glistening that had eaten into the glassy stone like roots into rock, and they pulsed with a slow red light. Nine smaller circles were spaced evenly around the rim of the great one, each the width of a man lying down. At the center stood a shallow basin of black iron, and above the basin hung a mist of red droplets, turning slowly in the air like a swarm of tiny insects.

Two of the nine small circles were occupied. In one lay Wei Zhen of the Formation Hall, his robes dark with blood at the wrists. In the other lay Lu Qing.

“His own cousin,” Su Lingxi breathed.

Gu Yan did not answer. He had opened the Lattice—only a sliver, carefully, mindful of the throb already starting behind his eyes—and was looking at the formation’s lines.

They were ugly. There was no other word. The structures he had seen in this Valley, the ruins and the Shard Beasts and the star chart, had been ancient and strange but clean, every line purposeful, every joint true. This was the opposite. The red lines were knotted and greedy, built to draw rather than to hold. They ran from the nine small circles inward toward the iron basin in long spiralling channels, and from the bound disciples, thin as hairs, he could see threads of their life being pulled out along those channels. Not blood only. Qi. The light of their stars.

And from the basin, a single thick line ran out across the plaza and away into the mist, toward the Valley’s heart. Toward the black spires he still had not seen. Toward the place where, every night, the tug in his chest came from.

It was not a formation for killing. It was a drill.

“They’re using the disciples to bore a hole,” he said. “Through whatever protects the inner zone.”

“Why?”

He shook his head. On the plaza, the two figures at the circle’s edge were talking, and their voices carried across the still air with that strange clarity the Valley gave to sound.

“—seven more,” said the one in red. It was a woman’s voice, low and amused, roughened at the edges like a voice that had been used for a long time. “Seven Star Sea bloods of decent quality, and the Nine-Blood Piercing Array will open the spire ring for one night. You promised me twelve, little duke.”

“I promised you a chance at twelve,” Lu Mingyuan said. His tone was the same courteous, faintly apologetic one he used at banquets. “Elder Han is a Heaven Seal 6th Seal sword master and he does not trust me. The ripple talisman scattered them, but I could not choose where each one fell. You have two. My uncle’s retainers are bringing two more. The rest will come to us—they’ll be searching for each other, and the mist carries sound.”

“And the prince?”

“The prince is not to be touched.” Something hardened, just slightly, in Lu Mingyuan’s voice. “We agreed. A Third Prince dead in the Valley means a war between the royal house and every duke the King suspects. I don’t need a war. I need time.”

“Ah, yes. Time.” The woman laughed softly. She was tall and thin, and her robe was made of some silken red stuff that hung from her shoulders in long ragged panels, like wings. Her face, when she turned it toward the light of the formation, was pale and fine-boned and might have been beautiful, except that her eyes were the colour of old blood and had no whites at all. “Do you know what lies at the end of that line, Lu Mingyuan? A corpse. A real one. An Immortal’s body, one hundred thousand years dead, and still its blood has not dried. One drop of Immortal blood, refined properly, will take a Heaven Seal to Mandate. Ten drops, and my Sect Master steps into Sovereign. And you—”

“I’m aware of the arithmetic, Elder Crimson Moth.”

“You are aware of your arithmetic.” She tilted her head, bird-like. “The Lu clan has been a ducal house for four hundred years. Four hundred years of bowing to the Cang. Your patriarch is Heaven Seal 8th Seal and will die there. And you, the cleverest heir your family has produced in three generations, are Late Star Sea at eighteen with six stars, while a seven-star Third Prince your own age walks ahead of you into every room.” She smiled. “I do understand you, little duke. Better than you’d like.”

Lu Mingyuan did not answer at once. When he did, his voice was perfectly calm.

“This kingdom,” he said, “is one of a hundred and eighty in the Eastern Wastes. The Eastern Wastes is a quarter of one continent. Principal Wen likes to say it at every lecture, as if it were a reason to be humble. I’ve always heard it differently. If the Cang Kingdom is a pond, then being a duck in it is not much of an ambition.” He glanced down at his bound cousin. “Lu Qing will be honoured at home as a martyr of the Valley. My uncle will grieve very convincingly.”

On the paving, Lu Qing’s eyes were open. He was not unconscious. He was listening.

Gu Yan’s hands had closed into fists against the stone.

“He’s not cardboard,” Su Lingxi whispered. She was staring at Lu Mingyuan with a horrified kind of attention. “He actually believes it.”

“That makes it worse,” said Gu Yan.

The mist on the far side of the plaza stirred, and a group of figures came out of it.

Four of them wore the grey of Lu Hengshan’s retainers. Between them walked two Academy disciples, unbound, talking. One was broad as a door and limping slightly, with a frost-burn scar across his shoulder. The other was slimmer, sword at his hip, and moved with the careful stiffness of someone favouring a cracked rib.

Mo Ji. Gu Feng.

“—told you the Elder would be this way,” one of the retainers was saying cheerfully. “Young Master Lu found him. Says there’s a safe circle for the night.”

Gu Yan was halfway to his feet before Su Lingxi’s hand fastened on his collar and dragged him down.

“Don’t.”

“That’s Mo Ji—”

“I know who it is.” Her grip did not loosen. “And she is a Late Heaven Seal, and you are Early Star Sea, and if you run out there now you’ll be the third body in the circle and he’ll have the seat next to you.”

He knew she was right. He hated that she was right.

On the plaza, Mo Ji had stopped walking.

He was looking at the circle. At the red lines. At Wei Zhen and Lu Qing lying in their small rings with their wrists leaking slow threads of light. Gu Yan saw his friend’s broad face go still and then go very, very red.

“Young Master Lu,” Mo Ji said, in a voice like rocks grinding together. “What is this?”

Lu Mingyuan sighed. “I’m sorry, Brother Mo. Truly. Your father’s debts will be forgiven, if it’s any comfort.”

Mo Ji hit the nearest retainer.

It was not a technique. It was a fist, thrown with the whole of a 9th Vein body behind it, Earth-attribute qi packed around his knuckles like a hammerhead. The retainer—a Mid Star Sea, a full realm above him—had not expected it and did not have time to brace. He went backward off his feet and did not get up. Mo Ji was already turning, already roaring. “Gu Feng, run!”

Gu Feng did not run. He drew. His four stars blazed in the mist and his sword came out in a bright arc that drove the second retainer back three steps. For a moment the two of them stood back to back, the main-line heir of the Gu clan and the blacksmith’s son from Cangdu, and the Lu retainers hesitated.

Then Crimson Moth moved.

She did not seem to cross the distance. One moment she was at the edge of the circle and the next she was among them, her red robe flaring out like wings, and the air around her turned thick and hot and wet. Gu Yan felt it from three hundred paces away—a wave of pressure that made his teeth ache and his gorge rise. The smell of it reached him a heartbeat later: iron and rot.

That was a Heaven Seal domain of intent. He had felt Han Qiu’s once, briefly, in a lesson. Han Qiu’s had been a cold clean pressure like the edge of a blade against the skin. This was like being pushed face-first into a slaughterhouse floor.

Gu Feng’s sword stopped in mid-swing as if it had struck mud.

Mo Ji kept moving.

It was the stupidest and bravest thing Gu Yan had ever seen. His friend’s body was Earth-attribute, iron-heavy, built over seventeen years at an anvil, and the domain that froze Gu Feng’s lighter frame only slowed him. He shoved Gu Feng backward with one arm—sent him staggering out of the domain’s edge into the mist—and threw himself at Crimson Moth with the other.

She caught his fist in one pale hand.

“Oh,” she said, sounding genuinely pleased. “Oh, what a heavy little body. What thick blood. You’re not even Star Sea, are you? But your blood is dense.”

She closed her hand. Gu Yan heard the bones of Mo Ji’s fingers break from three hundred paces away.

Mo Ji did not scream. He headbutted her.

It did no damage at all. She laughed, delighted, and a red light flowed out from her palm and over Mo Ji’s arm, his shoulder, his chest, and his eyes rolled up and he folded to his knees and then to the paving, and was still.

In the mist, Gu Feng’s footsteps were fleeing. Two retainers started after him; Lu Mingyuan raised a hand. “Leave him. He’s wounded and lost, and he doesn’t know the way out. He’ll come back toward the light, or the Valley will have him.” He looked down at Mo Ji. “Put the big one in the third circle.”

Gu Yan lay behind the column and did not move. It was the hardest thing he had ever done.

Beside him, Su Lingxi was very quiet. Her compass was open in her uninjured hand. The needle was not pointing at the plaza. It was pointing away, back the way they had come, and swinging slowly.

“I can find Han Qiu,” she said.

He turned his head.

“His fate is strong. Heaven Seal fates are always strong, they’re like beacons. I’ve been feeling him at the edge of the compass for the last hour. Two li that way. Maybe three. He’s moving fast—he’s searching.” She swallowed. “If I go now, I can reach him before dawn. If Lu Hengshan’s men haven’t found him first. If the ripples don’t move me again.”

“And if they bring the other four in before then?”

“Then they’ll be that much closer to opening it.” Her voice was steady, but her knuckles on the compass were white. “They need nine. They have three. Wei Zhen, Lu Qing, Mo Ji. Fang Rui is somewhere in this mist, and Gu Feng, and the others.”

“And me.”

“And you.” She met his eyes. “You can’t find Han Qiu. You don’t have the compass, and you don’t have a fate for it to find. You’d get lost.” A pause. “I’m not leaving you here to do something stupid.”

“I’m not going to do something stupid.”

“You were halfway to your feet.”

“I was,” he admitted. “I’m not now.” He looked back at the plaza, at the formation’s ugly red lines, at the thick channel running away toward the Valley’s heart. With the Lattice open, he could see the formation’s structure laid out like a badly built roof: every beam load-bearing, every joint forced. Somewhere in that tangle there would be a place where it could fail. There always was. “Go. Find him. Bring him here. I’ll stay and watch, and if they bring anyone else in, I’ll mark where they put them. And I’ll find the joint.”

“The joint?”

“Every structure has one.” He tried to smile. It did not come out very well. “My grandfather’s a carpenter.”

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she did something unexpected: she reached out and pressed two of her thin silver needles into the collar of his robe, one on each side.

“If you use your Lattice again,” she said quietly, so quietly that he was not sure she had said that word at all, “and your nose starts to bleed, pull these. They’ll numb the pain for a little while. Not long. Don’t waste them.” She rose into a crouch. “Don’t die, Gu Yan. I haven’t finished studying you.”

Then she was gone into the mist, light-footed, her compass held out ahead of her like a lamp.

He watched for an hour. Two.

The retainers brought in no one else. Crimson Moth sat cross-legged beside the iron basin with her eyes closed, drawing on the red mist that hung above it. Lu Mingyuan walked the rim of the formation slowly, adjusting things Gu Yan could not see. Mo Ji lay in the third circle, the threads of his dense, earthy qi beginning to unspool along the channel toward the basin.

Gu Yan studied the lines until his head was a single burning ache. The formation was clever. It had three load-bearing nodes, not one; the basin, and two points on the rim, hidden beneath the paving where the red roots had grown deepest. Break the basin and the rim nodes would hold. Break a rim node and the other two would compensate. To bring it down, all three would have to fail at once. He could not see how one person could do that.

He was so absorbed that he did not notice the change in the air until it was too late.

The smell came first. Iron and rot, close, very close.

“You know,” said a low, amused voice directly behind him, “I’ve been wondering for the past hour why I could see a little mouse lying behind that column, and yet not smell him.” A cold hand settled on the back of his neck, light as a moth landing. “Every living thing has blood, child. Every living thing has a fate. And you, somehow, have neither. Do you know how interesting that is to a woman like me?”

Gu Yan did not think. He pulled both of Su Lingxi’s needles, and ran.
