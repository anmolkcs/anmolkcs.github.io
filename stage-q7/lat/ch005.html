They raised the new bracket sets into the eastern gallery of the ancestral hall on a cold, bright morning nine days before the trial, and so it happened that Gu Yan was lying on his back on a scaffold under the eaves, with a mallet in one hand and the weight of a gallery roof resting on a jack beside his ear, when the clan council began to argue about the forest.

The ancestral hall was the oldest building in Jade Ridge, a long, high, dim room of dark pillars and darker tablets, where the names of thirty generations of Gu ancestors stood in gilt characters on their shelves above the altar and the air always smelled of sandalwood ash. The eastern gallery opened onto it through a row of carved lattice screens. The council sat at the long table just beyond them. From the scaffold, through the carving, Gu Yan could see the tops of their heads.

He had not meant to listen. It was difficult not to.

“Three times in a month,” said a man’s voice: dry, level, the voice of someone used to reports. Captain Zhao Rui, who commanded the clan’s guards. He was not of the Gu blood, but had served the clan for twenty years and sat on its council by right of it. “Old Wu’s sheepfold, the Lin family’s goats, and last night two oxen from the outer farms on the north road. Not dragged off. Killed, and left.”

“Wolves,” said someone. Second Elder, Gu Yan thought, the patriarch’s cousin, a heavy, careful man who managed the clan’s tea fields.

“Wolves eat what they kill, elder.”

“Then something scared them off.”

“Possibly.” Zhao Rui did not sound as if he thought so. “There is also this. My hunters say the beasts on the forest’s edge have been moving in daylight. Foxes. Deer. Twice now, a pack of grey wolves crossing open ground at noon, with a pair of Iron-Back Boars running alongside them, and the wolves paying them no attention at all.”

There was a pause.

“That isn’t natural,” said Second Elder.

“No.”

“And the eyes,” said Zhao Rui. “Two of my men have said the same thing without being asked. The beasts they’ve seen this month have had red eyes. Not the shine you get from a torch. Red, like coals.”

Through the lattice screen, Gu Yan saw the man at the head of the table lift his hand, and the murmur that had begun died at once.

Gu Hong, Patriarch of the Gu clan of Jade Ridge, was fifty-eight, Late Star Sea, and the strongest man for a hundred li in every direction. He did not look it. He looked like a moderately prosperous tea merchant: a lean, neat man with a grey-shot beard trimmed short and long, ink-stained fingers. He had been patriarch for twenty years. In that time the clan had not gone to war with anyone, had bought two new tea estates, had married three daughters into the prefecture’s better families, and had grown from the second clan of Jade Ridge to its first, without ever once seeming to try. Gu Yan’s grandfather said of him, not entirely as praise, that he was the kind of man who checked a bridge for rot before he crossed it, and then sent someone else across first.

“How far in have your men gone?” the patriarch asked.

“To the Split Rock, and a little beyond.”

“Go further. Not far. Ten li in. Take your best, take no youths, and take no risks. I want to know whether something has moved down from the deep forest and is pushing the beasts out. If it is a Grade-3 beast king that’s lost its territory, we deal with it, or we send to the prefecture and pay them to deal with it.” He tapped one finger on the table. “Quietly. The Academy envoy arrives in less than a month. I will not have the Gu clan host the Academy in a town with wolves in the streets.”

“And the trial?” said Second Elder.

“The trial proceeds. As planned.” The patriarch’s head turned, and through the carving Gu Yan had the uncomfortable impression that he was looking straight up at the scaffold. “Elder Shouyi’s grandson is on that scaffold, I believe. He will have heard everything. Gu Yan, come down.”

He climbed down and bowed, wiping his hands on his trousers, with a sinking feeling. His grandfather, on the other ladder, had gone still.

The patriarch did not look angry. He looked at Gu Yan with mild, measuring interest, the way a merchant looks at a bolt of silk he has heard good things about but not yet handled.

“Instructor Tieshan tells me you broke a training stake,” he said.

“It was old wood, Patriarch.”

“So he said you’d say.” Gu Hong’s mouth moved, very slightly. “He also tells me you’re at the 7th Forging since last week, which I didn’t know, because nobody in this clan tells me anything until they have to. Your grandfather least of all.” A glance at the ladder. “You’re entering the trial?”

“Yes, Patriarch.”

“Good.” He seemed to mean it. “We need three. I’d like them to be three who will not embarrass us in front of Starfall Academy, and I’d like them to come home again in a few years stronger than they left, because that is the only reason to send them. I don’t care which branch they come from.” He paused. “I do care that they keep their mouths shut about clan business they overhear from scaffolds.”

“Yes, Patriarch.”

“Then go back up and finish my roof.”

Gu Feng was waiting at the foot of the hall steps when Gu Yan came out at noon to eat.

He was seventeen, the patriarch’s grandson by his eldest son, and the strongest of the clan’s youths by a distance that nobody disputed: tall, broad-shouldered, with a long, handsome, serious face and a spear that he carried as if it had grown out of his hand. He was at the 8th Forging, and the clan talked about him reaching the 9th before the trial, and he had won every sparring tournament among the youths for three years. He was proud. Everyone knew it. He did not hide it, and he did not make a show of it either; he wore it the way a good sword wears its edge.

“Gu Yan,” he said.

“Elder cousin.”

“I looked at that stake.” Gu Feng did not waste time. “After you’d gone. The break was nowhere near the old seam.”

Gu Yan said nothing. There was nothing useful to say.

“I’m not going to tell anyone,” Gu Feng said. “I don’t care how you did it. I’d like to know, but that’s your business. I only wanted to say…” He seemed to search for the words. “I’ve been fighting the same twenty people for three years. I know how every one of them moves. I’ve been looking forward to this trial because I thought there would be someone new in it.” He nodded at Gu Yan, once, like a man acknowledging a debt. “I’ll see you in the arena.”

He walked away. Gu Yan watched him go, his rice cake forgotten in his hand.

“He likes you,” said Gu Lan’s voice from behind a pillar, where she had evidently been eavesdropping. “That’s how he looks when he likes people. Terrifying, isn’t it?”

“Do you hide behind pillars all day?”

“Only the useful ones.” She fell into step beside him, stealing half his rice cake with the ease of long practice. Then, more quietly: “Xiu’s father went out with the hunters this morning. He told Xiu’s mother not to wait up. He’s never said that before.”

They walked a while without speaking. The town around them looked as it always did: washing on the lines, a knife-grinder calling from his barrow, old men playing Go under the locust tree by the well. But Gu Yan noticed things he would not have noticed a month ago. The goatherd’s boy was bringing his flock in at noon instead of dusk. The Lin family had hung a second lantern over their door. At the north gate, the watchtower had two men in it instead of one, and both were facing the forest.

Nobody had said anything. Everybody had heard something.

The hunters came back from the forest two days later, at dusk, with a body on a pole.

Gu Yan was at the north gate because his grandfather had sent him to buy lamp oil at the Lin family’s shop, which was beside it, and he was coming out with the jar when the shout went up from the watchtower and the gate swung open. Six of Captain Zhao Rui’s men came through it, moving fast, dirty, tired. Two of them carried the pole between them, and slung from it, legs bound, was a wolf.

It was a Grade-2 Shadow Wolf. Gu Yan knew it from the bestiary’s woodcuts: a long, lean, black-furred thing larger than a calf, with a pale stripe down its spine. It was the kind of beast that the clan’s hunters would usually take a whole team, and a good deal of luck, to kill.

There was not a mark on it.

A small crowd had gathered by the gatehouse lanterns. The hunters set the wolf down on the cobbles. Zhao Rui came through after them, a lean grey-haired man with a face like a hatchet, and knelt beside it, and the crowd drew back a step.

“Found it at the edge of the deep forest, nine li in,” he said, to no one in particular. “Lying in the open. And six more like it in a gully a little further on. We brought one back. I didn’t care to bring the others.”

“What killed it?” someone asked.

“I don’t know.”

Gu Yan put down the jar of lamp oil and moved closer, around the edge of the crowd, until he could see the wolf clearly in the lantern light.

Its fur was dull. That was the first thing he noticed. Not dirty; dull, as if the shine had been rubbed out of it. Its body looked shrunken somehow, the ribs too prominent, the skin too loose over them, as if the beast had starved for a month. But Shadow Wolves did not starve in autumn. Its lips were drawn back from its teeth. Its eyes were open. They were a faded, brownish red, like old rust.

He looked at it the way he had learned to look.

Since the first node had lit, the lines came easily, without pain. They came now, rising out of the dead wolf, and he understood at once that something was very wrong.

A dead animal still had lines. He had seen them in the boar’s carcass, in the hams that hung in the Lin family’s shop, in the ox that had died in the Wang family’s field last spring. When a thing died, the lines of its body did not vanish; they slackened, like ropes no longer under strain, and dimmed slowly over days, and gave way. The joins came loose. He had come to think of it as the way death looked.

This was not that.

The lines in the wolf’s body had not slackened. They had collapsed. They had drawn inward, shrivelled, each one thin and dry and grey, like the beds of streams after a drought, and they all, every one of them, led to the same place. At the wolf’s throat, under the black fur, was a cluster of tiny marks. Not a wound. He would not have seen them with his ordinary eyes. A ring of seven pinpricks, arranged in a perfect circle, no wider than a copper coin. Every line in the beast’s body ran toward that circle and ended there, cut off sharp, as if something had been drawn out through those seven points until there was nothing left to draw.

Not torn. Not bitten. Drained.

Something had emptied this wolf of whatever it was that made it a living thing, and thrown the husk away.

“You.”

Gu Yan looked up. Zhao Rui was looking at him across the body, his hatchet face unreadable in the lantern light.

“Elder Shouyi’s boy. You’ve got a strange look on you. What do you see?”

The crowd had turned to look at him. He thought of his grandfather’s hand gripping his wrist. You do not speak about lines.

“It doesn’t look like it fought anything, Captain,” he said carefully. “No wounds on the paws. No broken teeth. The fur’s not torn. Whatever happened to it, it didn’t put up a fight.” He hesitated. “It looks like it just… went empty.”

Zhao Rui looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked back at the wolf, at its shrunken flanks and its rust-coloured eyes, and something in his face changed, as if he had been thinking the same thing and had not wanted to say it.

“Yes,” he said quietly. “That’s what it looks like.”

He stood up and turned to his men. “Take it to the patriarch. Wrap it first. And none of you talk about this in the town.”

They were lifting the pole when the second shout came from the watchtower, high and sharp. Hooves, hammering on the north road, coming fast. A rider burst out of the dusk and through the half-closed gate, one of the outer-farm boys on a farm horse lathered white, his face grey with terror.

“Captain! Captain Zhao!” He nearly fell out of the saddle. “The Liu farmstead, out past the mill… the whole herd. Forty head of cattle. They’re lying in the field, all of them, and there’s not a mark on them. And Old Liu’s dog…” He swallowed. “Old Liu’s dog is standing in the middle of them. Just standing there. And its eyes are red, Captain. And it won’t stop howling at the moon.”
