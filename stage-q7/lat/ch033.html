The mist swallowed sound the way deep snow does.

They entered the middle zone at first light, roped together in pairs as Han Qiu ordered, each pair within arm’s reach of the next. Gu Yan was paired with Su Lingxi. Ahead of them walked Mo Ji and Gu Feng; behind, Wei Zhen and Fang Rui. Beyond those four he could see nothing at all, only grey-white vapour pressing so close that the copper sun was a smear and the ruins on either side were dark shapes that might have been walls or might have been cliffs.

The gravity was worse here. It came in waves. One step it was three times the outside world, dragging at his knees until his Starbone creaked; the next it eased so suddenly that he nearly stumbled forward. Su Lingxi was pale with effort. She was Early Star Sea with five stars, ice and starlight, light-footed and precise, and the Valley’s weight punished exactly that kind of body.

“Breathe from the dantian,” he said quietly. “Let your weight sit low. Don’t fight the change—wait for it.”

“I know how to breathe,” she said. A pause. “Thank you.”

The dripping sound grew louder as they went. It came from everywhere and nowhere. Once Gu Yan thought he glimpsed, through a gap in the mist, the red line he had seen the evening before, curving away into the haze; but when he opened the Lattice to look, the mist itself was full of strange broken lines, tangled and shifting, and his head began to pound, and he closed it again.

They had walked perhaps two hours when the world folded.

He felt it before he saw it. The Lattice in his sea of consciousness jerked like a startled animal, and every line around him—the ruins, the paving, the mist, the rope between him and Su Lingxi—bent. Not broke. Bent, all together, in one direction, the way reflections bend in a pond when a stone is dropped into it.

“Ripple!” someone shouted ahead—Fang Rui, her voice suddenly tiny and far away. “Spatial ripple! Hold your—”

The ground went out from under his feet.

He was not falling. He was being moved. There was a sensation of being pulled sideways through something thick, like being dragged through honey, and a pressure on his ears and eyes that went on and on. He had hold of the rope. He held on to it with both hands and felt Su Lingxi’s weight jerk at the other end. Then the honey thinned and something hard struck his shoulder and he was rolling across stone in near-darkness, and the rope went slack, and somewhere close by Su Lingxi cried out.

When he could see again, he was lying on a curved floor under a broken dome.

There was light, dim and silvery, coming in through a jagged gap high above. The air was very still and very cold. The dripping sound was gone. So was the mist. So was everyone else.

“Su Lingxi.”

“Here.” Her voice came from his left, steadier than he expected. “I’m not hurt. I think I sprained something in my hand.” She sat up slowly, cradling her right wrist. Her hair had come loose from its pins, and there was a long scrape along her cheekbone. “Where are we?”

Gu Yan got to his feet. Every bone he had hurt, but the Starbone had taken the impact; nothing was broken. He looked around.

They were inside a vast round chamber. The floor was a shallow bowl of the same gold-veined glassy stone, some sixty paces across—a modest room, for its makers. The walls curved up and inward to form a dome, most of which had collapsed: the eastern half was a slope of enormous rubble, and through the hole above it showed a patch of violet sky. Around the walls, at the height of a giant’s chest, ran a broad ledge, and on the ledge sat objects. Rings of bronze, nested one inside another. Long tubes of crystal mounted on pivots. A disk of black stone as wide as a threshing floor, its face engraved with thousands of tiny points.

Su Lingxi drew in her breath.

“It’s an observatory,” she said.

She was on her feet before he could stop her, crossing the bowl of the floor toward the black disk, heedless of her wrist. He followed. The disk was set into the wall at a slight angle, facing upward toward where the dome’s apex had been. Its surface was not stone at all; it was something darker, smoother, a polished black that seemed to have depth, like looking into a well at night. The tiny points engraved on it were not engraved. They were lit, very faintly, each one a pinprick of silver.

“A star chart,” Su Lingxi whispered. “Look at it. Look at how many.”

There were tens of thousands. More. The longer he looked, the more he saw, finer and finer points crowding into the black until it was less a chart than a sky.

“Are these the stars of this world?” he asked. “Of the—the Domain it came from?”

“I don’t know. Some of them.” She reached out with her uninjured hand and touched the disk’s edge, very lightly. Her compass, hanging at her belt, began to spin. “Some of them aren’t stars at all. Look. There. And there.” She pointed to clusters of points arranged not in constellations but in shapes, tight geometric patterns, squares within squares. “They’re marking something. Positions. Places.”

He looked where she pointed.

Then, slowly, he opened the Lattice.

He was prepared for the pain. He was not prepared for what he saw.

The star chart woke.

Every point on the black disk sprouted lines. Not the lines of the disk itself—the disk’s own structure was simple, a single slab of some strange material—but lines that came from the light inside the points, as if each star remembered where it had been and was reaching back toward that place. The lines rose off the disk and crossed in the air of the ruined observatory, faint and silver, weaving together into a structure he could almost grasp: a frame, enormous, intricate, hung with lights like lanterns on a festival scaffold.

It looked like the Lattice.

Not exactly. Not his nine nodes. But the same kind of thing: a grid, with points of light caught at its intersections, and lines running between them that were not distance or direction but something more fundamental. The way things were joined.

He staggered. Blood ran hot from both nostrils.

“Gu Yan!” Su Lingxi’s hand closed on his sleeve. “Stop. Whatever you’re doing, stop—”

“I can’t read it,” he said thickly. He could not look away. “It’s—it’s too large, I can see the shape but not what it means, it’s like looking at a map in a language I—”

“Then let me read it.”

She did not let go of his sleeve. With her injured hand she lifted her jade compass, and he heard her breathing change, slow and deep, as she drew on her own five stars. A thin cool light rose off her—ice and starlight, the Observatory’s art—and touched the face of the disk.

“The Observatory teaches that stars remember,” she said. Her voice had gone distant, careful, as it did when she lectured him in the archive. “Not the stars themselves. Their light. Light carries the shape of where it came from. If you know how to listen, a star’s light will tell you what it saw. That’s how we read fate, a little—we read what the stars remember about a person. That’s how my compass works.” A pause. Her hand tightened on his sleeve. “This light remembers a great deal.”

He felt her reading pass into the chart. And because his Lattice was open and touching the same lines, he felt it pass into him.

It was like being handed a key.

Suddenly the lines had meaning—not all of it, not even a hundredth of it, but enough. The points on the chart were not stars; they were worlds, and the lines between them were the paths of light from one to another, and the light remembered. It remembered being a city, tall and full, under a violet sky. It remembered the other cities, on the other points, the long silver roads between them. It remembered the weight of its own laws, heavy and certain. It remembered the light of three thousand smaller lanterns hanging far below, dim and warm.

It remembered falling.

He saw it—or the light did, and showed him. A shudder through the whole frame. Lines snapping, one after another, like the strings of a lute cut with a knife. A piece of the frame tearing loose. The city under the violet sky tilting, sliding, dropping, through layer after layer of something that burned, toward a small blue-green lantern far below.

And then it was over, and he was standing in a ruined observatory with blood running down his chin and Su Lingxi gripping his arm, and in his sea of consciousness, the fourth node of the Lattice had lit.

It lit softly, unlike the others. Structure had blazed. Starbone had burned. Sword had cut its way into being like a blade unsheathed. This one simply opened, the way an eye opens, a cool silver glow spreading through the empty circle of the node until it was full. And with it came understanding, as plain and solid as a joint fitting home: Starlight. What the stars remember. Light was not only energy to be drawn into bones. Light was a record. Every star he had ever drawn on had been telling him where it had been, and he had never known how to hear it.

The lit nodes reinforced each other. He felt the four of them—Structure, Starbone, Sword, Starlight—settle into a new arrangement, the silver lines between them thickening. The first row of three was complete already; now the fourth, the start of the second row, hummed against the first like a tuning fork.

And in his Star Sea, the nine gray stars stirred.

One of them changed. The third star—the star that held his Sword comprehension, the one that had blinked on his first night in the Valley—grew brighter. Not silver, not yet; still gray to any mirror. But at its heart something had kindled, thin and keen, a sliver of edge. His sword intent. It had always been a flicker before, a thing that came and went with his concentration. Now it lived in the star. He could feel it there, like a blade lying on a table, waiting to be picked up.

He came back to himself sitting on the curved floor with his back against the ledge. Su Lingxi was kneeling in front of him, wiping the blood off his face with the hem of her sleeve. Her own face was very close and very pale.

“You stopped breathing,” she said. “For about ten breaths. I was going to hit you.”

“Thank you for not hitting me.”

“I hadn’t decided yet.” She sat back on her heels. Her hands were shaking slightly, he saw, and she folded them in her lap to hide it. “What did you see?”

He thought about how to answer. He had kept the Lattice secret for ten years. Grandpa had told him to keep it secret until he died. And this was a girl from the Royal Star Observatory, whose family served the King, who had cornered him in an archive and made a bargain to study him like a strange comet.

She had also just put her own reading into his mind without hesitating, because he was bleeding and she had decided he needed help.

“I saw it fall,” he said finally. “This place. I saw where it came from. It was one of a great many. They were all joined together, like—” He stopped. “Like a frame. And something broke the frame, and this piece fell.”

She was silent for a long moment.

“The Shattering,” she said. “The old texts call it that. One line in the Observatory’s oldest catalogue. In the age before the Cang, the heavens shattered and stars fell like rain. Everyone thinks it’s poetry.”

“It isn’t.”

“No.” She looked up at the black disk, still faintly glowing above them. “When I read it, I felt you. In the reading. That’s never happened before. My compass has never shown me anything for you, and just now I could feel you, like a—” She frowned, searching for the word with the same precision she used for everything. “Like a thread pulled tight. Running out of the chart and into you.” She paused. “And then up.”

“Up?”

“Up. I don’t know. That’s what it felt like.” She shook her head sharply and got to her feet, brisk again, all Observatory. “Can you walk? We need to find the others. Han Qiu will be furious.”

He let her pull him up. Her hand was cold and steady, and she held on a moment longer than she needed to before letting go. Neither of them mentioned it.

It took them until nightfall to find a way out of the observatory, climbing the slope of rubble beneath the broken dome. When they emerged onto its rim, the mist lay below them like a white sea, stretching away in every direction, and above it the unfamiliar stars were coming out.

Gu Yan looked at them, and for the first time, he could hear them. A faint chorus, at the edge of perception. Each one remembering.

Then Su Lingxi caught his arm and pointed.

Below them, perhaps two li away, the mist had parted. Through the gap he could see a broad plaza ringed by broken columns, and on the plaza, laid out in a vast circle, lines of dark red light pulsing slowly, wetly, like the beating of an enormous heart.

At the circle’s edge stood a figure in pale blue. Even at this distance, Gu Yan knew the set of those shoulders.

Lu Mingyuan was talking to someone in a long red robe. And at their feet, bound and motionless, lay two Academy disciples.
