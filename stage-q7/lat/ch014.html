Of the nine thousand who had stood in the square at dawn, a little over three thousand were permitted to pass through the gate the next morning.

They were led up the white stair, which today lay quiet and harmless beneath their feet, nothing but stone, and across the terrace at its top where the Third Prince had stood, and through a gate of dark wood into a courtyard paved with black slate. In the middle of the courtyard stood a single stele.

It was not large. Perhaps twice the height of a man, a slab of grey-green stone with a rounded top, its face worn almost smooth. Whatever had once been carved on it had long since weathered to shallow ghosts. Ancient pines grew around the courtyard’s edges and dropped their needles on the slate, and the air had the cold stillness of a temple.

“The Heart-Mirror Stele,” said the old woman in grey, who stood beside it. Gu Yan had learned overnight that she was Elder Ruan of the Examination Hall, and that she was a Heaven Seal cultivator of the 4th Seal, and that candidates who argued with her scores did not become disciples. “It was moved here from the Dao Stele Forest two hundred years ago. Around it, the Academy has laid an illusion formation. You will enter in groups of fifty. The formation will show you your own heart.”

A murmur went through the crowd.

“It is not a test of strength,” Elder Ruan went on. “It is a test of what you are made of. Fear, greed, rage, despair, the formation will find whatever lies deepest and show it to you as truth. Some of you will see your worst moment. Some of you will see what you want most. Your task is to recognise the illusion as illusion and walk out of it. The longer you endure, and the more clearly you return, the higher your score.” She looked over them with eyes like flint. “Some of you will not return clearly. You will be pulled out. It will be unpleasant. You will be given tea.”

“I hope it’s good tea,” murmured Gu Lan.

“It’s terrible tea,” said Mo Ji, with the confidence of someone whose cousin’s friend had told him.

They went in by lots. Gu Yan’s group was the eleventh.

He watched the groups before him. Fifty candidates would file into the courtyard and sit cross-legged on the slate in a ring around the stele, and the formation would wake: a faint shimmer in the air, like heat over a summer road, and then the candidates’ faces would go slack, their eyes open and unseeing. Some smiled. Some wept. One boy in the third group began to scream and was pulled out within twenty breaths by an attendant who struck him lightly between the shoulder blades; he came to, gasping, and was led away to the tea. Others sat for an incense stick, two incense sticks, as still as statues, and then blinked and looked around in bewilderment, and were told their scores.

Gu Feng went in the eighth group and came out after nearly two sticks, white and silent, and would not say what he had seen. Gu Lan went in the ninth and came out quickly, laughing and crying at the same time, and said she had seen her mother’s cooking and that it was so delicious she knew immediately it couldn’t be real. Mo Ji went in the tenth. He sat for one stick with his jaw clenched and his huge fists knotted on his knees, and then he stood up abruptly in the middle of the illusion, shook himself like a dog, and walked out of the ring on his own.

“I saw the anvil being carried off,” he said when Gu Yan asked. He did not smile. “And my father standing there. I told him I’d buy it back.” He looked at his hands. “Then I knew it wasn’t real. Because in the real one I’d have been fighting.”

Then it was the eleventh group.

Gu Yan sat on the cold slate with forty-nine strangers and looked at the stele. Up close, through the first node’s sight, he could see the formation lines laid around it, a beautiful, intricate web like frost on a window, fed by spirit stones buried at nine points beneath the slate. And under the formation, in the stone itself, other lines. Very faint. Very old. They did not belong to the Academy’s work at all.

The shimmer rose.

He let it take him.

He was in the workshop.

It was so complete that for a moment he forgot to doubt it. The smell of cedar shavings and pine resin and the linseed oil Grandpa used on his tools; the slant of morning light through the open front; the bench under his hands, its top scarred with sixty years of cuts. A half-finished cabinet stood on the floor, its joints dry-fitted. Grandpa sat on his stool by the door, whittling, as he did in the mornings when his chest was bad.

“You’ll need to plane that edge again,” Grandpa said without looking up. “It’s not true.”

Gu Yan looked at the edge. It was not true. He picked up the plane and set it to the wood, and the long curl of shaving came up pale and perfect, and he felt the old, quiet joy of it.

This is what I want, he thought. This is what I left.

The thought was so clear and so sad that it woke something in him, and the joy turned and showed its other face. He had left this. He had chosen to. He looked at Grandpa’s hands on the whittling knife, and saw, as he had seen on the hill above Jade Ridge, the blood on the old man’s chin. The way his breath rattled now.

“Grandpa,” he said. “Your chest.”

“Is fine. Plane your board.”

“It isn’t fine. You burned what was left of your cultivation. For me.”

Grandpa stopped whittling. He looked up, and his eyes were not quite his eyes; they were too steady, too kind. “Then stay,” he said. “Stay and look after me. You don’t have to go. Nobody would blame you. The world out there is very big, Yan’er, and it doesn’t care about one carpenter’s boy.”

It was exactly what he had been afraid Grandpa would say, and exactly what Grandpa never would have said.

“No,” said Gu Yan gently. “You’d hit me with the plane if I stayed.”

The workshop shuddered like a reflection in water. And behind Grandpa, in the open doorway, where the yard should have been, there stood a man.

He had his back to Gu Yan. He was tall, in a grey travelling robe, with a sword at his hip whose wrapped hilt was worn smooth in two places. He was looking up. Not at the sky over Jade Ridge, but at something above it, and as Gu Yan watched, the man raised one hand and laid it flat against the air, as a man might lay his hand against a wall.

“Father,” said Gu Yan.

The man did not turn around.

The workshop dissolved. The man dissolved. And Gu Yan was standing in darkness, and in front of him, filling his vision, was the Lattice.

He had seen it every day for ten years. Nine nodes in a square frame. Silver lines. Two lit.

But now the lines did not stop at the frame’s edge.

They ran on. Out from each side of the square, silver threads extended into the dark, faint at first and then brightening as he looked at them, and where they ran they met other threads, and crossed, and formed other nodes. Another square of nine beside his own. Another above it. Another, and another. The grid went out in every direction, and it was not a flat thing; it went up, and down, and into directions he had no words for, nodes upon nodes upon nodes, each a tiny point of cold light, until the frame he had always thought was the whole of the Lattice was a speck, a single cell in a structure so vast that his mind could not hold—

His mind could not hold it.

It was like trying to drink a river. He felt himself stretch, and thin, and something in the back of his skull gave a wet, terrible pop—

He was on the slate. He was on his side, curled around his own head, and someone’s hand was hard between his shoulder blades, and blood was running freely from both nostrils onto the black stone.

“—out,” the attendant was saying, alarmed. “He’s out, Elder, I didn’t strike him, he just—”

“I saw.” Elder Ruan was crouching beside him. Up close, her face was lined like a dry riverbed. She held two fingers against his wrist, and he felt a cool thread of her qi slip into him, examining, and then withdraw. “Mind-shock,” she said. “Not serious. Boy. Can you hear me?”

“Yes, Elder.” His voice was hoarse.

“You were in for a little over one stick. Your heart was steady, then you went somewhere the stele did not send you, and it threw you out.” Her eyes held his. “What did you see?”

The Lattice was back in his mind, dim and small and quiet. Nine nodes. The frame. He could not see any lines running beyond it. But he knew they were there now, the way you know a room is there after someone has blown out the lamp.

“My father,” he said. “I haven’t seen him since I was six.”

It was true. It was not all of the truth, but it was true, and Elder Ruan studied him for a moment and then nodded and stood.

“It happens,” she said. “Old wounds are deep. Sometimes they go deeper than the formation was built for.” She turned to the recorder at the table. “Eleventh group, number thirty-one. Gu Yan. Middle grade.”

Middle grade. Of the six grades, it was the fourth. Respectable. Unremarkable.

He sat up slowly, pinching his nose. An attendant brought him a cup. Mo Ji was right; the tea was terrible, bitter and lukewarm, and he drank all of it because his hands needed something to do. The other members of his group were still sitting in their ring around the stele, their faces moving with private dreams. One girl was smiling. A boy was clenching and unclenching his hands.

The old lines in the stone. He had seen them before the illusion took him: faint, ancient, nothing to do with the Academy’s formation.

He looked at them again now, with the sight, carefully, from the very edge of his strength.

They were a grid.

Not a grid like his. Not silver, not nine nodes. Just a few crossing lines, so worn as to be almost nothing, buried deep in the grey-green stone like the fossil of a leaf. But they crossed at right angles. They had the same rightness as the Lattice, the same feeling of a pattern that was not made but simply was.

His head throbbed. He let the sight go.

By the time the last group was done, the sun was low and red over the western wall. The candidates who had passed both stages, a little under fifteen hundred now, were told to return at dawn for the duels. Gu Yan went to find his cousins and Mo Ji, and found instead, in the shadow of the dark wooden gate, waiting, the girl in pale blue.

She was standing exactly where he would have to pass. That was clearly not an accident.

Up close she was younger than he had thought, his own age or near it, with a narrow, serious face and eyes so dark they were almost black. She wore the plain blue robe of no great family, but the silver pin in her hair was worked with a pattern of tiny constellations, and the jade compass hung from her sash on a cord of white silk. Her fingertips, he noticed, were calloused at the ends, the way an embroiderer’s are.

“Gu Yan,” she said. “From Jade Ridge. Seven hundred and ninety-one on the stair. Middle grade on the stele.”

“You’ve been reading the scores.”

“The recorder’s table is not hard to read.” She did not smile. “Su Lingxi. Of the Observatory.”

The Royal Star Observatory. Even in Jade Ridge, people had heard of the Observatory; they were the ones who read the stars for the king, who set the calendar, whose almanacs every farmer bought at the new year. Gu Yan bowed politely. She returned the bow, precisely, by the exact degree a junior of one family owes a junior of another.

Then she said, with no preamble whatsoever:

“Why do you have no fate?”

He stared at her.

She held up the compass. It was a small, beautiful thing, a disc of white jade inlaid with silver rings, and at its centre a needle of some dark metal hung balanced on a single point. Fine characters ran around the rings: names of stars. Names of hours.

“Every living thing,” said Su Lingxi, “is woven into the heavens. Into fate. It is not mysticism; it is measurement. The compass reads the thread that ties a person to the stars. A strong person, a strong thread. A weak person, a weak one. A person about to die, a fraying one.” She turned the compass so he could see it. “I have read four hundred and eleven people today. Everyone has a thread. The Third Prince’s is so bright it nearly burned my eyes.”

She stepped closer and held the compass up between them.

The needle did not move.

It did not point at him. It did not point away from him. It hung in perfect balance on its point, utterly still, exactly as if there were no one standing in front of it at all.

“Yesterday on the stair,” she said quietly, “I thought my compass was broken. I had it checked by my uncle this morning. It is not broken.” She lowered it. Her dark eyes searched his face, not unkindly, but with the fierce, impersonal interest of a scholar who has found a word that is in no dictionary. “So I am asking you. Gu Yan of Jade Ridge. Why does the sky not know that you exist?”
