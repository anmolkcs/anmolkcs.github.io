By the time Gu Yan reached the north gate, the outer farms were burning.

He could see it from the top of the gate street: a line of orange light along the valley floor where the Liu farmstead and the Wang family’s barns and the long thatched sheds of the tea-pickers’ village stood, or had stood. Smoke climbed into the low clouds and turned their undersides the colour of a forge. Between the fires and the town walls, the dark fields were moving. Not the way grass moves in wind. Moving like water, in a single broad tide, rolling toward the town.

And down the road toward the gate, ahead of the tide, the people of the outer farms were running.

The gatehouse was a storm of noise. Guards were shouting, the horn on the tower was still braying, children were screaming somewhere behind him in the street. The great gate stood open, both leaves of it, and the farmers poured through: men carrying children, women dragging elderly parents by the arm, a boy with a goat in his arms, a cart piled with bedding that had lost a wheel and was being pushed, lurching, by four people at once.

On the wall above the gate, Captain Zhao Rui was bellowing at the gatekeepers to hold, hold, hold.

The patriarch had given his orders in the arena in less time than it took to draw breath. Every cultivator of the 9th Forging and above to the walls. Everyone else to the inner town, to the ancestral hall and the granaries, to guard the old and the children. Gu Lan had opened her mouth to argue and Gu Hong had looked at her once, and she had closed it again and gone, white with fury, to herd terrified families up the hill.

Gu Yan had his father’s sword at his hip, his left shoulder bound tight with a strip torn from his tunic, and the blood from the spear cut had stopped running. Gu Feng was beside him, his spear in his hand. Neither of them had said a word since the arena.

“Wall or gate?” said Gu Feng.

“Gate,” said Gu Yan. “Until they’re all in.”

The first beasts reached the road while the last of the farmers were still on it.

They came out of the dark at the edge of the gate lanterns’ light: wolves first, grey wolves, a dozen, running low and silent, faster than any wolf had a right to run. Their eyes caught the lantern light and gave it back red. Not the green or gold of beasts’ eyes at night. Red, like embers in a brazier when you blow on them.

A farmer at the tail of the crowd, an old man with a sack over his shoulder, looked back and saw them and fell.

Gu Yan was running before he had decided to. Out through the gate against the tide of people, sword drawn, the iron jian cold in his hand. Behind him he heard Gu Feng swear and follow. The lead wolf reached the old man first and went for his throat.

Gu Yan did not think. He saw the wolf’s lines as it leapt: the long braided bundles of force running from its hind legs along its spine and through its neck to its jaws, the whole beast one stretched line aimed at the old man’s throat. And in the middle of that line, where the neck joined the shoulders, the place where it all bent.

He cut there.

The iron jian went through fur and flesh and the joint of the spine as if they were a knot in soft pine. The wolf fell in two pieces across the old man’s legs. Gu Yan hauled the farmer upright by his collar and shoved him toward the gate, and turned, and the second wolf was already in the air.

He let the Flowing Cloud take him. The sword drifted, met the wolf’s open jaws edge-first, and turned them aside; the beast’s own weight carried it past him, and his blade came round behind it and opened the tendons of its hind legs. Beside him, Gu Feng’s spear punched through the chest of the third wolf and withdrew and punched through the fourth, the first and second tides of the Rolling Tide Spear, so fast they were a single sound.

Then the rest of the pack were on them, and there was no more thinking.

He never knew, afterward, how long they held the road. Long enough. Long enough for the last cart to lurch through the gate. Long enough for his arm to grow heavy and his breath to come ragged and a wolf’s claws to open three bloody furrows down his thigh. He fought with his back to Gu Feng’s, the spear’s reach covering the sword’s short range, the sword covering the space inside the spear’s reach. He did not have to see his cousin. He could feel him: the push of the spear’s thrusts through the ground, the rhythm of the tides, one, two, three.

And all the time, he used the lines.

They came easily now, without pain, without effort, as easily as seeing colours. In the chaos of the fight they were the one clear thing. Every beast that came at him was a structure, and every structure had a joint where it bent, and the iron jian went to those joints and nowhere else. He did not have the strength to cut through bone. He did not need to. He cut tendons, the places where muscle met bone; he cut the hinges of jaws, the backs of knees. He did not swing once more than he had to. He could not afford to.

“Inside!” Zhao Rui’s voice, from the wall above. “Inside, you fools! Close the gate!”

The gatekeepers were heaving at the great leaves. Gu Yan and Gu Feng gave ground step by step until the gate’s shadow was over them, and then they turned and ran, and the gate swung shut behind them with a crash that shook the walls. Something heavy slammed into the other side a heartbeat later. And another. The bar dropped into its brackets.

Gu Yan leaned against the inside of the gate with his chest heaving. On the other side, something was clawing at the wood.

“Wall,” gasped Gu Feng.

“Wall,” Gu Yan agreed.

From the wall, he saw the whole of it.

Jade Ridge’s walls were old and modest: rammed earth faced with stone, two zhang high, with a timber walkway along the top behind a palisade of sharpened logs. They had been built three hundred years ago to keep out bandits. They had never been built for this.

Below him, the fields beyond the north wall were a sea of beasts.

Wolves. Boars. Deer with their antlers lowered. Black Pine foxes as large as dogs. Mountain cats. Badgers. A pair of great grey bears lumbering among them. Things he had seen only in the bestiary, like the long-bodied, scaly Rock Lizards that came down from the high crags only once in a decade, and things he did not recognise at all. They pressed against the base of the wall in a solid heaving mass, climbing over each other, wolves running up the backs of boars, cats leaping from the bears’ shoulders to claw at the palisade. And every one of them, every single one, had red eyes.

They did not fight each other. That was the thing his mind kept returning to, as he hacked at a mountain cat that had got its forelegs over the palisade, as he kicked a Rock Lizard’s snout away from the walkway’s edge. Wolves ran beside deer. Cats climbed over boars. The bears did not swat the foxes that scrabbled between their feet. Each beast in that seething tide acted as if it were the only thing in the world, and the only thing in the world it wanted was to come over the wall.

The guards fought along the walkway, two dozen men, clan cultivators at the 9th Forging and the Vein Awakening realm, Gu Tieshan among them roaring like a bull and smashing beasts off the palisade with an iron staff. It was not enough. In three places, the beasts were over the palisade. Gu Yan saw one guard pulled down off the walkway by a wolf and vanish screaming into the tide below. He saw another, a young man he had sold a chest to last summer, fall back with his face laid open by a bear’s claw.

Then the patriarch came.

Gu Hong walked along the top of the wall as if he were walking through his tea fields. He wore a plain grey robe and held no weapon. Where he passed, the beasts that had come over the palisade simply stopped, as if they had struck a wall of glass, and then were flung back over it into the dark. Around him the air was full of light: four faint points of it, circling him like moths around a lantern, pale gold, each the size of a fingertip. His stars. Gu Yan had heard of them, the stars of a Star Sea cultivator, the condensed comprehensions that lived in the dantian and lent their power to every blow. He had never seen them made visible, as they were made visible now in the fury of the patriarch’s qi.

At the wall’s northwest corner, a section of palisade gave way with a crack like thunder, and through the breach came a thing the size of a cart.

It was a rhinoceros, or something like one. Its hide was black and seamed like old cracked lacquer, and a single horn as long as a spear rose from its snout, and its little eyes burned brighter red than any of the others. Grade-3, Gu Yan thought numbly. An Iron-Horn Rhino. The kind of beast a whole team of Vein Awakening hunters would flee from.

The patriarch turned, and went toward it, and did not run.

Gu Yan did not see the fight. He saw the beginning of it, the rhino lowering its head and charging along the walkway, timbers splintering under it, and Gu Hong lifting one hand, palm out, with all four of his golden stars blazing at once, and a sound like a mountain falling. Then a wolf came over the palisade at him, and he had to kill it, and when he looked again the patriarch and the rhino were down off the wall, among the tide, in a storm of light and dust and screaming.

It was in the next breathing space, leaning on the palisade with his sword arm trembling and the sweat freezing on him, that Gu Yan saw the threads.

He was looking at the tide below the wall, at the beasts’ lines, as he had been looking all night, picking out joints. And he noticed, at last, what had been there all along.

Each beast had a thread.

It was not like the other lines. It was thin, thinner than the finest of them, and it was not silver. It was a dull, dark crimson, the colour of old blood, and it came out of each beast at the same place: the throat, just below the jaw. Where the ring of seven pinpricks had been on the drained wolf at the north gate. A crimson thread rose from every beast in that tide, rose up a little way into the air and bent and ran away northwest, toward the dark.

There were thousands of them. He had not seen them because they were so fine and because there were so many, the way one does not see rain until one looks for it. Now that he saw them he could not stop seeing them. They streamed off the tide of beasts like silk from a thousand spindles, and bent all together toward the northwest, and drew together as they went, and wound into one.

He followed them with his eyes.

Northwest of the wall, half a li beyond the burning tea-pickers’ village, there was a low bald hill the townsfolk called Crow Hill, where nothing grew but a single dead pine. The crimson threads ran there. All of them. They gathered from the whole tide and ran up the slope of that hill like the strands of a rope coming to its knot, and at the top of the hill, by the dead pine, where the firelight from the village barely reached, they ended.

There was someone there.

A figure, standing very still. He could not see a face. He could see a long robe the colour of dried blood, and a hood, and a hand held out in front of the figure, and from that hand hung something small that caught the firelight and gave it back crimson.

A bell.

As he watched, the hand moved. The bell swung.

He did not hear it. It was too far, half a li away over the roaring of the tide. But he saw what happened. Along the thousands of crimson threads, all at once, a pulse ran, a ripple of dark light, from the hill to the beasts. And every beast below the wall shuddered as if it had been whipped and threw itself at the stone with redoubled fury, and three more sections of the palisade groaned and bent.

The hand moved again. The bell swung. The pulse ran down the threads.

“Feng,” said Gu Yan. His voice came out quiet and very calm. “Elder cousin. Look at Crow Hill.”

Gu Feng, two paces along the walkway, wiping wolf’s blood out of his eyes, looked. He saw nothing, Gu Yan knew. A dark hill, a dead tree, perhaps a shape beside it. But Gu Feng looked at Gu Yan’s face, and whatever he saw there, he did not ask how.

“Someone is doing this,” Gu Yan said.

On the hill, the hooded figure turned its head.

At half a li, in the dark, there was no way it could have picked one boy out of the defenders on the wall. Gu Yan told himself that, afterward, many times. But at that moment he felt it as certainly as he had felt the starlight in his bones: a gaze from under that hood, finding him. Cold and interested. As a man might look at a beetle that has done something unexpected on the edge of a table.

The bell swung once more, slowly. And all along the north wall, every red eye in the tide turned toward him.
