Gu Yan did not sleep the night after the lecture.

Your father stood in that same seat. He lay on his bunk and turned the words over until they wore smooth. Gu Changfeng had studied here. Gu Changfeng had sat under the bronze dome and seen the three thousand worlds hanging in the dark and asked what they hung on. And then, some years later, he had pressed a cold grey stone into his six-year-old son’s chest and walked away.

In the morning he went to Han Qiu’s courtyard and cut bamboo until his arms shook. Han Qiu watched him for an hour without a word, then said, “You’re cutting angry. Angry cuts are wide. Go to the Stele Forest. Sit in front of the Seventh Sword Stele until you stop being an idiot.”

“Elder, did you know my father?”

Han Qiu was silent for a long moment. “Everyone of my generation knew your father,” he said at last. “That is all I will say about him today. Go.”

The Dao Stele Forest lay in a high valley behind the Star Tower, reached by a path of worn steps through a stand of ancient cypress. It was not a forest of trees. It was a forest of stone.

Hundreds of steles stood among the cypresses, tall slabs of rock and jade and metal, some as high as houses, some no taller than a child. Each one had been carved, or struck, or burned, or simply touched by a master who had left part of their comprehension in it. Some bore characters, some images, some nothing at all but a single gouge. The air among them was dense and strange; walking between two steles could feel like walking between two people arguing in languages you did not speak.

Disciples sat cross-legged before the steles in ones and twos, eyes closed, faces still. Merit points were deducted by the hour, and the deacon at the gate recorded each disciple’s stele with a brush on a long scroll.

“Seventh Sword Stele,” Gu Yan told him.

The deacon raised an eyebrow. “Elder Han’s?”

“He sent me.”

“Then you’ll be bleeding by noon.” The deacon wrote his name. “Third row from the north wall. Don’t touch it.”

The Seventh Sword Stele was a slab of black iron three zhang high, scarred by a single diagonal cut that ran from its upper left corner almost to its base. The cut was not deep. It had been made, according to the bronze plaque at its foot, by the Academy’s third principal, nine hundred years ago, with a wooden sword.

Gu Yan sat down in front of it and looked.

He did not use the lines at first. He simply looked, as his grandfather had taught him to look at a finished piece of joinery before trying to understand how it was made. The cut was clean. Its edges were sharp even now. And it was patient. That was the word that came to him. It had not been made in anger, or in haste, or to prove anything. It had been made by someone who had decided, long before lifting the wooden sword, exactly where the iron would part, and had waited until the iron agreed.

Then he let the lines come.

The stele blazed. Not with light, with intent. The cut was full of it, nine centuries of it, compressed into a single stroke: thin, white, absolutely certain. It was so like what he had felt when the third node lit that for a moment he thought the stele was looking back at him.

In the Lattice, the third node pulsed in answer.

He did not try to understand. He let the stele’s intent wash through him like water through a sluice, and with each pass, the cold, white line inside him grew a little finer. The anger of the night washed out with it. He found himself thinking about his grandfather’s workshop: the smell of pine shavings, the old man’s ruined hands on a chisel, the precise, unhurried way he would mark a joint with a single stroke of charcoal and then cut to the line without ever looking at it twice.

A cut is a line chosen. And the choosing happened before the cut, not during. The stroke was only the end of a decision.

By noon he was not bleeding. By the second bell he had cut nothing, and understood a great deal, and his legs had gone numb.

He stood, stretched, and noticed for the first time a stele at the very edge of the forest, half hidden by a cypress, that no one was sitting in front of.

It was broken.

That was the first thing he saw. It stood perhaps two zhang high, but its top had sheared away at a ragged angle, as if snapped off by a giant hand, and a long crack ran down its face. The stone was a colour he had never seen: dark grey shading into a faint, cold violet, like a bruise in winter light. No characters. No images. Its surface was rough and pitted.

The bronze plaque at its foot was green with age. Nameless Stele. Recovered from beyond the Valley’s threshold, Year 3,410 of the Cang Calendar. Comprehended by none. Disciples are advised not to waste merit.

Below it, in smaller characters, someone had added: The dead stone.

Gu Yan almost walked away. His merit was nearly spent and his head ached from the iron stele. But something about the broken stone held him, the way a crooked board in a stack holds a carpenter’s eye.

He looked at it with the lines.

At first, nothing. The stone’s lines were strange, denser than any rock he knew, and they did not lie the way stone lines should, in layers and grains. They ran straight. Perfectly straight, crossing each other at precise angles.

He blinked and looked again.

A grid.

Faint, almost worn away, buried beneath the pitted surface: a grid of fine lines, crossing and recrossing, meeting at small knots of denser stone. Nodes. It was not carved. It was in the stone, the way grain is in wood, as if the stone had grown around it, or been made from it.

In his mind the Lattice blazed silver, all nine nodes at once, the three lit ones flaring so brightly that he gasped.

It was the same pattern. Not similar. The same. The same angles, the same spacing, the same proportions between lines and nodes. As if someone had taken the frame that lived inside his head and pressed it into stone.

He did not decide to touch it. His hand was simply on it.

The grid opened.

It did not unfold. It continued. Beyond the cracked surface of the stele the lines ran on into a darkness that was not inside the stone and not inside his head, but somewhere between, nine nodes becoming eighty-one, eighty-one becoming seven hundred and twenty-nine, the frame repeating and repeating outward in every direction, each cell joined to every other, an endless scaffold of silver going down and up and out—

He could not see the end of it. There was no end of it.

And it was broken. Somewhere ahead, far beyond the seven hundred and twenty-nine, the lines snapped. He saw jagged ends of silver drifting in the dark like severed tendons, a wound in the grid as wide as a sky, and through the wound something cold and enormous moved.

His mind tried to hold all of it and could not. It was like trying to drink a river. The nine nodes of his own Lattice strained, their lines singing like overtightened strings. He felt his consciousness thinning, spreading outward along the endless grid, the way ink spreads into wet paper, losing its edges, losing its shape, losing—

Gu Yan.

—losing the boy who sat on the workshop roof, losing the smell of pine, losing—

Gu Yan.

Cold. A sharp point of cold at the base of his skull, another between his shoulder blades, a third in the hollow of his left wrist. Like icicles driven into him. The cold had a shape. It was a shape he knew.

You are not a river. Come back.

The cold pulled, and he followed it, and the endless grid snapped shut like a book closing.

He was lying on his back among the cypress roots with his face wet and his ears ringing. Blood ran from his nose and his ears and, he realised, from the corners of his eyes. Su Lingxi knelt over him. Three thin silver needles stood in his body: one at his neck, one in his back, one in his wrist. Her face was white, and her lips were blue with cold, as if she had poured her own warmth into the needles.

“Don’t move,” she said. Her voice was not steady. “Don’t speak. Breathe.”

He breathed. Each breath felt like it was arriving from very far away.

“You touched the dead stone,” she said. “Everyone knows you don’t touch the dead stone. Three disciples went mad from it in the last century. One of them still lives in the Academy’s infirmary and draws squares on the walls all day.” She pulled the needle from his neck with a small, precise twist. “Your fate thread appeared.”

“What?”

“I said don’t speak.” She pulled the second needle. “I was sitting at the Ice Stele two rows over. I felt my compass go cold, so cold it burned my hand. When I opened it, the needle was pointing at you, and for the first time since I met you there was a thread. One thread. It went up.” She hesitated. “Then it started to unravel, as though something was pulling it apart from the far end. So I came and pinned you down.”

She pulled the third needle and sat back on her heels.

Gu Yan lay still and looked up through the cypress branches at the sky. A ceiling, he thought. And above the ceiling, a frame. And the frame went on forever, and somewhere in it there was a wound.

“Thank you,” he said.

“You’re welcome.” She wiped her hands on her sleeve, then, after a moment, wiped the blood from his cheek with the same sleeve, roughly, as if she were cleaning a dirty mirror. “What did you see?”

He thought about lying. He thought about his grandfather’s warning, and the Principal’s gaze sliding over him like a hand searching for a joint.

“Lines,” he said. “I see lines in things. I always have. The stele has lines in it like nothing else in this world. Straight. In a grid.” That much was true. It was not everything. “When I touched it, the grid kept going. I couldn’t find the end.”

Su Lingxi was quiet for a long time.

“That,” she said finally, “is the stupidest and most interesting thing anyone has ever told me.” She looked at the stele. “Read the plaque again.”

He sat up, slowly, his head swimming, and read it.

Recovered from beyond the Valley’s threshold.

“Which valley?” he asked.

“There’s only one valley people mean when they say the Valley in Cangdu.” Su Lingxi’s voice was low. “The records of the Observatory are full of it. Every ten years, its outer boundary weakens and the Academy and the royal family send teams inside. They bring back strange stones, strange beasts, strange metals no forge in the kingdom can melt. The stars above it are wrong. The Observatory has argued for two hundred years about whether the Valley fell from somewhere.” She touched the cold violet stone with one fingertip, carefully, and drew back. “This came from Fallen Star Valley.”

He did not sleep that night either, but for a different reason.

His meridians hurt. Not the clean pain of breaking a gate, but a deep, spreading ache, as if the endless grid had pressed its pattern into his channels and they had not yet forgotten the shape. He sat cross-legged by the window and circulated his qi slowly, gently, the way his grandfather worked a stiff joint loose with oil and patience.

Around the second hour after midnight, something changed.

The ache gathered. It drew together along his meridians, following the lines the grid had pressed into them, and converged on the eighth gate, a knot of fibres beneath the heart. He had expected to spend a month on that gate. Now he saw, with a clarity that startled him, that the grid had already shown him how it was made. He chose the line. The knot parted.

The eighth vein opened like a breath held too long and finally released. Warmth flooded his chest, steady and deep, and the ache in his channels eased all at once, as though the new vein had been the thing they were straining toward all along.

Eighth Vein.

He sat for a while afterward, listening to Mo Ji snore, feeling the new qi settle. Then he took out a sheet of paper and a stick of charcoal and drew what he had seen in the stele: the grid, the nodes, the endless repetition. He drew it until his hand cramped. Then he looked at it, and burned it in the lamp, because his grandfather had told him never to speak of the lines, and drawing was a kind of speaking.

The next morning, the deacon of the Stele Forest walked up the steps to Han Qiu’s courtyard and asked to speak with the Elder privately.

Gu Yan, cutting bamboo at the far end of the grove, could not hear what they said. But he saw the deacon’s face, pale and uneasy, and he saw Han Qiu’s head turn, slowly, until the Elder’s dark eyes rested on him.

When the deacon had gone, Han Qiu walked over and stood beside him without speaking. Gu Yan kept cutting.

“The Nameless Stele,” Han Qiu said at last. “The dead stone. The deacon says it has been cold to the touch for six hundred years. Colder than ice in summer. Disciples used to put their wine jars against it.” He paused. “This morning it is warm.”

Gu Yan’s sword stopped in the middle of a stroke.

“Warm,” said Han Qiu, “like a hand that has just been held.”
