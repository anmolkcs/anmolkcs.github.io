Once a year, on the fifteenth night of the ninth month, Cangdu forgot that it was a fortress.

The great gates of the inner city stood open. The hundred-zhang walls were hung from battlement to moat with paper lanterns, red and gold and white, so many that from the Academy’s hill the whole capital seemed to be on fire. On the Mandate-made river, lantern boats drifted in their thousands, each one a floating pavilion of lacquer and silk lit from within, and above them, held aloft by small formations bought from street vendors for three copper coins apiece, hung lanterns that floated free in the air like slow, tame stars.

“The Festival of a Thousand Lanterns,” Mo Ji announced, spreading his arms as if he had personally arranged it. “Which is a lie, by the way. My father counted once. It’s more like two hundred thousand.”

“Your father counted two hundred thousand lanterns?” Gu Lan asked.

“He was drunk. It took him all night. But he was very thorough.”

Gu Lan laughed. She had bought a rabbit lantern from the first stall they passed and was carrying it on a stick, bobbing it over the heads of the crowd. In the red light she looked younger than fifteen, and very much like the girl who used to run into the workshop in Jade Ridge with news before anyone else had heard it.

Gu Yan walked a half step behind her, watching the crowd.

He had not meant to. He had meant, for one night, to be a sixteen-year-old in a city of lanterns with his cousin and his best friend and nothing on his mind but sugared hawthorns. Su Lingxi had not come; the Observatory held rites on the festival night, and its daughters were expected to stand on the star platform until dawn reading omens for the King. She had told him this with an expression of such profound boredom that he had almost laughed.

But since the dead stone, he could not stop seeing.

The lines came easier now, without the needle of pain behind his eye. Eighth Vein, the first row of the Lattice lit, his mind stretched and somehow strengthened by the endless grid. He saw the crowd as a river of qi: the dull, steady glow of ordinary people; here and there the brighter, ordered channels of cultivators; the faint warm threads that connected parents to the children on their shoulders. It was beautiful. He could have watched it all night.

He was watching it when he saw the red thread.

It was thin, taut and sticky, exactly like the thread in the lane between the dormitories. It ran through the crowd at knee height, weaving between the legs of the festival-goers, and its end was fastened to the hem of Gu Lan’s robe.

“Lan,” he said.

She turned, smiling, rabbit lantern raised.

A man in a merchant’s grey coat stepped out of the crowd behind her, put one hand flat between her shoulder blades, and pushed.

It was not a strong push. But something flashed red between his fingers, a needle of blood-coloured qi, and Gu Lan’s eyes went wide and empty. Her lantern fell. Before it hit the ground, the man had caught her around the waist, lifted her as if she weighed nothing, and leapt.

He went straight up, onto a lantern-seller’s awning, then onto the eaves of a teahouse, and was gone over the roofline.

Gu Yan was already moving.

He did not remember climbing the teahouse. He remembered the tiles under his feet, slick with lamp oil and dew; the roofs of Cangdu spread out ahead of him like a frozen sea of grey waves, crests lit red and gold; and, forty zhang ahead, a grey coat running.

There were two others. They rose from the shadow of a chimney as the first man passed, falling in on either side of him like escorts, both in dark clothes, faces wrapped in cloth. Cultivators, both of them; he could see their channels burning.

Behind him, tiles cracked like thunder. Mo Ji landed on the roof, roaring something that might have been Gu Lan’s name or might have been a curse, and came on behind Gu Yan with all the grace of a falling anvil.

“Who are they?” Mo Ji bellowed.

“I don’t know. Keep up.”

He let the lines flood in. The roofs became a map: load-bearing beams glowing beneath the tiles, weak joints where old repairs had been made, the bracing of a bell tower, the dense steady glow of a stone parapet. The man carrying Gu Lan was fast, but he ran like someone who did not know the city, picking his path by eye. Gu Yan ran like a carpenter who had read the frame of every building he passed. He took the beams instead of the tiles. He cut corners across ridgelines that would have collapsed under anyone who stepped half a foot to the left.

He gained.

Thirty zhang. Twenty. The two escorts glanced back.

One of them peeled away and turned, planting his feet on a ridge. His hands came up, and the air in front of him darkened to the colour of old blood.

“Mo Ji!”

“I’ve got him!”

Mo Ji did not slow down. He lowered his head and his shoulders and ran straight into the dark-red haze like a bull into a gate. His Earth qi flared bronze around him, dense and heavy, and the blood haze broke against it and slid off like rain off a stone. The masked man had a heartbeat to look surprised before Mo Ji’s fist found his stomach, and the two of them went through the roof of a fabric shop in an explosion of tiles and silk.

Gu Yan did not look back.

The man in the grey coat stopped on the roof of a granary by the river, where the lantern boats drifted below like a burning field. The second escort stopped beside him. Gu Lan hung limp over his shoulder, her hair loose, her eyes open and seeing nothing.

Gu Yan landed on the granary roof ten paces from them and drew his father’s iron sword.

He saw them clearly now. The escort: Ninth Vein, all nine meridians open, his qi tinged a sullen red along the edges, as if dye had been poured into a clean river. And the man in grey: Ninth Vein too, but at its very peak, his channels swollen and overfull, and beneath them, coiled around his dantian like a sleeping snake, a knot of blood-red qi that did not belong to him. Stolen. It pulsed with a dozen different heartbeats.

“You’re quick,” the man in grey said. His voice was pleasant and ordinary, the voice of a man selling cloth. “Eighth Vein, aren’t you? Walk away, boy. We only want the girl.”

“Why?”

The man seemed amused that he had asked. “She has clean wood qi and a young body. Do you know how rare that is? Most of the girls her age in this city have been eating pills since they could walk. Their blood is sour with it.” He shifted Gu Lan on his shoulder. “Walk away. You don’t want to be drained. It’s not a pleasant way to die.”

Gu Yan said nothing. He looked at the man’s lines.

The escort moved first. He came in low and fast, a curved knife in each hand, red qi streaming from the blades like smoke. Gu Yan saw the attack before it began: the weight shifting onto the left foot, the right knife a feint, the left knife the true strike, aimed at the tendons of his sword arm. He stepped inside the feint and cut along the line where the escort’s wrist joined his forearm.

He chose the line. The iron sword went through the escort’s guard like a chisel through soft pine.

The man screamed and the left knife flew from fingers that no longer worked. Gu Yan turned his blade and struck him on the temple with the flat. The escort dropped and slid down the tiles, catching at the gutter.

The man in grey put Gu Lan down on the ridge, carefully, almost tenderly, and stepped over her.

“All right,” he said. “Let’s see.”

He was fast. Faster than anything Gu Yan had fought. The stolen blood in his dantian flared and his body seemed to blur, and suddenly he was inside Gu Yan’s guard, palm flat, the same red needle flashing between his fingers. Gu Yan twisted. The needle grazed his ribs and where it touched, his qi drained, pouring out of him through the wound like water through a cracked bowl. His knees buckled. He hit the tiles and rolled, and the man’s heel came down where his head had been and smashed through the roof.

He came up gasping. His side was numb. Three of his eight veins had gone quiet, their qi pulled out through the shallow cut.

“It’s a shame,” the man said. “Your blood would be very good. I can feel it from here. It’s like starlight.” He smiled. “I’ll take you as well.”

He came again.

Gu Yan read him. The lines were clear: right palm, the needle, a step to the left. But his body was slower now, drained, and the man was a full sub-level above him and burning stolen blood like oil on a fire. Gu Yan blocked the palm with the flat of his sword and the force of it threw him back across the ridge, three paces, four, until his heels were at the edge of the roof and there was nothing behind him but forty zhang of air and the burning river.

He’s stronger. He’s faster. He’s going to win.

In the Lattice, the second node burned.

He felt it then, as clearly as he had felt anything: the starlight he had drunk on a hundred nights, lying in his bones like ore in a mountain. He had always let it rise slowly, evenly, refining his body a little at a time. He had never simply called it.

He called it.

Starlight came up out of his bones like a flood through a broken dam. It poured into his channels and filled the drained veins and kept coming, cold and silver and fierce, until his eight meridians were brimming and the ninth gate, the last knot deep in the dantian, was hammered by a tide of light that it had never been built to withstand.

He did not wait for it to break. He chose the line.

The ninth gate opened.

It was like the top of his skull lifting off. Qi roared through all nine meridians at once, a complete circuit for the first time, a great wheel of power spinning from his dantian through every limb and back again, and on its rim the starlight sang. His senses exploded outward. He could hear the boats on the river, the crackle of every lantern on the walls, Gu Lan’s slow breath on the ridge, the man in grey’s twelve stolen heartbeats.

Ninth Vein.

The man in grey stopped, mid-step. His pleasant face changed.

Gu Yan came off the edge of the roof like an arrow off a string.

He did not cut at the man. He cut at the line between the man and the stolen blood, the faint red thread that joined the knot in his dantian to his own channels, the place where something that did not belong had been grafted on. The iron sword drew a single silver stroke through the air.

The man in grey screamed. Not in pain; in loss. Red qi burst from his body in a cloud, a dozen heartbeats fleeing like birds from a struck tree, and he staggered backward, suddenly ordinary, suddenly only a Ninth Vein cultivator with a cut across his robe and terror in his eyes.

Gu Yan raised the sword again.

The man threw something. A pellet; it burst on the tiles between them into a cloud of choking red smoke. Gu Yan cut through it and found nothing. Through the lines he saw the man fleeing across the rooftops, faster than he should have been able to, burning his own blood now to run. He could have chased him. He looked at Gu Lan lying on the ridge with her eyes open and empty, and did not.

She woke a quarter of an hour later, in Mo Ji’s arms, on the steps of the granary, surrounded by city guards with spears and lanterns. Mo Ji had arrived limping, his robe torn, dragging the first escort behind him like a sack of grain. The second, the one with the cut wrist, had disappeared from the gutter during the fight.

“Yan?” Gu Lan whispered. “There was a man. He pushed me.”

“I know.” He took her hand. It was cold. “You’re safe.”

She cried then, quietly, and Mo Ji held her and looked over her head at Gu Yan with an expression that said: we’re going to talk about this.

The guard captain was a tired-looking woman in lacquered armour who had clearly not planned to spend the festival night on a granary roof. She listened to their story, sent runners to the Academy and the city magistrate, and then crouched by the place where the man in grey had stood.

“Something here,” she said.

It was a dagger, lying in a gap between two tiles where it must have fallen when the stolen blood burst out of him. Short, straight, the blade dark grey with a faint rippled pattern, the hilt wrapped in black cord. The captain turned it in her lantern light. On the base of the blade, small and neat, was a maker’s mark: a deer standing beneath a cloud.

Mo Ji went very still.

“I know that mark,” he said. His voice was flat. “Every blacksmith in Cangdu knows that mark. That’s the Northgate forge.” He looked at Gu Yan. “The Lu clan’s forge.”

The captain’s face closed like a door. She wrapped the dagger in a cloth and tucked it into her armour.

“The Lu clan’s Northgate forge,” she said, “makes four thousand blades a year, young man. It sells them to every armoury and every market stall from here to the western border. I have one in my own kitchen.” She stood. “This is a weapon. It’s not a name.”

She was right. Gu Yan knew she was right. A dagger dropped on a roof could be a mistake, or a clue, or a gift left on purpose by someone who wanted the city to look at the Lu clan. Lines that led to a wall were not a confession, and a knife was not a signature.

And yet.

He looked across the burning river toward the inner city, where the great ducal houses stood on their hill behind walls of white plaster, and among their gates hung a lacquered board with two characters in gold.

The next morning, there was a knock at the dormitory door before the first bell. Mo Ji opened it, scowling, and the scowl fell off his face.

Two royal guards stood in the corridor in armour of deep blue, the dragon of the Cang royal house worked in silver on their breastplates. The elder of them bowed, very correctly, to Gu Yan.

“Disciple Gu Yan,” he said. “His Highness the Third Prince requests the pleasure of your company.”
