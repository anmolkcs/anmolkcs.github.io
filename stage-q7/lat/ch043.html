The Grand Tournament of the Cang Kingdom was held, as it had been held for eight hundred years, in the Arena of Ten Thousand Steps.

It lay a few li north of Cangdu’s walls, in a bowl of hills that the first King had pressed flat with his Mandate when the kingdom was young. Its tiers climbed the hillsides in rings of grey stone, ten thousand of them, so many that a spectator in the topmost row saw the fighting platforms below as a scatter of coins on a tabletop. On the morning the Tournament opened, every one of those steps was full. Gu Yan, walking out through the entrants’ tunnel with the rest of the Starfall contingent, heard the crowd before he saw it — a sound like surf, or like rain on a vast roof — and then he came out into the light and saw it, and stopped walking for a moment without meaning to.

Mo Ji walked into his back. “Move, you’re blocking—” Then Mo Ji saw it too, and stopped as well.

There were more people in the arena than lived in all of Jade Ridge and the three towns around it. Ten times more. Banners of every clan and prefecture snapped above the tiers. Hawkers moved along the aisles with baskets of roasted chestnuts and sugared lotus root. On the floor of the bowl, a hundred square fighting platforms of white jade stood in ten rows of ten, each one ringed with a shimmering wall of formation-light, and around them, filling every gap, stood the entrants: ten thousand young cultivators in the colours of their clans and schools, all under twenty-five, every one of them the best their family or town or regiment had to offer.

High above it all, the Taixu ship hung against the morning sky. Its shadow lay across the northern tiers, and none of the people sitting in that shade seemed to mind.

“Ten thousand,” said Gu Lan faintly.

“Nine thousand eight hundred and forty-one,” said Su Lingxi, who had been reading the registration lists. “Some withdrew when they saw the envoy’s ship.”

“That’s still a lot of people to beat.”

“You don’t have to beat all of them. Only the ones in front of you.”

The rules were simple, and the herald announced them from the royal pavilion in a voice amplified by formation until it rolled across the bowl like thunder.

The first three days were preliminaries. Each entrant would fight on whichever platform the lots assigned, against whoever the lots assigned. A fighter was eliminated after two losses. Victory came by surrender, by being forced from the platform, or by the judge’s call when one fighter could no longer continue. Killing was forbidden. Crippling was forbidden. The use of poisons, demonic arts, and arts that harmed the root of cultivation was forbidden on pain of death, and the King himself would watch.

At the end of the preliminaries, a hundred and twenty-eight would remain. They would be placed in a bracket, and from that point, a single loss meant the end.

Gu Yan drew his first lot from a jade urn: Platform Sixty-Three, Third Bell. He went to find it.

His name was posted beside the platform on a wooden board, as every entrant’s was, with his school and his realm and the number of his stars. People were reading it as he approached. He watched their faces change: first the flicker of interest at Starfall Academy, Mid Star Sea, then the confusion at Stars: 0, then something that was usually pity and occasionally amusement.

“Is that a mistake?” one of them said. “How can you be Star Sea with no stars?”

“It happens,” said someone else knowledgeably. “Deformed Star Sea. My uncle’s neighbour’s son had it. You open the sea but nothing lights. They say it’s like being born blind.”

“Then how is he Mid Star Sea?”

“Pills, probably. His family must be rich.”

Gu Yan, whose family’s entire wealth was a carpentry workshop with a recently mended roof, said nothing and went to stand by the platform steps.

His first opponent was a spearman from a mercenary company in the southern prefectures, a lean man of twenty-three at Early Star Sea with three stars, who looked at Gu Yan’s board, looked at Gu Yan, and visibly relaxed.

The judge called the start. The spearman came in fast and low, his spear-tip weaving in a pattern meant to dazzle — left, right, high, a feint toward the knee — and Gu Yan watched the lines of it and saw that every flourish began from the same anchoring motion of the man’s back hip. He waited for the fourth repetition, stepped inside the spear’s arc as the hip turned, and laid the flat of his iron sword against the man’s wrist.

The spear went skidding across the jade. The spearman stared at his empty hand.

“I surrender,” he said, after a moment, sounding more puzzled than anything.

The second fight, that afternoon, was a girl from a small sect in the western hills who fought with a pair of iron fans and filled the air around the platform with a fine, stinging mist of qi. Gu Yan’s eyes watered. He could barely see her through it. But the mist had lines too — currents, eddies, the paths her fans drove it along — and in the centre of each eddy was the fan that made it. He stopped trying to see her and cut toward the stillness at the heart of the swirl. His sword met steel. One fan clattered away. She retreated across the platform, blinking, and the judge called it when Gu Yan’s blade stopped a finger from her collarbone.

“That’s not a deformed Star Sea,” she said, as they bowed. She sounded aggrieved. “Whatever you have, that’s not it.”

“I’m told it’s rare,” said Gu Yan.

The third, on the morning of the second day, was a broad, solemn young man from a farming prefecture in the north who fought with an iron hoe. Gu Yan nearly laughed when he saw it, and then stopped wanting to laugh at the first exchange. The hoe was a real weapon in those hands, heavy and patient, its blade swinging in short chopping arcs that broke the jade where they landed. The man had cleared fields with it, Gu Yan thought; every line of his body was the line of someone who had swung that same tool ten thousand times at stubborn earth. There was no flourish to read and no feint to wait for. In the end Gu Yan won the way his grandfather planed a warped board — patiently, a shaving at a time, taking the edge off each swing until the farmer’s arms were too tired to lift the hoe above his shoulder. The judge called it. The young man bowed very low, and said, “Thank you for the lesson,” and Gu Yan found he meant it when he bowed back.

He fought five times in three days and won all five.

He did it, as far as he could manage, quietly. He used only Flowing Cloud Sword, the Mortal-grade art his grandfather had given him in Jade Ridge, which every academy sword-disciple had seen a hundred times and no one would find remarkable. He did not use sword intent beyond a flicker. He did not use the step through space; Han Qiu’s warning had lodged in him like a splinter, and besides, he had no need. He kept his qi to what a Mid Star Sea cultivator with very few stars might plausibly have, and let his body — his starbone body, which no Mirror in the kingdom had been made to measure — do the rest.

It was harder than winning outright. Twice he took blows he could have avoided, because avoiding them would have meant moving in ways no ordinary fighter could. On the second evening he went back to the dormitory with a bruise across his ribs the size of a dinner plate and lay on the roof under the stars and let them sink into the damaged bone, wincing, while Mo Ji sat on the tiles beside him eating plums and narrating his own fights.

“—and then the big one, the one from the salt mines, he tried to headbutt me, can you believe it, and I thought, very well, if that’s what we’re doing—”

“You headbutted him back.”

“I headbutted him back! Four stars against two! He went down like a sack.” Mo Ji spat a plum stone over the eaves. “Gu Lan won both of hers. Gu Feng lost once, to a girl from the Zhao clan, but he won the other three. Su Lingxi hasn’t even been touched. And Cang Yi—” He shook his head. “I watched one of his. The other fellow was Late Star Sea, five stars, from the Northern Garrison. Cang Yi didn’t move his feet. Not once. He just stood there, and the other fellow kept attacking, and every time, the spear was already where he was going to be. In the end the man just put down his sword and walked off the platform.”

“He’s better than he was,” said Gu Yan, thinking of the exhibition bout in the academy arena, and a spear-tip resting against his throat.

“Everyone’s better than they were.” Mo Ji’s voice changed. “Have you seen the Lu clan’s fights?”

Gu Yan turned his head.

He went to see for himself the next morning, the last day of the preliminaries.

The Lu clan had entered thirty-one of its young people. That alone was strange — most great clans entered five or six of their best and kept the rest at home, rather than risk them being humiliated in public. The Lu clan had sent cousins of cousins, steward’s sons, a boy of sixteen who was barely into the Fifth Vein and who lost his first fight in about the time it took to bow. As though the clan had wanted to show the kingdom that it had nothing to hide, and nothing to be ashamed of, and was not afraid of being seen.

Most of them lost, sooner or later, in ordinary ways.

One did not.

His name was Lu Kang. He was twenty, a grandson of the clan’s Third Elder, at Late Star Sea with five stars — a good talent, by the kingdom’s measure, though no one had spoken of him much before. He fought with his bare hands in a style the board called Crimson Tide Palm, Earth-grade low, a Lu family art. He had won six fights in three days without losing. And every one of the six opponents he had beaten had left the platform pale.

Not injured, exactly. They walked off on their own feet. But they walked slowly, and their faces were the colour of tallow, and the one Gu Yan watched him beat that morning — a sturdy young woman from a river-patrol family, Mid Star Sea, four stars — sat down on the steps of the platform afterwards with her head between her knees and did not get up for some time.

Gu Yan watched the whole fight from the rail of the next platform, and he watched it in the way only he could.

Lu Kang’s palms struck, and where they struck, his qi went into the woman’s body in a red-tinged wave, as it should for a fire-leaning art. That was ordinary. But when his palm withdrew, something came back with it. A thin thread, so fine it was almost not there — a hair of the woman’s own qi and, twisted around it, something darker and wetter that the lines showed him as a faint rusty smear. It ran from her body into Lu Kang’s palm, and from his palm up into his arm, and then it vanished inside him, behind a knot of lines in his chest that were folded so tightly that Gu Yan could not see where they led.

The judge noticed nothing. The woman’s own teacher, waving a fan over her on the steps, noticed nothing. It was too small, too quick, and too well hidden.

But Gu Yan had seen something like it before. He had seen it in the drained carcass of a beast on the edge of the Black Pine Forest, nearly two years ago, and in the body of a dead disciple in the outer court of Starfall Academy, and in the great red-black heart of the blood formation in the middle zone of Fallen Star Valley, beating like a sick animal while Mo Ji hung in its chains.

He made himself walk away before anyone noticed him staring.

That evening, a hundred and twenty-eight names went up on the great bracket board in the royal pavilion, glowing gold against black stone, arranged in sixty-four pairs that would become thirty-two, then sixteen, and onward toward the one name at the top.

All of the Starfall group who had gone to the Valley were there. Gu Lan was there too, to her own visible astonishment, in a far corner of the lower bracket; Gu Feng had squeezed through with his single loss. Su Lingxi and Cang Yi were on the opposite half of the board from Gu Yan, and from each other. Lu Mingyuan, Peak Star Sea now according to his board, sat at the head of a section in the upper half, three rounds away from Gu Yan’s.

Gu Yan read along the line from his own name to the name beside it, the opponent he would face when the bracket opened tomorrow morning.

Lu Kang. Lu clan. Late Star Sea. Stars: 5.

He stood looking at it for a while, with the crowd jostling around him and the chestnut-sellers calling, and above him the ship of the Taixu Holy Land turning its slow, enormous fans against the evening stars.

Then he went to find Han Qiu.
