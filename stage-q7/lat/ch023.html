The Hall of Ten Thousand Stars was built into the side of the mountain like a cupped hand. Its tiers of stone benches descended in rings toward a small round dais, and above them rose a dome of dark bronze, so vast that the lamps along its rim looked like a necklace of sparks.

Four hundred new disciples filled the lower tiers. Behind them sat several hundred older disciples who had heard the lecture before and come anyway, and in the highest ring, a row of elders. Han Qiu was among them, arms folded, eyes closed, as if he intended to sleep.

Su Lingxi, on Gu Yan’s other side, said nothing. Her compass sat open on her knee. She had been unusually quiet all morning.

The lamps went out.

There was no signal. In the dark, a single old man was standing on the dais, though no one had seen him walk there.

Principal Wen Taiyuan looked like what he had been for most of his life: a scholar. He was thin and stooped, with a white beard bound in a plain cord and a robe of undyed linen. His hands were ink-stained. If Gu Yan had passed him in the market at Jade Ridge, he would have taken him for a village schoolmaster.

Then the old man spoke, and the air in the hall changed.

“Good morning,” he said, in an ordinary voice that nevertheless reached every seat. “I am going to show you where you are.”

He lifted one ink-stained hand.

The bronze dome opened.

It did not slide or fold. It simply ceased to be there, and in its place was sky: the full, cold, enormous blue of a mountain morning, so sudden and so wide that several disciples cried out and grabbed the bench in front of them. Wind poured into the hall, smelling of snow. Gu Yan felt the pressure behind it. It was not qi, exactly, not the way Han Qiu’s presence was qi. It was law. In the space of this hall, the old man’s will had become the rule of the world, and the world had obeyed. A Mandate domain.

“Hold on to your benches,” the Principal said mildly. “We are going up.”

The hall did not move. The sky moved. Or rather, the view did, as though the whole amphitheatre had become an eye and that eye was rising. The Academy fell away below them. The Star Tower became a needle, then a pin. Cangdu spread out, its hundred-zhang walls a grey ring, its Mandate-made river a silver thread, its thousand lanterns invisible in daylight. Then the capital itself shrank into a smudge on a green plain.

“This,” said the Principal, “is the Cang Kingdom.”

Gu Yan saw the Black Pine Forest to the south-east, a dark stain, and somewhere at its edge, too small to see, Jade Ridge Town, his grandfather’s workshop, the roof where he had lain night after night drinking starlight. The whole of his life before this autumn could have been covered by the Principal’s thumbnail.

“Eleven million people. Perhaps forty cultivators at Heaven Seal. Two at Mandate: myself and the King.” The view kept rising. “You came here thinking it was large. Most of you have never seen its edges.”

The Cang Kingdom became a patch. Around it, other patches appeared: kingdoms green and brown and grey, separated by mountain spines and wide rivers and dark blots of wilderness.

“The Eastern Wastes,” the Principal said. “One hundred and eighty-one kingdoms at present count. The number changes. Kingdoms are founded and fall. Some of them are ten times our size. Some are ruled by beasts. Over all of them, in the far north, sits the Taixu Holy Land, whose Master is a Sovereign of the third step.” He paused. “The Holy Land does not rule the Eastern Wastes. It does not need to. When it speaks, kingdoms listen.”

Still the view rose. The Eastern Wastes, a sprawl so vast that the Cang Kingdom was now invisible, revealed its own edges: an ocean to the east, gold with morning, and to the west a great range of mountains whose peaks pierced the clouds.

Beyond the mountains, the land continued.

“The Western Desolation,” said the Principal. “The Southern Frontier. The Northern Expanse.” Each name came with a sweep of his hand, and with each sweep, a new immensity unrolled on the sky: deserts like burnt bronze, forests so dense they were black, ice fields white as bone. “Four regions. Together, one continent. The Primal Continent. The Eastern Wastes is a quarter of it. The Cang Kingdom is one of a hundred and eighty-one kingdoms in a quarter of it.”

The view rose again, and the continent began to curve.

Gu Yan felt it in his stomach before he understood it with his eyes. The land bent away at its edges, gently, like the rim of a bowl turned upside down. Oceans appeared beyond the continent, deep blue and endless, and in those oceans, other landmasses: two, three, five, some as large as the Primal Continent, some broken into chains of islands.

“Azure Dust World,” said the Principal softly. “Our world. Seven continents. Uncounted seas. A billion lives, perhaps ten billion; no one has ever counted. Most of the people in this world will live and die without ever leaving the kingdom of their birth. Most of the cultivators will never leave their continent. And all of it, every sea, every mountain, every city and sect and Holy Land, lies beneath this.”

He raised his hand once more and pointed straight up.

The sky above the curve of the world was not empty.

At first Gu Yan thought it was cloud, very high, very faint. Then he saw that it did not move. It was a shimmer, a skin, a faint pale iridescence like the inside of a shell, stretched across the whole of the heavens. Through it, the stars showed, but blurred, as if seen through ice.

“The World Membrane,” said the Principal. “The sky is not endless. It is a ceiling.”

Gu Yan stopped breathing.

The sky is a ceiling. His grandfather had said those words on the workshop step in Jade Ridge, looking up with his crippled hands folded in his lap, and Gu Yan had thought it was an old man’s bitterness. It had not been bitterness. It had been knowledge.

“No one knows what made it,” the Principal went on. “The oldest texts say it has always been there. It is not a wall in the way a city wall is a wall. You could fly toward it for a year and never touch it. But nothing passes through. No cultivator of this world has ever flown out. No light from outside comes in except the starlight, and the starlight comes in changed.” He lowered his hand. “There is one exception.”

The view turned, as though the great eye of the hall had shifted its gaze. Far across the curve of the world, beyond the seas, at the heart of the greatest continent of all, Gu Yan saw a thread.

It was so thin at this distance that it might have been a hair on a lens. It rose from the land, straight and silver-white, through the clouds, through the high pale sky, all the way up to the Membrane. And where it touched the Membrane, the Membrane brightened, as if a needle had been pressed through silk.

“The Sky Pillar,” said the Principal. “It stands at the centre of Azure Dust World, on the Central Continent, which none of you will see for a very long time, if ever. Its base is a thousand li across. Its height no one has measured. It is the only road out of our world.”

The hall was utterly silent.

“Only a Sovereign may climb it. Not because there is a law against it. Because anyone weaker is crushed by the pressure of the Pillar before they have climbed a hundred zhang. A Sovereign who reaches its summit faces the Ascension Tribulation, heavenly lightning that strips them down to their foundation and asks whether there is anything left. Most die. Those who survive pass through the Membrane.” He let the words settle. “In the last three thousand years, from the whole of the Eastern Wastes, nine have ascended.”

Nine. In three thousand years. From a hundred and eighty kingdoms.

“I stood once on the western shore of our continent,” said the Principal, “when I was a much younger man, and looked at the Sky Pillar across ten thousand li of sea. It looked exactly as it looks now. A thread. I travelled toward it for forty days and it never grew larger. Then I turned around and came home and became a teacher, because I understood that I would never reach it.” He said this without regret, like a man reporting the weather. “Now. Where do they go, the nine?”

He closed his hand, and the view of Azure Dust World shrank.

It shrank the way the Cang Kingdom had shrunk, the way the continent had shrunk, but faster now, and the shrinking did not stop. The world became a sphere, then a bead, a pale blue bead wrapped in its shimmering skin, hanging in a darkness without stars. And then, around it, other beads appeared.

Dozens. Hundreds. They hung in the dark like lanterns at a festival, each one wrapped in its own faint membrane, some blue, some red, some gold, some dark as iron. The dark between them went on and on.

“There are, according to every text that has survived, three thousand Lower Worlds,” said the Principal. “Ours is one. Most are like ours. Some are dead. Some are ruled by beasts, or by demons, or by things for which we have no names. They do not know about us. We barely know about them. We know their number only because those who ascended once sent word back down, long ago, before the ways between grew so hard to travel.”

The three thousand lanterns hung in the dark, and above them the darkness brightened.

“Above the Lower Worlds,” said the Principal, “lie the Celestial Domains.”

He did not show them. Perhaps he could not. What appeared above the hanging worlds was only light, a vast diffuse glow like dawn behind a mountain, so wide that the three thousand worlds beneath it looked like dust motes in a sunbeam.

“One hundred and eight Domains. Each larger than a hundred worlds like ours. The cultivators who live there do not use spiritual qi. They use immortal essence, which is to spiritual qi as the sea is to a cup of water. Their lowest are Earth Immortals. Above them, Heaven Immortals. Above them, Golden Immortals, whose names the ascended texts do not dare to write in full. The nine of our region who climbed the Sky Pillar arrived at the lowest edge of the lowest Domain and were, so the texts say, considered promising children.”

A sound went through the hall, half laugh, half groan. Gu Yan did not make it. He was staring at the light.

“And above the Celestial Domains?” a voice asked from the front. Bold, clear. Gu Yan recognised the Third Prince, sitting alone in the first row.

The Principal smiled, faintly. “Above the Celestial Domains, the texts grow uncertain. They speak of the Thirty-Three Heavens, stacked one above another like the floors of a tower, each one a realm of law the size of a continent of continents, where True Gods live. Whether the texts are true, I cannot tell you. The ascended who sent word back had never seen them. They had only heard of them, the way a village child hears of the capital.” He spread his hands, and the light above the worlds faded. “And above that, the texts are silent. Not uncertain. Silent. As if whoever wrote them had come to a place where there were no words.”

The view of the dark and the hanging worlds held a moment longer, then rushed downward, and the Membrane, and the curve of the world, and the continent, and the Eastern Wastes, and the Cang Kingdom, and the capital, and the Academy, and the hall, until they were sitting under a bronze dome again with the lamps lit, as though nothing had happened at all.

Someone in the third row was crying, quietly.

“That,” said Principal Wen Taiyuan, “is where you are. I show it to every new season. Some of you will now feel very small. That is correct; you are. Some of you will now feel that nothing you do matters. That is incorrect. Every one of the nine sat on a bench like yours once, in some hall like this one, and felt small.” He folded his ink-stained hands. “I will take questions.”

There were many questions, and the Principal answered them all patiently. Lu Mingyuan, from the inner court benches, asked with polished courtesy what the shortest path to Sovereign was for a cultivator of the Eastern Wastes, and the Principal said, “Join the Taixu Holy Land, and be very lucky, and live a very long time,” which drew laughter. Cang Yi asked why the Membrane existed at all.

“If you find out,” the Principal said, “come and tell me.”

Gu Yan’s hand went up before he had decided to raise it.

The Principal’s eyes found him across the tiers. “Yes. The young man from Jade Ridge.”

Gu Yan stood. His mouth was dry. Beside him, Su Lingxi had gone very still, and he felt rather than saw her fingers close over her compass.

“Principal,” he said. “If the worlds hang one above another, then something must hold them. A lantern hangs from a cord. A roof hangs on a frame. What do the worlds hang on?” He hesitated, then said it anyway. “Are there lines between them?”

The hall did not go quiet, because it was already quiet. But something in the quality of the silence changed.

The Principal did not answer at once. He looked at Gu Yan, and for the first time that morning his gaze was not a teacher’s gaze. It was sharp, focused, the gaze of a Mandate cultivator who had just noticed something in his domain that he had not put there. Gu Yan felt it pass over him like a hand over a carved panel, searching for the joint.

In his mind, the Lattice went dark. He did not know how he did it. He simply pulled every thread of attention away from the nine nodes and held them still, the way you hold your breath when someone walks past your hiding place.

The gaze lingered. Then it withdrew.

“That is a carpenter’s question,” the Principal said lightly. “There are old texts, a few, that speak of a frame beneath the heavens. Most scholars consider them heresy. The Academy does not keep them.” He smiled. “Everything hangs on something, young man. That is a good thing to remember. It is also a good thing not to say too loudly. Sit down. Next question.”

Gu Yan sat. His heart was beating so hard that he could feel it in his teeth.

When the lecture ended and the disciples flooded out into the afternoon, talking all at once, a deacon touched his sleeve at the door.

“The Principal asks for a moment of your time.”

Mo Ji and Gu Lan looked at him. Su Lingxi looked at the deacon. Gu Yan nodded and followed him back down the tiers to the dais, where the old man stood alone, gazing up at the bronze dome as though he could still see through it.

“Gu Yan,” said the Principal, without turning. “Grandson of Gu Shouyi, who held the Heaven Seal before his injury. Recorded disciple of Han Qiu, who does not take disciples.” He turned. “Where did you hear the phrase lines between worlds?”

“Nowhere, Principal. I’m a carpenter’s grandson. Everything I’ve ever made hung on something.”

“A carpenter’s answer,” the old man said. It was impossible to tell whether he believed it. He was silent for a while. “Questions that are too precise attract answers, Gu Yan. Not always from teachers. Be careful whom you ask.”

“I will, Principal.”

He bowed and turned to go. He was at the edge of the dais when the old man spoke again, so quietly that it might have been to himself.

“Your father stood in that same seat, twenty years ago, and asked me nearly the same question.”

Gu Yan stopped.

When he turned around, the dais was empty.
