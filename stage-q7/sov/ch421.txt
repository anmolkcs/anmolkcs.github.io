<p>For two days after Sub-Bailiff Zhao Kuan's riders disappeared down the southern gorge, the smithy at the heart of Ash-Brook Village stood cold and silent.</p>
<p>Inside the stone lean-to, Old Tie Shan lay propped at a forty-five-degree angle against sacks of charcoal dust so his broken right collarbone wouldn't shift while he slept. Even in restless fever-dozes, the old blacksmith's single right hand twitched beneath its tight hemp sling, his fingers curling around an invisible pair of tongs while he muttered his son's name: <em>"Niu'er... keep your head down in Drift Four... your old man is hammering as fast as he can..."</em></p>
<p>Outside under the shed eaves, the winter wind drove grey sleet across the cold stone anvil. Every time a villager walked past with a bucket of water or an armful of brushwood, their eyes lingered with quiet dread on the dark hearth. In a frontier settlement under the Black-Briar Barren Ridge, the blacksmith's forge was the beating heart of survival: without it, dulled axes could not cut iron-pine firewood, broken arrowheads could not be re-tipped for the winter hunt, and most terrifying of all, not a single ounce of the <strong>eight hundred jin of Grade-One Refined Cold-Slag Iron</strong> demanded by Pit Overseer Luo Shan could be smelted before the twentieth day of the twelfth month.</p>
<p>Yet on the morning of the eighteenth day of the tenth month, before the first cock crowed, the sound of sawing oak timber and the <em>clink</em> of a cold chisel woke Old Tie Shan from his stupor.</p>
<p>Gritting his teeth against the stabbing pain in his shoulder, the old man pushed open the plank door of the lean-to with his boot and peered out into the torchlit shed.</p>
<p>Lu Chen was kneeling beside the stone anvil block, his bare upper torso wrapped in fresh black herb-crust and his left forearm protected by Widow Qin's hyena-hide bracer. Around the base of the anvil, he had dug two narrow trenches into the packed cinders and anchored a heavy, hinged A-frame of seasoned char-oak timber connected by a twisted rawhide cable to a pair of long-jawed iron tongs mounted directly over the edge of the anvil face.</p>
<p>"Boy... what in the Mountain God's name have you built onto my anvil?" Old Tie Shan rasped, shuffling out with a sheepskin cloak draped over his splinted shoulder.</p>
<p>Lu Chen drove a final iron pin through the oak hinge, stood up, and placed his left foot onto a broad wooden treadle-lever projecting from the base of the anvil block. When he pressed his heel down with two hundred jin of force, the rawhide cable pulled a sliding iron ring up the handles of the mounted tongs, clamping their serrated jaws shut over a twenty-pound block of pig-iron with a vise-like grip that did not budge a hair's breadth! When he lifted his heel, a bent ash-wood spring-pole overhead snapped the tongs open again.</p>
<p>"A striker needs two hands on the forty-pound sledge to drive the cold-shatter slag out of the iron," Lu Chen explained, wiping coal dust from his brow with the back of his wrist. "With your right arm in a sling, nobody in the village has the wrist strength to hold a glowing bloom steady on the anvil against a full-power sledge blow. So I built a <strong>Foot-Treadle Spring Vise</strong>. My left heel holds the iron down on the anvil face; my left hand turns the bar between blows; and when the bar is locked, both hands swing the forty-pound sledge."</p>
<p>Old Tie Shan stared at the oak treadle, the rawhide pulley, and the spring-pole overhead. It was a simple, rugged mechanical linkage derived from antique water-trip hammers Lu Chen had once studied in provincial craftsmanship treatises on Earth—constructed entirely out of scrap timber, braided ox-hide, and three spare iron pins.</p>
<p>"You... you built a third arm out of oak and hide," Old Tie Shan whispered, his single eye glistening in the torchlight. "What about the bellows? Even with a foot-vise, you can't pull the air-lever and watch the color of the fire at the same time!"</p>
<p>"He doesn't have to, Uncle Tie!"</p>
<p>A breathless young voice called out from the shed doorway as fourteen-year-old <strong>Shi Tou</strong> trotted in out of the snow, his cheeks bright red and a thick padded leather vest strapped over his shoulders—followed closely by <strong>Er Gou</strong> and two other thirteen-year-old village boys carrying baskets of crushed charcoal.</p>
<p>"Grandpa gave us leave from the snare-lines for the whole month!" Shi Tou said proudly, grabbing the worn ash-wood handle of the ox-hide bellows with both hands and planting his wedged boots in the cinder grooves just the way Lu Chen had taught him. "The four of us are taking two-hour shifts on the bellows lever! And Uncle Tie—you just sit right there on that backed log stool by the hearth with a pine stick in your teeth and yell when the iron turns the right shade of yellow!"</p>
