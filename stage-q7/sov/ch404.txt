<p>That night, the wind howling down from the Black-Briar Barren Ridge shook the slate tiles of the lean-to like rattling bones.</p>
<p>As the caustic heat of the Ash-Root and Rock-Adder Gall worked through his torn capillaries, a high, shivering fever settled over Lu Chen. Yet even in the grip of mortal fever, the core of his mind remained anchored by the cracked gray slate of the Chrono-Soul Prism. He did not rave or thrash on the straw; he lay flat on his back with the rusted three-inch spear collar pressed against his breastbone, watching the boundary between waking pain and memory blur in the dark.</p>
<p>In the dream, he was fifteen years old again in the back room of the six-table tavern in Cloudstride City, listening to the rain drum on the alley tiles while his father Lu Shan sharpened a cleaver on a wet creek-stone and his mother Meng Wan hummed a folk tune by the stove. Then the dream shifted: the wooden tavern walls became the crystal barrier of Warm-Spring Valley, and his mother was standing on the other side of nine layers of petrified gray time, holding out a steaming bowl of longevity noodles with two golden poached eggs on top—her lips parted in a silent cry that would not finish for ten thousand years.</p>
<p>Lu Chen opened his eyes in the freezing dark of the lean-to.</p>
<p>His face was wet, though the moisture froze almost instantly in the draft seeping through the chinks of the mud wall. For three hundred years as an Empyrean and Primordial Sovereign, he had forgotten what helplessness tasted like. Now he tasted it in every gritty breath: the knowledge that he was separated from his parents, from Su Qingxue, and from Su Wan'er not merely by distance, but by an entire dimensional order where he could not even lift his own body off a pile of rotten straw without his ribs screaming.</p>
<p><em>Grief that stays in the throat only rots the lungs,</em> Lu Chen told himself silently, wiping his cheek with the back of his torn sleeve. <em>Father practiced his horse stance with an old leg wound for twenty years without complaining once. Qingxue waited two hundred and seventy years inside an ice lotus before she ever felt the sun on her skin. All I have to do is stand up.</em></p>
<p>An hour before dawn, while the sky outside was still the black of cold soot, Lu Chen began the process of standing.</p>
<p>Under the tenfold gravity of the Great Desolate Origin Universe, a crippled mortal could not simply push off the floor with brute force; doing so would rip his unhealed intercostal muscles and reopen the cracks in his seventh, eighth, and ninth left ribs.</p>
<p>Using <strong>Prism Stillness</strong> to monitor the tension across every muscle group within his three-pace perception, Lu Chen first rolled onto his uninjured right side, tucking his chin to keep his spinal alignment straight. He slid his right knee up until his shin took thirty percent of his torso's weight, braced his right palm flat against the rough stone wall at a forty-five-degree angle—the exact load-bearing vector he had once used to prop up cracked bronze tripods in museum restoration—and exhaled slowly as he drove through his right heel.</p>
<p><em>Creak... pop.</em></p>
<p>Sweat poured down his spine and soaked into his hemp waist-cord. His thighs trembled violently as the full weight of his starved frame—magnifying tenfold under the frontier's Origin-Gravity—settled onto his kneecaps and ankles. The soles of his bare feet felt as if they were sinking into cold iron. For ten breaths he stood hunched with one shoulder against the mud wall, waiting for the black dizziness in his ears to recede.</p>
<p>Then he took one step toward the plank door. Then a second.</p>
<p>By the time he pulled the wooden latch aside and stepped out under the eaves of the open-sided smithy shed, he had walked seven paces, and every muscle in his calves was knotted hard as dry rope.</p>
<p>At the center of the shed, Old Tie Shan was already kneeling on one knee before the stone forge-hearth, holding a bundle of dry pine-shavings with his iron tongs while blowing on a buried coal ember until his gray beard glowed orange. Beside the hearth sat a small black iron pot, from which rose the thin, earthy smell of boiled coarse yellow millet mixed with chopped wild bitter-greens.</p>
<p>Hearing the scrape of bare feet on the frozen cinder floor, Old Tie Shan paused and looked over his shoulder. Seeing Lu Chen standing upright against the timber post—pale as bone, barefoot in the frost, yet holding his spine straight without leaning on a crutch—the old blacksmith blinked his good eye in genuine surprise.</p>
<p>"I thought I'd have to drag you out by the heels at noon," Old Tie Shan grunted, reaching behind the hearth to toss a pair of worn, straw-stuffed rawhide sandals across the dirt. "Put those on before the cinder-frost takes your toes. A striker with no toes can't brace his heel when the hammer comes down."</p>
<p>Lu Chen bent slowly at the hips, keeping his cracked left ribs immobilized, tied the stiff rawhide sandals onto his feet, and bowed slightly. "Thank you, Master Tie."</p>
<p>"Don't call me Master," the old man muttered, ladling a full wooden bowl of thick, gritty yellow millet gruel from the iron pot and shoving it into Lu Chen's hands. "Everyone in Ash-Brook calls me Old Tie. Eat every grain, including the husks at the bottom. Once that bowl is clean, we'll see if your arms have enough marrow left to pull the ox-hide bellows."</p>
