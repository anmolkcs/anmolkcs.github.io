<p>By the fifth morning after waking in the lean-to, the three hairline cracks in Lu Chen's left ribs had knitted enough under a plaster of Granny Liu's bone-knotting mud that he could draw a full breath without tasting blood.</p>
<p>His palms, wrapped in fresh linen over layers of torn blisters and hardening yellow calluses, had pulled the ox-hide bellows for four straight days, helping Old Tie Shan test the diagonal slag-expulsion method on small hand-chisels. Every single chisel had come out of the quench tank ringing with a clear, high note, free of internal cold-shatter veins.</p>
<p>Yet to forge the remaining twenty-two heavy tribute mattocks before the fifteenth of the month, the smithy was running dangerously low on both high-grade <strong>Black-Slag Ore</strong> and <strong>Bitter-Pine Charcoal</strong>.</p>
<p>"The best ore isn't in the lower creek," Old Tie Shan told Lu Chen over a dawn breakfast of salted turnip soup and millet cakes. "It breaks out of the red shale cliff halfway up <strong>Frost-Tooth Gully</strong>, two miles up the ridge. Usually <strong>Shi Tou</strong> and two of the village lads haul a basket down every afternoon before dark, but young <strong>Er Gou</strong> twisted his knee in a frost-rut yesterday. Your legs have got some meat back on them, Lu Chen. Go up the gully with Shi Tou this morning. Don't try to carry a full hundred-jin basket on your first climb—half a basket under this ridge's gravity will crush a new man's kneecaps into gravel."</p>
<p>Half an hour later, wearing a borrowed sleeveless sheepskin vest over his patched hemp shirt and carrying a heavy iron pry-bar as a walking staff, Lu Chen met <strong>Shi Tou (Little Stone)</strong> at the northern log-gate of Ash-Brook Village.</p>
<p>Uncle Shi's fourteen-year-old grandson was built like a stubborn mountain badger—short, broad-backed, with frost-chapped cheeks, a shock of unruly straw-colored hair tied with a leather thong, and a pair of oversized rawhide boots stuffed with dry moss that had belonged to his dead father. Slung across his narrow shoulders was a woven rattan pack-basket reinforced with rawhide straps, and tucked into his belt was a crude, fire-hardened wooden spear tipped with a scrap of sharpened hoop-iron.</p>
<p>"Grandpa said I'm to watch you on the shale switchbacks so you don't tumble back into the creek where I found you," Shi Tou said, trying to sound like a veteran mountain hand while eyeing the six-inch iron pry-bar in Lu Chen's bandaged hands. "If your chest starts rattling from the thin air up by the Three-Pine Ledge, tap the rock twice with your bar. Nobody laughs at a man stopping to catch his wind on Frost-Tooth Gully."</p>
<p>"I'll remember that, Little Brother Shi," Lu Chen smiled faintly, adjusting the rawhide straps of his own pack-basket. "Lead the way."</p>
<p>The moment they stepped beyond the village palisade and began climbing the forty-degree incline of Frost-Tooth Gully, Lu Chen understood why the villagers spoke of the ridge trail with grim respect.</p>
<p>Inside the sheltered basin of Ash-Brook Village, the surrounding hills broke the worst of the high-altitude gale. Out on the exposed shale switchbacks of the gully, the <strong>Black-Briar Wind</strong> shrieked straight down from the upper peaks, carrying razor-sharp needles of sleet and a dense, swirling fog of grey <strong>Desolate Ash-Qi</strong>. Moreover, because the red shale cliff contained a rich subterranean vein of heavy origin-iron, the local gravitational pull along the trail increased from tenfold to nearly <strong>twelvefold</strong>.</p>
<p>With every upward step, Lu Chen's rawhide sandals bit into frozen gravel that tried to slide out from under his heels. After three hundred yards, the air grew so cold and abrasive that each breath felt like swallowing dry salt. Ahead of him, fourteen-year-old Shi Tou walked with the low, swaying, flat-footed gait of someone born to the mountain—yet even the boy's breath was coming in loud, ragged white plumes, and his small shoulders hunched beneath the biting sleet.</p>
<p>Lu Chen kept his pace two steps behind the boy, sinking his awareness into <strong>Prism Stillness</strong>.</p>
<p>Through that three-pace sphere of stillness, he watched how Shi Tou placed his oversized boots on the frost-heaved shale, and he also saw the flaw in the boy's posture: because Shi Tou's left boot was half a size looser than his right, every third step twisted the boy's left ankle inward by three degrees, grinding the cartilage of his left knee and wasting nearly a fifth of his strength fighting his own balance.</p>
<p>When they reached the shelter of an overhanging granite boulder halfway up the gully, where a trickle of frozen rust-water hung in yellow icicles, Shi Tou dropped his basket with a gasp and slumped against the rock, rubbing his swollen left knee with both gloveless hands.</p>
<p>Without a word, Lu Chen knelt beside the boy, pulled two flat, wedge-shaped chips of dry pine-bark from his pocket that he had picked up near the village gate, and untied the rawhide laces of Shi Tou's oversized left boot.</p>
<p>"Hey—Big Brother Lu, what are you doing?" Shi Tou stammered, his ears turning red.</p>
<p>"Your father's boot is wider at the heel than yours," Lu Chen said calmly, sliding the two shaped pine-bark wedges along the outer edge of the moss lining and re-lacing the rawhide leather in a figure-eight ankle-lock used by northern infantrymen on Earth. "Every time you step on tilted shale, your heel slides sideways and twists your knee. Stand up and put your weight on it now."</p>
<p>Shi Tou stood up skeptically and stamped his left foot against the frozen rock. His eyes went wide. The boot now hugged his ankle like a second skin, locking his shin straight over the center of the sole.</p>
<p>"It doesn't wobble at all!" the fourteen-year-old breathed, stamping twice more in amazement before looking at Lu Chen with shining eyes. "Big Brother Lu, even Uncle Tie didn't see why my left knee always swells up on the gully climb!"</p>
<p>"A spearman's strength starts from the ground up," Lu Chen said, picking up his iron pry-bar as the sleet lashed around the boulder. "If the root is crooked, the spear-tip shakes. Come on—let's go pry out that ore before the snow gets deeper."</p>
