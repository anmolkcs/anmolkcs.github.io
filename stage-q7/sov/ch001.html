Rain lashed against the tall leaded windows of the provincial museum’s basement restoration lab, each gust rattling the glass like knuckles rapping on a coffin lid.

Under the sterile white ring of a magnifying lamp, Lu Chen held his breath. His right hand—steady as cast iron from eight years of micro-chiseling bronze vessels and practicing traditional northern big-spear routines every dawn—guided an ultrasonic scalpel along the encrusted seam of an unnamed artifact.

The catalog tag beside his elbow read simply: Item #094-B. Unclassified silicate polyhedron. Excavated three hundred meters beneath the karst sinkhole at Mount Wuling. Age: Indeterminate.

It was no bigger than a pigeon’s egg, shaped into nine irregular facets of dull, unpolished ash-gray stone. For three days, Lu Chen had tried everything from deionized solvent baths to diamond-tipped picks to remove the petrified rust clinging to its ninth facet. Nothing worked. Worse, the diamond picks had dulled against the stone as if scraping against neutron-star matter.

“Last pass before midnight,” Lu Chen muttered, rolling his stiff shoulders.

He angled the fine carbon-steel needle into the hairline groove on the ninth facet and applied a controlled three ounces of downward pressure.

Snap.

The needle tip sheared clean off. Before Lu Chen could pull his hand back, the jagged stub of the steel shaft skittered across his left thumb, slicing a shallow, half-inch crescent through the calloused skin. A bead of dark red blood welled up and dripped directly onto the exposed ninth facet of the gray polyhedron.

Lu Chen reached for a sterile gauze pad—and froze.

The blood did not pool or roll off the stone. The dull gray surface drank it in like parched desert sand swallowing a single raindrop.

In the next heartbeat, the hum of the air conditioner, the patter of the rain against the basement window, and even the ticking of the wall clock ceased. They did not fade; they simply stopped existing.

Fine silver-gray lines ignited beneath the nine facets of the stone. They were not carved grooves, but microscopic geometric pathways so intricate that staring at them for half a second made Lu Chen’s optic nerves burn like hot wires. The polyhedron rose half an inch off the rubber mat, rotating slowly against gravity.

A sound like an ancient bronze bell struck at the bottom of a frozen ocean echoed directly inside Lu Chen’s skull.

Crack.

Space around the worktable fractured like black ice. A vortex of colorless, timeless light erupted from the core of the nine-faceted prism, swallowing Lu Chen’s hand, his chest, and his consciousness before a single scream could leave his throat.

Cold.

Then the suffocating stench of congealed beast blood, wet sawdust, and rancid tallow.

Lu Chen gasped, his lungs spasming as if he had been dragged out from beneath a frozen river. His eyes snapped open to a ceiling of blackened pine rafters hung with iron meat hooks. From those hooks dangled the skinned carcasses of wolf-sized creatures with scaled bellies and serrated spines, dripping dark crimson fluid into wooden troughs below.

“He’s breathing! Old Liu, don’t throw him on the bone-cart yet—the kid’s chest just moved!”

A rough, oil-stained hand slapped Lu Chen’s cheek twice, hard enough to make his ears ring.

Lu Chen instinctively rolled to his side, his fingers curling to seize the wrist of the man slapping him, only to collapse back onto the straw mat as a wave of stabbing pain tore through his ribs and forehead.

The hand he had raised was not his twenty-four-year-old restorer’s hand. The fingers were leaner, shorter, and covered in raw blisters, horn-like calluses around the palm, and dozens of pale nick-scars around the knuckles.

At the same instant, fifteen years of foreign memories slammed into his mind like a breached dam.

Sky Spill Continent. Azure Sky Kingdom. Cloudstride City.

Lu Chen, fifteen years old, only son of the third branch of the declining Lu Clan.

In this world, martial道 (Dao) reigned supreme. A mortal who tempered their flesh, viscera, tendons, and bones to the limit could split boulders with a bare palm, while powerhouses who condensed True Essence and stepped into the Xiantian Realm could sever rivers, ride the wind, and live for centuries.

Yet the road of martial arts was built on mountains of gold and talent.

In the Azure Sky Kingdom, a youth’s martial aptitude was measured by how many meridians naturally resonated with heaven-and-earth origin energy during the twelve-year-old Awakening Ceremony. Standard martial disciples possessed Grade 3 talent; clan geniuses boasted Grade 4 or 5; and the legendary peerless prodigies of the royal capital reached Grade 6.

This body’s talent was a pitiful Grade 2, Upper-Tier.

Worse, his parents—who had left the main Lu Clan compound after his grandfather’s death to run a modest six-table tavern in the southern quarter—had spent eight years hoarding every silver tael they earned to purchase a Recommendation Token left behind by a distant ancestor. That token allowed one descendant below age sixteen to bypass the preliminary screening of the Seven Star Martial House in the royal capital and participate directly in the annual entrance disciple trial.

Even if a Grade 2 youth had only a one-in-ten-thousand chance of passing the Seven Star Martial House’s entrance trial, passing even the first round would qualify him as an outer steward of the kingdom’s garrison, securing his family’s safety for life.

Three days ago, Wang Yigao—the pampered second young master of the wealthy Wang Hardware & Pill Merchant House, whose own talent was Grade 3 Middle-Tier—had set his eyes on the Lu family’s Recommendation Token. When Lu Chen’s father refused to sell the ancestral token for a humiliating thirty taels of silver, Wang Yigao’s retainers had cornered the young Lu Chen in an alley outside the martial hall, fracturing two of his ribs with a “sparring blow” and warning him that if the token was not handed over within ten days, the tavern’s lease would be revoked and his father’s legs broken.

Refusing to tell his parents how badly he was injured, the fifteen-year-old youth had dragged himself to the back kitchens of the Crimson Scale Pavilion—the largest spirit-beast restaurant in Cloudstride City—where he worked twelve hours a day as a lowest-rank apprentice deboner, hoping to earn enough silver to buy a single packet of Bone-Knitting Powder. Half an hour ago, after ten straight hours of hacking through the iron-hard cartilage of a Black-Spined Boar with an internal injury, his heart blood had given out.

And into that empty, cooling vessel, the soul of Lu Chen from Earth had descended.

“Drink this, kid. It’s leftover ginger-salt湯 (broth) from the cauldron, not worth a copper coin, so don’t look at me like you owe me your life.”

A squat, barrel-chested man in his fifties with a braided gray beard and arms thicker than a teenager’s thighs shoved a chipped clay bowl against Lu Chen’s cracked lips. This was Master Liu, the head butcher of the Third Kitchen Yard at the Crimson Scale Pavilion.

Lu Chen swallowed the scalding, salty ginger broth in slow gulps. As the heat slid down his throat, he subconsciously pressed his right palm against the center of his sternum.

Beneath his rough hemp tunic, there was no wound. Yet when he closed his eyes and turned his focus inward, he felt it immediately.

Suspended above the dim, flickering hearth of his spiritual sea sat a tiny, nine-faceted gray crystal—the Chrono-Soul Prism.

It looked identical to the stone from the museum basement, except that it now existed simultaneously in his marrow and his soul. Even without circulating any energy, a faint, cool current radiated from the Prism’s facets, soothing the torn intercostal muscles around his bruised ribs and knitting the hairline fracture at an imperceptible but steady pace.

“Master Liu,” Lu Chen rasped, his voice hoarse as gravel. “How long was I out?”

“Less than the time it takes half an incense stick to burn,” Master Liu grunted, wiping his bloody cleaver on his leather apron. Though his tone was gruff, his eyes held a trace of pity. “Little Chen, listen to this old man. Deboning spirit beasts isn’t work for a half-crippled scholar’s body. Even First-Order spirit beasts have tendons tougher than cowhide soaked in桐 (tung) oil. The other apprentices in the yard have at least reached the middle of First Stage Body Transformation—Strength Training, with six or seven hundred jin of strength in their arms. You’ve barely scraped five hundred jin, and you’re hiding an internal bruise in your chest. Go home.”

“If I go home now, Master Liu, how much silver do I get for today’s tally?” Lu Chen asked quietly, propping himself up against the wooden chopping block.

Master Liu frowned. “The Pavilion’s rule is ironclad: three whole carcasses deboned cleanly before sundown equals thirty copper coins. You’ve finished two and a half. Leave now, and Steward Zhao at the front desk won’t give you a single copper—he’ll dock you ten coppers for spoiling the boar’s loin.”

Lu Chen looked toward the heavy timber rack beside the chopping table. Resting across the iron pegs was his tool: a Black-Iron Boning Knife, two feet long, three inches wide at the belly, tapering to a needle-sharp crescent tip. Forged from pig iron mixed with a trace of black-sand ore, the knife weighed a full thirty-two jin (roughly thirty-eight pounds).

To an ordinary mortal on Earth, swinging a thirty-eight-pound blade all day was madness; only in a world where spirit beasts possessed hides that snapped thin kitchen knives could such a monstrous cleaver-knife be used for deboning.

“I Still have an hour before the evening drum,” Lu Chen said.

He gripped the edge of the chopping table, planted his feet shoulder-width apart, and stood up.

His knees trembled once, sweat beading on his pale forehead as the bruised ribs protested, and then his spine locked straight. On Earth, he had spent eight years standing in low horse stances on frozen courtyard flagstones before dawn and ten hours hunched over microscopic restoration work without blinking. If the fifteen-year-old youth who owned this body had died for the sake of his parents and his dignity, then he, Lu Chen, would carry that debt to the end.

He wrapped his calloused right hand around the grease-slicked ironwood hilt of the thirty-two-jin boning knife and lifted it off the rack.

“Stubborn mule,” Master Liu muttered under his breath, though he didn’t reach out to stop him. Instead, the old butcher tossed a sharpening whetstone onto the corner of the table. “Finish that Black-Spined Boar’s hindquarter before the oil lamps are lit. If you nick the marrow-bone or leave more than three钱 (qian) of meat on the spine, don’t bother coming back tomorrow.”

“Understood.”

Lu Chen stepped up to the massive carcass of the Black-Spined Boar. He drew a long, slow breath, letting the cold, silent presence of the Chrono-Soul Prism in his chest steady the tremor in his fingers, and pressed the thirty-two-jin black-iron blade into the seam between tendon and bone.
