<p>With all twenty-eight tribute mattock-heads forged, quenched, and stacked neatly on the slate rack three days ahead of the fifteenth-day deadline, the suffocating tension that had hung over Old Tie Shan's smithy lifted for the first evening all autumn.</p>
<p>After dark, instead of extinguishing the hearth completely, Old Tie Shan banked the coals with dry birch bark, hung the iron pot on the tripod hook, and tossed in a palm-sized chunk of salted wild boar-fat that <strong>Uncle Shi</strong> had brought over, along with two handfuls of dried mountain mushrooms, wild garlic, and yellow millet.</p>
<p>Soon the rich, savory aroma of boiling meat-fat and garlic filled the smoky shed, drawing <strong>Shi Tou</strong>—who had been lingering outside the gate pretending to whittle a trap-peg—into the circle of firelight with his stomach rumbling audibly. A moment later, Uncle Shi himself ducked under the eaves carrying a small clay jug of home-brewed <strong>Bitter-Berry Rough Wine</strong>, followed by <strong>Widow Qin</strong> and little blind <strong>Xiao Ya</strong>, who brought four baked ash-potatoes wrapped in oak leaves.</p>
<p>There was no long cedar table or ten-thousand-year osmanthus wine here—only six people sitting on split pine logs and upturned ore-baskets around a dying blacksmith's hearth while sleet rattled against the slate roof.</p>
<p>Yet as Old Tie Shan ladled the thick, steaming boar-fat millet stew into chipped clay bowls—making sure the biggest piece of fatty rind went into little Xiao Ya's bowl and the second biggest into Lu Chen's—Lu Chen felt a quiet warmth settle into the hollow beneath his ribs.</p>
<p>"Drink a mouthful of this berry-rot, Lu Chen," Uncle Shi said, uncorking the clay jug and pouring a shallow cup for Old Tie and another for Lu Chen. "It tastes like horse-piss and pine needles, and it's got no spirit-qi in it for real cultivators, but it chases the gully-damp out of a working man's knees."</p>
<p>Lu Chen took the cup with both bandaged hands, toasted Uncle Shi and Old Tie Shan respectfully, and drank it down. The rough berry liquor burned pleasantly down his throat, warming his stomach without the caustic tear of snake-gall.</p>
<p>Across the fire, Old Tie Shan took a slow sip from his own bowl, his gaze drifting toward the empty corner by the coal heap where a smaller straw pallet had once lain. Reaching inside the collar of his scorched leather apron, the one-armed blacksmith pulled out a cord of braided horsehair on which hung half of a stamped iron mine-tally—broken jaggedly down the center and marked with the character <strong>"Seven"</strong>.</p>
<p>"Three autumns ago," Old Tie Shan said quietly, his rough thumb rubbing the jagged break of the iron tally, "the autumn rains washed out the lower bridge, and Ash-Brook came up nine mattocks and two hundred jin of pig-iron short on the tax ledger. My <strong>Tie Niu</strong> had just turned nineteen that month. Built like an ox, he was—had just finished his <strong>Tier One Ash-Skin Scouring</strong> and was set to marry the miller's girl in the spring. When Sub-Bailiff Zhao Kuan rode in with the debt-chains and threatened to confiscate the village's only forge-anvil, I tried to put my own neck in the iron collar."</p>
<p>Uncle Shi stared into the coals, his three-fingered left hand clenching on his knee. "Zhao Kuan laughed and said Pit #7 didn't want a one-armed old man who'd die in a week. When Old Tie swung his hammer to fight them, four bailiff guards pinned him to the mud and broke his left arm at the shoulder so badly Granny Liu had to take it off with a bone-saw to stop the mortification. Tie Niu dropped to his knees in the mud, bit his own finger to sign the three-year blood-contract in his father's place, and snapped this tally in half before they marched him up to <strong>Black-Slag Canyon</strong>."</p>
<p>Old Tie Shan's voice dropped to a hoarse whisper. "Three years is up this coming twelfth month, at the Great Winter Solstice. But last month, a carter who hauls sulfur from Black-Slag Canyon told us that the pit boss of Shaft #7—<strong>Scarface Luo Shan</strong>—claimed Tie Niu 'broke a mine-cart axle' and tacked another fifty taels of silver-iron debt onto his sentence. Nobody who gets their sentence extended by Luo Shan ever walks out of Pit #7 alive."</p>
<p>Around the fire, Shi Tou bit his lip hard to keep his eyes dry, while Widow Qin gently stroked little Xiao Ya's hair as the blind girl listened silently.</p>
<p>Lu Chen looked at the broken iron tally bearing the number <em>Seven</em> in Old Tie Shan's scarred hand, and then touched the rusted three-inch iron collar of his own shattered spear tied over his heart.</p>
<p>He did not make any grand, boastful promises that night—he was still a mortal whose skin had barely begun to harden, and Scarface Luo Shan was a <strong>Peak Tier Four (Slag-Bone Perfection)</strong> powerhouse backed by the Black-Rock Garrison.</p>
<p>Instead, Lu Chen picked up the iron poker, banked a fresh log of char-oak over the coals so the warmth reached Old Tie Shan's empty left sleeve, and spoke in a level, quiet voice:</p>
<p>"Old Tie. Between now and the Winter Solstice in the twelfth month, there are still eighty days. Keep that half-tally safe inside your apron. When the twelfth month comes, you won't have to walk up to Black-Slag Canyon alone."</p>
