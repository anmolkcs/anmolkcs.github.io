<p>As the grey morning light crept over the jagged spine of the <strong>Black-Briar Barren Ridge</strong>, Lu Chen sat on an upturned pine log beside the warming forge hearth, eating the coarse millet gruel one slow spoonful at a time.</p>
<p>The millet of the Great Desolate Origin Universe was called <strong>Iron-Husk Millet</strong>. Even after an hour of boiling, the grains remained hard and chewy as buckshot, leaving a dry, metallic astringency on the tongue. Yet as the hot gruel reached his stomach and broke down, Lu Chen felt a faint trickle of dense, earthy warmth seep into his starved limbs—far heavier and more stubborn than mortal grain in the lower worlds, providing the raw caloric fuel needed to keep a mortal heart beating under tenfold gravity.</p>
<p>While he ate, he looked out from the smithy shed across <strong>Ash-Brook Village</strong>.</p>
<p>The settlement consisted of barely seventy low huts built from unmortared black river-stone and chinked with yellow clay, huddled along a half-frozen stream whose water ran dark grey from upstream volcanic ash. A ten-foot palisade of sharpened iron-pine logs enclosed the village, its top beams bristling with thorny black-briar vines woven to deter night-prowling beasts.</p>
<p>There was no leisure here, not even among the old or the very young. Despite the biting wind that whipped sleet across the rutted mud lane, gaunt women in patched hemp coats were already hauling heavy wooden buckets from the creek, their shoulders permanently rounded from bearing yokes under the crushing gravity. Old men with coughing, rattling chests sat under low eaves splitting black-slag ore with hand-sledges so the smithy could smelt out the iron threads, while boys barely eight or nine years old carried baskets of dried sheep dung and pine roots toward the communal kilns.</p>
<p>"Hard place to look at, isn't it?" Old Tie Shan said quietly, using his single right hand and his knee to clamp a rusted ploughshare into the iron vise beside the anvil. "Seventy-three mouths left in Ash-Brook this autumn. Twenty years ago, there were a hundred and forty. If the <strong>Desolate Ash-Qi</strong> doesn't stiffen your lungs by forty, the ridge beasts get you in the woods, or the <strong>Black-Rock Town</strong> tax-riders haul your sons away to the canyon mines when the winter iron quota comes up short."</p>
<p>At the mention of sons and the canyon mines, the old blacksmith's jaw tightened, and his rasp-file bit into the rusted ploughshare with a harsh screech.</p>
<p>Before Lu Chen could reply, a soft <em>tap-tap-tap</em> of a willow stick sounded on the frozen mud path outside the shed.</p>
<p>A thin woman in her late twenties wearing a faded grey headscarf approached the smithy, carrying a basket of unwashed sheep's wool for the bellows gaskets. Holding onto the hem of her skirt was a little girl no more than seven years old, tapping a peeled willow switch ahead of her small straw shoes. The little girl's face was pinched with winter cold, and her wide eyes were a milky, unseeing pearl-grey—clouded over by what the frontier called <strong>Ash-Fever Cataracts</strong>.</p>
<p>The woman could not speak; a jagged white scar across her throat from an old bandit raid had taken her voice years ago. This was <strong>Widow Qin (Mute Sister Qin)</strong> and her blind daughter <strong>Xiao Ya</strong>, whose husband had died saving Uncle Shi from a ridge-boar two winters back.</p>
<p>Setting the wool basket beside the hearth, Widow Qin signed quickly with her chapped hands toward Old Tie Shan, then pointed toward Lu Chen on the log and held up a folded, freshly patched tunic of thick coarse hemp.</p>
<p>"Mute Qin says your old clothes were shredded to ribbons in the gully," Old Tie Shan translated gruffly, though his voice gentled as he looked down at the little blind girl warming her small hands near the outer stones of the hearth. "She stitched up one of her late husband's old work-shirts for you last night in exchange for me sharpening her turnip-hoe."</p>
<p>Little Xiao Ya tilted her head toward the sound of Lu Chen's quiet breathing. Fumbling inside the patched pocket of her vest, she pulled out three warm, fire-roasted <strong>Bitter-Oak Acorns</strong> and held them out in both small palms toward the direction of the log.</p>
<p>"Big Brother," Xiao Ya whispered in a small, clear voice, her milky grey eyes turned toward his knees. "Granny Liu said the man in Uncle Tie's shed drank snake-gall and didn't cry. Snake-gall stays bitter on the tongue for three days. If you eat roasted acorns after, the sweet comes back."</p>
<p>Lu Chen froze.</p>
<p>For a heartbeat, the smoky smithy shed vanished, replaced by the memory of his mother Meng Wan pressing a piece of brown rock-sugar into his palm after he drank bitter bone-mending medicine at age fifteen, and young Su Wan'er smiling brightly across the alchemy court of the Royal Capital. A sharp, physical ache tightened around Lu Chen's throat—far worse than the three cracked ribs in his chest.</p>
<p>He reached out carefully with both scarred hands, cupped his palms beneath Xiao Ya's tiny, frost-reddened fingers so she wouldn't drop the acorns, and took only one, gently folding her fingers back around the other two.</p>
<p>"Thank you, Xiao Ya," Lu Chen said softly, his hoarse voice warm and steady. "One is enough. The bitter taste is already gone."</p>
