A First-Order Black-Spined Boar weighed over eight hundred jin alive. Even dead and bled, its fascia—the silvery-white membrane binding muscle to bone—was tough enough to deflect an ordinary woodcutter’s axe.

In the back kitchens of the Crimson Scale Pavilion, spirit beast carcasses could not simply be hacked apart with brute force. Spirit beasts stored trace amounts of heaven-and-earth origin energy inside their flesh and marrow. If a butcher smashed the bones or tore the main blood vessels crudely, that essence would dissipate into the air within minutes, turning a dish worth ten taels of silver into dry, tasteless firewood.

A true deboner had to slide a heavy blade strictly along the natural cavities between joints, severing hundreds of criss-crossing ligaments without leaving a single scratch on the underlying bone.

Shhk.

Lu Chen pushed the thirty-two-jin black-iron knife forward. Halfway down the boar’s lumbar ridge, the tip caught against a calcified knot of gristle. A dull jar ran up his wrist, sending a lance of pain into his bruised ribs.

Across the yard, a tall, pockmarked youth in a clean linen vest let out a mocking snort. That was Zhao Hu, Steward Zhao’s nephew. At seventeen, Zhao Hu had already reached the Peak of First Stage Strength Training, boasting nine hundred jin of arm strength, and only worked in the deboning yard to pilfer beast blood and scraps for his own cultivation.

“Look at Little Chen,” Zhao Hu sneered to the two apprentices beside him while casually slicing through a Deer-Horned Sheep’s shoulder. “Moving his knife like an eighty-year-old grandmother threading a needle. Steward Zhao already promised my cousin his workbench starting next month. Why waste lamp oil watching a cripple play with iron?”

Lu Chen didn’t even glance in Zhao Hu’s direction.

To react to a barking dog when you didn’t yet have the strength to break its teeth was the behavior of a child. On Earth, Lu Chen had spent weeks teasing apart carbonized bamboo slips that would crumble if breathed on too hard. Compared to that, the arrogance of a kitchen bully was less than wind passing his ear.

Instead, Lu Chen closed his eyes for three breaths.

Why did the blade catch?

In his previous host’s memories, deboning was taught by rote repetition: memorize where the joints were and force the heavy knife through with raw wrist strength. Because the previous Lu Chen lacked strength, every cut required straining his shoulders and ribs until blood pooled in his throat.

No, Lu Chen thought, his consciousness brushing against the first facet of the gray Chrono-Soul Prism inside his spiritual sea. A museum restorer doesn’t break petrified stone by hitting it harder; he finds the microscopic cleavage plane where the crystal lattice wants to part.

As his mind touched the cold gray surface of the Prism, a strange phenomenon occurred.

There was no flash of light, no mechanical voice. Instead, an overwhelming sense of Absolute Stillness washed through his cerebral cortex.

The noisy clatter of cleavers in the kitchen yard, the hiss of boiling cauldrons, and the throbbing ache in his ribs seemed to recede behind a pane of thick glass. In his mind’s eye, the memory of his last twenty cuts replayed with crystalline clarity—every micro-degree of his wrist’s tilt, the subtle drag of the boar’s fascia against the beveled steel, the exact spot where the tendon fibers twisted clockwise before anchoring into the third vertebra.

Under this Prism Stillness, his perception wasn’t magically given the answer, nor did his strength increase by a single jin. What changed was his ability to observe and deduce without a trace of mental clutter.

When Lu Chen opened his eyes, the dark irises seemed deeper, still as two winter wells.

He adjusted his grip on the ironwood hilt by half an inch, shifting his thumb from the spine of the blade to the side flat. Instead of pushing from his injured chest and shoulder, he sank his weight half an inch into his heels, letting the natural twist of his waist transfer momentum through his elbow into the thirty-two-jin blade—the exact kinetic chain of a northern big-spear thrust.

Shhhk—!

A soft, whisper-thin sound rose from the carcass.

The black-iron tip glided into the lumbar seam at a seventeen-degree angle. Where the calcified tendon twisted clockwise, Lu Chen gave his wrist a tiny, imperceptible counterclockwise flick.

The tough silvery tendon parted like warm butter.

Shhk. Shhk. Shhk.

One cut. Two cuts. Ten cuts.

Lu Chen didn’t speed up recklessly, nor did he pause. His breathing fell into a deep, measured cadence—inhale for four beats as the blade drew back along the rib fascia, exhale in a long, compressed thread through his teeth as the tip slid through the joint capsule.

Twenty minutes passed.

Master Liu, who had been smoking his brass pipe by the doorway while waiting to rescue the ruined boar loin, slowly lowered his pipe from his mouth.

Over at the corner workbench, Lu Chen’s right arm moved in clean, economical arcs. There was no hacking, no sawing, and no grunt of exertion. The thirty-two-jin black-iron knife seemed to have lost twenty jin of its dead weight, flowing around the gnarled spine of the Black-Spined Boar like water streaming around riverbed stones.

Clack.

With a final upward flick of the crescent tip, Lu Chen lifted a forty-jin strip of prime dorsal loin away from the carcass and laid it onto the oiled wooden tray.

Left behind on the chopping block was the boar’s complete vertebral column and ribcage. In the orange glow of the newly lit tallow lamps, the bones gleamed an immaculate, pearly white—not a single shred of red meat clung to the ridges, and not a single white knife-scar marred the smooth periosteum covering the bone.

“Clean bone… unbroken periosteum,” Master Liu murmured, stepping forward and running his rough thumb along the third lumbar vertebra. Even for a veteran butcher who had worked ten years at the Pavilion, stripping a Black-Spined Boar’s dorsal ridge without nicking the bone membrane required peak focus.

Zhao Hu’s smirk stiffened on his pockmarked face. He craned his neck, expecting to see hacked bone shards, only to see Master Liu pick up a wooden tally token and stamp it with red ink.

“Three carcasses complete. Zero spoilage,” Master Liu announced loudly enough for the whole yard to hear. He tossed the stamped wooden tally into Lu Chen’s palm, his bushy eyebrows twitching as he studied the boy’s calm face. “Your wrist angle changed on the back nine ribs. Who taught you how to unload force through your waist?”

“When my ribs hurt too much to push with my shoulders, I had to let the knife’s own weight do the work, Master Liu,” Lu Chen replied respectfully, wiping the black-iron blade clean with a dry cloth before returning it to the rack.

Master Liu stared at him for a long second, then let out a short bark of laughter. “Pain is a better teacher than half the drill instructors in Cloudstride City. Take your tally to the front counter, collect your thirty coppers, and go straight to the apothecary for some bruise plaster. Tomorrow morning at the fifth watch, there’s a batch of Iron-Backed Flood Fish coming in from the Black Water River. Debone five of those without rupturing their bitter-gall sacs, and the kitchen pays double—sixty coppers.”

“Thank you, Master Liu. I’ll be here before the fifth watch.”

For the next five days, Lu Chen lived a life reduced to three things: sleep, ginger broth, and the thirty-two-jin black-iron knife.

Five hours before dawn, he arrived at the Crimson Scale Pavilion. Ten hours later, his hemp shirt was stiff with dried salt from his sweat and his right hand was so swollen he had to pry his fingers off the knife hilt one by one with his left hand.

Three thousand cuts a day. Fifteen thousand cuts in five days.

With every hundred cuts, he sank his mind into the icy clarity of Prism Stillness, comparing the resistance of beast tendons to the flow of strength through his own muscles.

By the fifth afternoon, the swelling in his fingers had vanished, replaced by a layer of tough, amber-colored callus that felt warm to the touch. More importantly, the bruised ribs had completely healed, and every time he exhaled and drove the thirty-two-jin knife through the spine of an Iron-Backed Flood Fish, a faint drumming sound echoed beneath his skin—the unmistakable sign of muscle fibers condensing toward the absolute peak of First Stage Strength Training: a full 950 jin of raw force!

And on the evening of the sixth day, an iron cage dragged by four armored horses rumbled into the back courtyard of the Crimson Scale Pavilion.
