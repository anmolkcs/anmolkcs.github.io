# Chapter 45: Top Sixteen

The rounds of sixty-four and thirty-two passed in two days, and Gu Yan would remember little of either except a feeling of being watched.

He won both fights. The first was against a Zhao clan swordswoman whose blade was quick and honest; the second against a monk from a hill temple who fought with prayer beads and apologised every time he struck. Neither was easy, and neither required anything Gu Yan did not wish to show. But after the Lu Kang match, nothing he did went unobserved. When he walked out to a platform the crowd around it doubled. Clan representatives with brush and paper sat in the front rows, writing down how he stood. And in the royal pavilion, a little apart from the King and the princes, a man in an undyed grey robe sat with a cup of tea and watched every exchange with polite, untroubled attention.

"He's watching you," Mo Ji said.

"He's watching everyone."

"He's watching you *more*."

On the evening of the second day, sixteen names remained on the great board in gold. Starfall Academy had five of them: Gu Yan, Mo Ji, Su Lingxi, Lu Mingyuan, and the Third Prince. Gu Feng had gone out in the round of thirty-two to a Northern Garrison captain, and come off the platform with a split lip, looking oddly at peace. Gu Lan had gone out in the round of sixty-four after a fight so fierce the crowd had stood to applaud her, and had cried afterwards in the tunnel for exactly as long as it took Gu Yan to find her, and then wiped her face and gone to buy chestnuts.

The round of sixteen would be fought in a single day, on the central platform, one match at a time.

* * *

Su Lingxi fought third.

Her opponent was the second son of the Duke of Hongzhou, a fire cultivator of twenty-two at Late Star Sea with six stars, a thick-necked, good-humoured young man named Hong Lie who had burned his way through the bracket without once being forced back a step. He bowed to Su Lingxi with real courtesy. Then he lit the air.

The whole platform turned orange. Flames rose off Hong Lie's shoulders like a cloak, and when he punched, a fist of fire the size of an ox-cart roared across the jade. The heat reached the front tiers. People shielded their faces.

Su Lingxi was not where it landed.

Gu Yan, at the rail of the Starfall section, watched with his heart in his throat, and then, slowly, with growing delight. She did not fight the fire. She did not even seem to look at it. She stood with her jade compass open in her left hand and her eyes on its needle, and she moved — a half-step left, a turn, a lean — always a moment before the fire came, as though she were reading the place it would arrive from a chart. Which, he realised, was exactly what she was doing. The compass read fate, a little. And a man about to strike has, for an instant, a very small and very certain fate.

Hong Lie pressed harder. His flames grew. Su Lingxi drifted through them like frost through an open window.

Then her right hand moved.

Gu Yan barely saw the needles. They were thin as hairs and pale as starlight, and she threw nine of them in a single flick of her wrist, and they went into the fire and through it as though the fire were not there. Hong Lie grunted. Something had stung him in the shoulder, the elbow, the side of the neck. He punched again — and his fist came out of the flames bare. His fire guttered. He stared at his hand.

Nine needles stood in his body, on nine points along his meridians, each one humming faintly with cold. They had not wounded him. They had *pinned* him: not his flesh but his qi, holding each of the channels shut at a single point, like nails through a sluice gate.

"You can pull them out," Su Lingxi said. "It will take you a few breaths. In that time I will put another nine in, and those will go deeper." Her voice was perfectly polite. "I would rather not."

Hong Lie looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed, a big helpless laugh, and raised his hand to the judge. "I surrender. Heaven, I surrender. Somebody tell my father I was beaten by an astronomer."

She bowed to him, quite gravely, and walked off the platform without hurrying, and the arena, after a stunned silence, roared.

When she reached the Starfall rail she passed Gu Yan without looking at him and said, very low, "Close your mouth."

He closed it.

* * *

Mo Ji fought fifth, and it was nothing like elegant.

His opponent was a man from the Iron Mountains in the far north whom the board called simply *Bao*, of no clan and no school, twenty-four years old and Mid Star Sea with three stars, and nearly a head taller than Mo Ji, which Gu Yan would not have thought possible. He wore no shirt. His chest was a map of scars. He had come down from a mining camp three months ago, the rumour went, walked the whole way to Cangdu, and won every fight in the bracket by the simple method of hitting people until they stopped standing up.

Mo Ji looked at him across the platform and cracked his knuckles, and Gu Yan, who knew him, saw a slow and terrible happiness come into his face.

What followed was the longest match of the round. There was very little technique in it. Mo Ji's Mountain-Bearing Fist was an Earth-grade art, and Bao's style seemed to be no art at all, only a lifetime of swinging picks at rock; and the two of them met in the middle of the platform and simply traded blows. Each punch landed with a sound like a mallet on a barrel. Mo Ji's lip split. Bao's eye closed. Mo Ji went down on one knee and came back up. Bao went down on one knee and came back up. The jade cracked under their feet in a widening star.

Mo Ji had four stars to Bao's three, and the art, and a smith's understanding of where force went. But Bao had a head of height and a body like a cliff, and for a long while it seemed to Gu Yan that the fight would be decided only by whichever of them fell first out of plain exhaustion.

It was decided, in the end, by an anvil.

Gu Yan saw the moment Mo Ji understood it. Bao's punches came from the shoulders, huge and looping; Mo Ji's came from the ground, through the legs and hips. Mo Ji stopped trading. He planted his feet wide and let the next punch land on his forearms, and grunted, and did not move — and the force of it, which should have staggered him, went down through his body into the cracked jade like a hammer-blow into an anvil, and stopped there. The platform split under his heels.

Bao's eyes went wide. He had felt, for the first time, what it was to hit something that did not give.

Mo Ji's answering fist came up from the soles of his feet. It hit Bao in the centre of the chest. The big man left the ground — not far, a hand's breadth — and came down flat on his back at the platform's edge and lay there, blinking at the sky.

After a while he started to laugh, too.

Mo Ji walked off with his left eye swollen shut and two fingers broken and blood all down his front, grinning like a boy at a fair, and sat down hard on the Starfall bench and said to no one in particular, "I'd like to do that again someday. Not today."

* * *

Cang Yi fought eighth, and it took one breath.

His opponent was a genius of the Yan ducal clan, twenty-three, Peak Star Sea with six stars — a famous name, a man who had been tipped for the top four. He came out with a long saber and a face full of grim determination. The judge called the start. The Yan heir drew his saber and lunged.

Cang Yi's spear moved once. Gu Yan, watching with every scrap of perception he had, saw the line of it: not toward the saber, nor toward the man, but toward a point in the empty air a pace in front of the Yan heir's chest, where the man was going to be a fraction of a moment later. The spear-tip arrived at that point. So did the Yan heir.

He stopped, with the spear-tip resting against his breastbone, and the saber still drawn back, and a look of pure astonishment on his face.

Then he lowered the saber and bowed, very deeply, and conceded.

Cang Yi bowed in return and walked off the platform. As he passed the Starfall rail, he met Gu Yan's eyes and inclined his head slightly — a greeting, or an acknowledgement, or an invitation. Gu Yan found he could not tell which.

* * *

Gu Yan fought last.

The board beside the platform read: *Ye Han. Iron Wall Army, Western Frontier. Late Star Sea. Stars: 6.*

He was twenty-one, thin and pale, with close-cropped hair and the brown leather armour of a frontier soldier, scuffed and mended. He carried a plain straight sword in a plain scabbard. He did not look at the crowd. He did not look at the royal pavilion. He looked at Gu Yan, briefly, the way a man looks at weather, and drew.

Gu Yan drew too, and the lines came, and he understood within the first exchange that he was in trouble.

He had become used to reading fighters. Every fighter he had met had lines in their movements — habits, flourishes, the places where their body began a motion before their mind decided it. The spearman's hip. Lu Kang's surges. Even Cang Yi had lines, though they were deep and few. Gu Yan's whole art depended on them.

Ye Han had almost none.

His sword did not flourish. It did not feint. It went from where it was to where it needed to be along the shortest path, every time, and nothing in his body announced it beforehand. It was the most economical swordsmanship Gu Yan had ever seen, and there was a coldness in its intent — a frost-edged stillness — that made the air around the blade glitter faintly. The board had a name for it: *Frost Line Sword*, Earth-grade high. Gu Yan thought it was more than that. It was a sword that had been learned against people who were trying to kill its wielder, on a frontier where there were no second chances.

He gave ground. He had to. Three times in the first ten exchanges Ye Han's blade came at him along a line he saw only at the last instant, and three times he turned it aside by a hair. The fourth time he was too slow, and the frost-cold edge opened a shallow cut across his ribs, and the cold went into the wound and spread there like ice on a pond.

He backed to the centre of the platform and stopped reading.

It was the hardest thing he had done in the Tournament. Since Jade Ridge he had lived by the lines; they were how he understood the world. But a sword was not a road, Han Qiu had said. A sword was a decision.

When Ye Han's next thrust came, Gu Yan did not look for the line it would take. He chose one of his own — straight through the centre of the thrust, against all sense — and cut along it, with intent.

The two blades met at their points. Frost against iron. For a moment they held there, quivering, and the air between them cracked like ice.

Then both men were moving at once, and it was no longer a matter of reading or of being read. It was intent against intent, line chosen against line chosen, and the platform rang. Gu Yan's starbone body was stronger than Ye Han's. Ye Han's sword was surer. Gu Yan took another cut on the thigh, and a third on the forearm; Ye Han took the flat of the iron sword across the wrist and a blow on the shoulder that drove him to one knee, and rose without a sound.

And at the last, as Ye Han's sword came at his throat along the shortest of all lines, Gu Yan felt — very clearly — the door Han Qiu had told him to keep shut.

He used it.

Space folded. One step. He was not where Ye Han's sword arrived; he was half a pace to the left of it, and the world snapped back around him with a pain like a nail driven through his temple, and the iron sword was resting along the side of Ye Han's neck.

Silence.

Ye Han looked at the blade by his jaw out of the corner of his eye. Then he lowered his own sword.

"Yield," he said.

The arena exploded.

* * *

Afterwards, in the shadow of the tunnel, with blood drying on his ribs and his head still pounding from the step, Gu Yan found Ye Han waiting for him.

"You cut like a carpenter," the soldier said.

"I've been told."

"It was a compliment." Ye Han's voice was flat. "On the frontier, the men who live are the ones who build things that hold." He looked at Gu Yan a moment longer. "The Blood Moon Sect raids our forts three times as often as two years ago. Every season, Lu clan supply convoys pass west through our lines with royal seals, and no one is allowed to search them." He turned to go. "If you are going to face Lu Mingyuan, do not let him touch you."

That night Han Qiu came to the dormitory. He did not scold Gu Yan for the step. He did not mention it at all.

"Lu Wenhe has come out of seclusion," he said. "The Lu patriarch. He arrived at the arena this evening, and he has asked to inspect the platform formations himself."
