# Chapter 27: Defeat

The Academy's Grand Arena was a bowl of white stone sunk into the earth below the Star Tower, ringed by tiers that could seat twenty thousand. On most days it sat empty except for deacons sweeping leaves. On the day of the autumn exhibition, it was full by the second bell.

"Twenty thousand," Mo Ji said, peering out from the tunnel mouth where the competitors waited. "Do you know what twenty thousand people sound like when they're disappointed? I've heard my father's customers when he raises the price of nails by a copper. Multiply that by—"

"Thank you, Mo Ji."

"I'm only saying. Don't look at the crowd." Mo Ji paused. "Also, the King's here."

Gu Yan looked at the crowd.

High on the northern tier, beneath a canopy of deep blue silk, sat a man in plain dark robes. He was not tall. He was not, at this distance, remarkable in any way Gu Yan could see. But the air above the canopy was subtly wrong, the way air above a road is wrong in summer, and when Gu Yan let the barest thread of lines into his eyes, he saw why. Around the King, for a radius of perhaps thirty zhang, the world obeyed a different law. The stone tiers, the silk, the very light bent toward him as if toward a weight at the bottom of a well.

Mid Mandate. The strongest man in the kingdom had come to watch his son spar with a carpenter's grandson.

"Don't look at the King," Mo Ji amended.

Su Lingxi stood in the shadow of the tunnel wall, arms folded. Gu Lan, pale but on her feet again, stood beside her. Neither of them had said much.

"He's stronger than you," Su Lingxi said at last.

"I know."

"I don't mean his realm. Everyone knows his realm." She looked at him with her precise, unsparing eyes. "I mean his fate thread is a rope, and yours is nothing, and in a fight, a rope is a rope." She hesitated. "Don't do anything stupid trying to prove otherwise."

"I won't."

"You will," she said. "But try not to."

* * *

The herald called their names. Gu Yan walked out into the light.

The noise hit him like a wave against a seawall: twenty thousand voices, mostly for the Prince, some for no one, a few, from the outer-court tiers, for the boy from Jade Ridge who had sat in the centre of the third floor. He stood on the white stone and let it wash past him and looked across the arena at Cang Yi.

The Prince carried a spear.

Gu Yan had not seen him with a weapon before. It was long, nearly two zhang, its shaft of dark wood bound in silver at the grip and the head a leaf-shaped blade of pale steel. Cang Yi held it loosely, butt resting on the stone, like a man leaning on a walking stick. He wore a plain grey training robe. No crown, no emblem. He bowed, exactly as one disciple bows to another.

Gu Yan bowed back, and drew his father's iron sword.

"Begin," said the elder officiating.

Neither of them moved.

Gu Yan looked at the lines. Cang Yi stood in a web of them, dense and luminous, his nine meridians full and steady and, beneath them, something Gu Yan had never seen clearly in a fight before: a Star Sea. It lay in the Prince's dantian like a pool of night, and in it seven stars burned. Each star was a knot of comprehension, and from each one ran threads of qi so fine and so many that they made the meridians around them look like ropes beside silk.

*So that's the difference.*

Cang Yi moved.

He did not lunge. He simply walked forward, and the spear came up off the stone and was suddenly in front of Gu Yan's throat, and Gu Yan had read it coming and still barely got the iron sword in the way. Steel rang. The impact went up his arm into his shoulder. He gave ground, a step, two, turned the next thrust aside along the line of the spearhead, and stepped in.

The crowd roared.

For the next forty breaths, Gu Yan fought better than he had ever fought in his life.

He read every attack. He saw the spear's lines before they existed: the shift of weight in Cang Yi's back foot, the turning of a wrist, the intent gathering a heartbeat before each thrust. He saw that the Prince favoured high thrusts on the second beat of a combination and low sweeps on the fourth. He slipped inside the spear's reach, where a long weapon becomes a liability, and stayed there, cutting short and close along lines that made the Prince retreat. Twice the Prince had to spin the shaft to block. Once, Gu Yan chose a line along the edge of the Prince's sleeve and cut it, cleanly, a hand's width from the skin, and the grey cloth fluttered loose in the wind.

His body held. Ninth Vein, every meridian open, and beneath it the starbone, dense and stubborn, absorbing impacts that should have broken his wrists. When the spear-butt caught him in the ribs, he grunted and kept his feet. When a sweep took his legs, he rolled and came up inside the next thrust.

A ripple went through the crowd. On the outer-court tiers, someone shouted his name.

Cang Yi stepped back. Three paces. He looked at his cut sleeve, then at Gu Yan.

"Good," the Prince said. He sounded genuinely pleased. "Now I'll stop holding back."

The seven stars rose.

They came up out of his Star Sea and into the open air, seven points of white light orbiting the Prince's body, and as they rose the pressure around him changed. It was not the King's law-bending weight; it was smaller, closer, a sphere of influence perhaps three zhang across. But within that sphere, the air thickened. Qi flooded toward Cang Yi from all sides like water rushing to a drain, and the stars drank it, and the Prince's lines became so bright and so many that Gu Yan's eyes watered trying to follow them.

"Cang Royal Spear," said Cang Yi quietly. "Seven Stars Pierce the Sky."

The first thrust was a single star.

Gu Yan read it. Of course he did; it was as clear as all the others. Right shoulder, a straight line. He brought the iron sword up to turn it along the edge.

The spearhead met his blade, and the star behind the thrust burst into it like a hammer of light, and the force went through the sword, through his arms, through his body, and threw him backward six paces across the stone.

He stayed on his feet by a hair. His arms were numb to the elbow.

*I read it. I read it perfectly, and it didn't matter.*

The second thrust was two stars. He did not try to block it. He slipped aside along a line he had seen open, and the spear passed a finger's width from his ribs, and the wind of its passage tore his robe. The third was three stars, and he saw it coming from a full breath away, and saw also that there was nowhere to go. The spear's lines filled every path. He chose the only line he had: through the attack, along the shaft, to the Prince's hands.

He cut.

For one heartbeat, he thought he had it. His sword came down along the silver-bound shaft, aimed at the gap between the Prince's fingers, a line chosen and true.

The fourth star flared. Qi poured into the shaft from the Prince's Star Sea, a sudden surge so dense that the lines of the wood itself changed, hardened, thickened, and Gu Yan's chosen line simply *closed*. His blade struck the spear like a chisel striking iron. It skidded off.

And then he understood, in the clearest way possible, the gap between Ninth Vein and Star Sea.

It was not speed. It was not strength. His body was as strong as the Prince's, perhaps stronger; the starbone made sure of that. His eyes were better. His sword, for one moment, had been sharper. But his qi ran through nine channels, broad and fast, like nine rivers. And Cang Yi's qi rose from a sea. When Gu Yan spent his strength, he spent it from the rivers, and the rivers ran low. When Cang Yi spent his, the sea refilled it. And each of those seven stars was a comprehension the Prince could pour into every strike, multiplied, amplified, a lever on the world.

Gu Yan could choose a line. Cang Yi could choose a line and then make the world heavier along it.

The fifth thrust came. Five stars. He saw it. He moved. It did not matter.

The spear caught the iron sword at its base and tore it from his hands. It spun away across the white stone and rang against the arena wall. Gu Yan threw himself sideways, rolled, came up empty-handed, reached for the starlight in his bones the way he had on the granary roof. It answered, cold and fierce, and he poured it into his legs and launched himself forward, inside the spear, inside the stars, with nothing but his fists—

The sixth star. The spear-butt came around in a blur and struck him in the chest, and the world went white.

When it came back, he was on his back on the stone. The sky above him was very blue. The pale leaf-shaped spearhead rested on his throat, so lightly that he could barely feel it.

The seventh star hung above the Prince's shoulder, unspent.

Twenty thousand people were silent.

Gu Yan lay still for a moment. His chest hurt. His arms hurt. His pride, he discovered, hurt less than he had expected.

"I yield," he said.

* * *

The roar of the crowd came back like surf. Cang Yi lifted the spear, reversed it, and held out his free hand. Gu Yan took it and was pulled to his feet.

"You cut my sleeve," the Prince said quietly, under the noise. "Before I raised the stars. No one my age has done that since I was twelve." He let go of Gu Yan's hand. "If you had a Star Sea, I think I would have lost."

"You didn't use the seventh star."

Cang Yi's mouth quirked. "No. I'd have had to explain to my father why I nearly killed a disciple in an exhibition." He bowed, more deeply than before. "Thank you for the match, Gu Yan."

"Thank you, Your Highness."

He walked back to the tunnel on legs that wobbled, retrieving his iron sword on the way. Its edge had a new notch near the hilt, where the fourth star had struck. He ran his thumb along it and did not mind.

* * *

"Wide," said Han Qiu.

They were in the courtyard on the northern slope, in the last of the evening light. Gu Yan sat on the stone bench with his chest bound in bandages and a bowl of bitter medicine cooling in his hands. Mo Ji and Gu Lan had walked him up the hill and then been sent away; Su Lingxi had come as far as the gate, looked at him for a long moment, and said, "That was stupid, and also very good," and left before he could answer.

"Elder?"

"Your third exchange after the stars rose. You went for his hands along the shaft." Han Qiu stood under the plum tree, hands behind his back. "It was a good line. It was the right line. And you cut it wide, because by then you were angry that reading him was not enough." He paused. "You will always be fighting people stronger than you. That will not change for a very long time. When you lose your temper about it, you lose a hair's breadth of edge. Against a Star Sea, a hair's breadth is the difference between a cut sleeve and a cut throat."

Gu Yan looked at the medicine. "Yes, Elder."

"Other than that," Han Qiu said, "you lost well." He was silent for a moment. "Very well. The King asked my name afterward. He does not usually ask names."

They sat in silence while the sky darkened. Far below, the lamps of the Academy came on one by one, and beyond them the lanterns of Cangdu, and beyond them the dark plains of a kingdom that was one of a hundred and eighty-one in a quarter of a continent, under a sky that was a ceiling.

"Rivers and a sea," Gu Yan said quietly.

Han Qiu glanced at him.

"That's the gap. My qi runs in channels. His rises from a sea. I can read every line he makes and it doesn't matter, because he can pour more into each one than I can carry." He put down the bowl. "I need a Star Sea."

"Everyone at Ninth Vein needs a Star Sea," said Han Qiu. "Most of them take three years to open one. Some take ten. Some never do."

"I don't have three years."

"Why not?"

Gu Yan did not answer. He did not know, exactly. Only that there was a frame inside his head that went on forever, and a wound in it, and somewhere above the ceiling of the world, his father had gone looking for the way up.

Han Qiu studied him for a long time in the dusk. Then he turned toward the house.

"Drink your medicine," he said over his shoulder. "When your ribs are healed, come to me, and we'll talk about what a Star Sea costs."

Gu Yan drank the medicine. It was even worse than the Prince's tea.

He had just set down the empty bowl when a paper crane came fluttering up over the courtyard wall, wobbling on a faint thread of qi, and landed on his knee. It was folded from the pale blue paper of the Observatory. He unfolded it.

The handwriting was small and precise and unmistakable.

*Archive. Tomorrow at the first bell. I found a record from ten years ago. Your family name is on it.*

*—S.*
