# Chapter 12: The Capital of a Thousand Lanterns

The light came down out of the dark like a slow, falling moon, and resolved into a boat.

It was perhaps twenty zhang long, its hull lacquered black and painted along the sides with a procession of silver cranes, and it floated a man's height above the road with no wings, no sail, no visible means of holding itself up at all. Paper lanterns hung from its rails by the hundred, red and gold and pale green, so that it moved inside a soft haze of its own light. On its prow, carved from a single piece of white jade, a falling star trailed a tail of fine engraved lines.

The spirit-horses stamped and blew. The driver swore under his breath.

Gu Yan did not swear. He was looking at the hull.

Beneath the lacquer, through the first node's sight, he could see the boat's true shape: a skeleton of glowing lines running through the keel and ribs, gathering at nine points along the bottom, where something pulsed like slow heartbeats. Formation arrays. Spirit stones set into them, he guessed, by the dozen, feeding a pattern that pushed against the earth the way a hand presses down on water. The pattern was beautiful. It was also, at one of the nine points, a little uneven, as if someone had repaired it in a hurry and not quite matched the old work.

"The Academy's," said the deacon, climbing down. "They send one for the last stretch when the recruits from the eastern prefectures arrive. It is meant," he added dryly, "to impress you."

"It's working," said Gu Lan.

They left the carriage and the tired horses with the driver, who would return to Jade Ridge, and climbed a rope ladder onto the deck with the other recruits. The boat rose. The ground fell away so smoothly that Gu Yan did not feel it go; he only noticed that the carriage had become the size of a shoe, and then of a bean, and then was lost in the darkness of the plain.

Gu Feng stood very still at the rail, gripping it with both hands. The salt-merchant's son was being quietly sick over the far side.

And ahead of them, Cangdu came up out of the night.

* * *

The walls came first.

From a distance they looked like a range of low cliffs. Closer, they kept growing, and growing, until the boat was sailing level with their top and Gu Yan understood that the cliffs were masonry, every block the size of a house, fitted so finely that even the Lattice's sight found the joints only as threads of shadow. A hundred zhang high. He did the arithmetic in his head and then stopped, because the number was not useful. It was simply more wall than he had known could exist.

Along the top ran a road wide enough for four wagons, and on it, every few hundred paces, stood towers with bronze mirrors that caught the lantern light and threw it outward across the plain in slow, sweeping beams.

Then the boat cleared the wall, and the city opened beneath him.

Lanterns. That was the first and overwhelming thing. Lanterns hung in every street, on every eave, strung in lines across the canals; lanterns floated on the water by the thousand, drifting in slow currents; and above the rooftops, everywhere, lantern boats like theirs and smaller ones, skiffs and barges and pleasure-craft, rode the night air in their own haloes of colour. The city was a bowl of light. Smoke and music rose out of it together. Gu Yan could hear a hundred kinds of noise at once: bells, drums, someone singing, the clatter of wheels on stone, a market crier, a dog, a roar of laughter from a wine-house, all of it blending into one deep, warm sound like the breathing of something enormous and content.

Through the centre of it ran a river.

It was not like the rivers on the plain. It was perfectly straight, as straight as a chalk line snapped on a board, and wider than the Jade Ridge market square, and its water glowed faintly blue from within. It ran from the far western wall to the eastern one and divided the city in two, and on its northern bank, on a rise of ground, stood a palace whose golden roofs climbed in tiers toward the sky.

"The Founder's River," said the deacon, beside him. "Nine hundred years ago, there was no river here. The first king of Cang stood on the western wall and drew his sword, and the earth opened for a hundred li, and the water came. That is what the Mandate realm means. Not strength. Authority. The land did what he told it."

Gu Yan looked at the straight, shining line of water and tried to imagine a man whose will could cut the world like that. He could not. He could only see the grain of it, faintly, even from up here: the riverbed was lined with lines of force that had not faded in nine hundred years, holding the cut open.

"Where is Starfall Academy?" asked Gu Lan.

The deacon pointed south, across the river.

On a hill in the southern quarter, apart from the crowded rooftops, stood a cluster of dark buildings among old trees, and rising above them, so tall that its top was lost against the stars, a single slender tower. Nine stories. Each story was ringed with a band of faint light, so that the whole structure looked like a needle threaded with nine silver beads.

"The Nine-Story Star Tower," said the deacon. "Your examination is in three days. Until then you will lodge at the Academy's guesthouse in the Lantern Ward. You may go into the city. You may not get into trouble." He looked at each of the three of them. "In Cangdu, trouble is very expensive."

* * *

Trouble found him the next afternoon, at a smithy.

He had gone out alone. Gu Lan had dragged Gu Feng off toward the silk markets; Gu Yan had wanted to see how a city this size was built, and had spent the morning walking, staring at roof-trusses and bridge-piers and the extraordinary, ingenious ways Cangdu's carpenters joined timbers that had to bear six stories of tile. By early afternoon he had wandered into a district of workshops on the southern bank, where the air was thick with charcoal smoke and the ringing of hammers, and he had stopped to listen, because one of the hammers was ringing differently from the others. Heavier. Cleaner. Someone who knew exactly where to strike.

The smithy was a narrow, deep building with its front open to the street. Over the door hung a board carved with a single character: 墨. Mo.

The hammer stopped.

"I've told you," said a big voice from inside. "The iron came in late because your own warehouse sent it late. I'm not paying interest on the days I was waiting for *your* iron."

"The contract," said another voice, smoother and quieter, "does not mention whose warehouse. It mentions a date."

Gu Yan stepped closer.

Inside, by the forge, stood a broad, grey-bearded man in a leather apron, with forearms like knotted rope. In front of him, very neat in a dark blue robe embroidered at the hem with a small silver deer, stood a middle-aged man with a soft face and an account book in his hand. Behind the man in blue were three guards. Behind the blacksmith, by the anvil, was a young man.

He was big. Not tall so much as wide, built like a door, with a broad brown face and a jaw that was set hard. He held a smith's hammer loosely in one hand and was very obviously trying not to use it.

"Steward Pang," said the blacksmith, "my family has worked this forge for six generations. I've never missed a debt. Give me two months."

"The Lu clan is not unreasonable," said Steward Pang. "We would be happy to accept the anvil in lieu of the interest. It is, I understand, the only thing in this shop of real value." He glanced at the anvil, a squat block of dark metal with a faint green sheen in the grain. Spirit iron, old and dense. "Then the principal can wait."

The young man moved. "That's my great-great-grandfather's—"

"Ji," said the blacksmith sharply.

"It's fine," said Steward Pang. "Take him."

That was all. Two of the guards stepped forward, not to the anvil, but to the young man, and one of them put a hand on his shoulder. It was not a blow. It was a test. Gu Yan could see it in the lines of the guard's arm, the qi gathered in the wrist and held back: *push me, and then we can call it assault, and then it will be much worse.*

The young man pushed.

He was strong. Startlingly strong; the guard skidded back two steps and hit the doorframe. But the second guard was already moving, and the third, and this one had drawn a short iron baton, and his qi was not held back. The baton came down not at the young man but at the blacksmith's right hand, resting on the anvil. A smith's hand.

Gu Yan did not decide to move. His body had read the line of that blow and gone before he did.

He came in under the baton, caught the guard's wrist with his left hand, not hard, just at the point where the tendons crossed and the strength of the swing was gathered, and turned it. The baton's line bent. It struck the anvil's horn instead of the hand, with a clang that rang the whole shop like a bell, and the guard yelped and dropped it, shaking his numbed fingers.

For a breath, nobody moved.

The guard stared at Gu Yan. He was 4th Vein, Gu Yan judged; the qi in his arm had been dense and fast. Gu Yan was 2nd Vein. But the guard's wrist had been joined to the rest of him in a particular way, and Gu Yan's grip had found the joint. And a body refined by starlight did not bruise as easily as the guard's had expected.

"My apologies," Gu Yan said. "I thought he might hurt his hand on the anvil."

Steward Pang looked at him for the first time. It was a careful look, the look of a man reading a page of an account book for errors. Gu Yan looked back, and, out of habit, let the first node's sight settle over him.

And found nothing.

Not nothing as in weakness. Nothing as in *smoothness.* Every person Gu Yan had looked at this way, the qi lines in them had a grain, an unevenness, places where they ran strong and places where they thinned. Steward Pang's lines were there, faintly, but over them lay something like oiled silk, a sheen that the sight slid off. He could not read the man at all.

It was the first time that had happened. It made the back of his neck go cold.

"You are not from Cangdu," said Steward Pang.

"No."

"A recruit for the examination." It was not a question; he had seen the Academy's guest-token at Gu Yan's belt. "Then you will know that the Academy prefers its candidates not to begin their time here with a quarrel." He turned to the blacksmith, and his voice was exactly as mild as before. "Master Mo. The Lu clan values long relationships. Two months. With interest, of course. I'll send the revised contract." He closed the account book. "Good day."

He left. The guards followed, the one with the numbed wrist giving Gu Yan a look that promised to remember him.

* * *

The young man let out a breath like a bellows emptying and set down his hammer.

"You," he said to Gu Yan, "are either very brave or very stupid."

"I'm a carpenter's grandson," Gu Yan said. "It's mostly the second."

The young man stared at him, and then laughed, a huge, startled laugh that seemed to come up from his boots. He held out a hand. It was the size of a shovel and calloused to the texture of bark.

"Mo Ji," he said. "That's my father. You saved his hand. A smith with a broken hand is a man who used to be a smith." His face sobered. "Thank you. Really."

"Gu Yan. From Jade Ridge."

"Never heard of it."

"No one has."

Master Mo was looking at the anvil, at the bright dent the baton had left in the horn. He touched it with his thumb. "That's Lu clan for you," he said heavily. "Never a blow they can be blamed for. They didn't break my hand. You'd have broken it, slamming it down on the anvil. That's what the report would have said." He shook his head and looked at Gu Yan. "You took the exam token from the Academy? You'll be sitting with my son, then. He's been training for this since he could lift a hammer."

"Since before," said Mo Ji. "I used to lift the anvil."

"You used to try to lift the anvil."

"I lifted one corner."

Master Mo sighed in the way of a man who has been having this argument for fifteen years, and went to wash his hands.

Mo Ji walked Gu Yan back toward the river as the lanterns came on. He talked the whole way. Gu Yan learned that he was seventeen, that he had opened his 3rd Vein the month before and that his fists, when he poured his qi into them, carried the weight of earth, so that a punch landed like a falling boulder; that he was terrible at anything requiring patience; that the Lu clan was one of the four ducal clans of Cang, owned a third of the iron trade in the capital and half the debts of the southern bank; that its heir, Lu Mingyuan, was at Starfall already, eighteen and Star Sea, and that nobody had ever seen him lose his temper.

"That's what's frightening about them," Mo Ji said. "They don't shout. They just write things down."

They stopped on a bridge over the Founder's River. Below them the water glowed its steady, impossible blue, and lantern boats drifted over it, their reflections trembling. Mo Ji leaned on the rail.

"Why'd you really do it?" he asked. "Step in. You don't know us."

Gu Yan considered the question honestly.

"He swung at a craftsman's hand," he said. "My grandfather has hands like your father's. I wouldn't like to see someone swing at them."

Mo Ji nodded slowly, as if that explained everything, and perhaps for him it did.

"Then we're friends," he said. "That's how it works. Ask anyone on the southern bank. Mo family doesn't forget." He grinned. "We'll climb that stair together. Whoever gets higher buys the wine."

"I don't drink wine."

"You will in Cangdu."

* * *

That night, in the narrow room at the Academy guesthouse, Gu Yan lay awake and listened to the city breathe.

He thought about the straight blue river, and the man who had cut it. He thought about the walls, and the boat that floated on a pattern of lines, and the tower on the hill threaded with nine silver beads. He thought about Steward Pang's smooth, unreadable surface, like oiled silk over something he could not see.

A man did not wear a covering like that by accident.

He reached into the pouch at his belt and took out the oilcloth bundle. He had not opened it since Jade Ridge. He unwrapped it now in the dark, by the faint light of the lanterns outside the window, and looked at the token of red metal with its carved crescent moon.

He let the sight settle over it.

Most of the token was dead metal. But at its centre, very faint, a single line still glowed, a thin, dark-red thread curled tight on itself like a sleeping worm, and it had a quality he recognised only now, because he had felt it this very afternoon. Not its colour. Its *sheen.*

The same smooth, sliding surface. The same feeling of a thing that did not want to be read.

Gu Yan wrapped the token up again very carefully and lay a long time with it held in his closed fist, while across the river the bells of the city rang the second watch.
