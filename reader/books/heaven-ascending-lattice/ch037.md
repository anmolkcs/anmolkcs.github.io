# Chapter 37: Silver

The climb out of the pit took him most of a night.

The funnel walls were black stone, glass-smooth in places and broken in others, and the gravity at the bottom was so heavy that every handhold felt like lifting a sack of iron. He climbed with his father's sword strapped across his back and the broken jade slip tucked into the pouch beside the letter. His left shoulder burned with every reach. He went up a zhang, rested, went up another.

He did not let himself think about Mo Ji. Thinking about Mo Ji made his hands rush, and rushing hands slipped.

Instead he looked at the stone.

With the Lattice open, the funnel's walls were full of lines: the old fault-lines of the Violet Dusk's laws, layered and compressed, running in long spirals toward the rim. Where they ran close together, the rock was strong and he could trust his weight to it. Where they spread apart, there was a weakness, a crack waiting to happen, and he went around. Halfway up, he discovered that if he climbed *along* the spirals rather than straight up them, the gravity eased slightly, as though the funnel had been built to be descended and climbed by its makers in exactly that way. A ramp, for giants. He followed it.

Above him, the false sky of lanterns had faded with the Remnant. The real one showed through the funnel's mouth now: violet, starless, with the dim copper glow of the Valley's unmoving sun bleeding across one edge. Near the rim, the thick red line of the drill still ran down the wall like a vein on the back of an old man's hand. It pulsed. Every pulse, he could taste Mo Ji's qi in it.

He climbed faster.

* * *

The ring was waiting for him.

He came over the lip of the funnel on hands and knees and lay for a moment on the black stone between the spires. They towered above him, lightless needles reaching into the violet, and the shimmering lock-pattern of their lines wound between them, dense and gleaming.

The Remnant had told him what would happen. Knowing did not make it easier.

He sat up. He checked the Dusk-Veil Seal over his Star Sea, the fine nine-sided mesh he had spent three hours weaving. It held. He could feel it lying there, light and precise, over the nine gray stars in their frame.

Then he stood and walked into the gap.

The ring's lines reached for him again, as they had when he fell in. The cold vast fingers. The pause. And then, instead of parting, they *closed*—gently, precisely, around the Lattice in his sea of consciousness, the way a hand closes around a key.

And turned.

He felt it in his teeth first, then in his bones. A deep, grinding resonance, as if something enormously heavy and enormously old had shifted on its hinge. The lock-pattern of the spires flared silver all at once, all the way around the ring, a circle of light so vast that he saw it run away on either side of him into the distance and curve out of sight.

In his Star Sea, the shutters opened.

The first star went. The one that held Structure—the grain of things, the first node he had ever lit, at sixteen, watching Grandpa fit a tenon into a mortise without a nail. It had been gray since the day his Star Sea opened, a dull pewter coin that the Academy's mirror could not see. Now it split open from within like a seed husk, and out of it came *light*. Not white. Not gold. Silver—the silver of the Lattice itself, the silver of the lines, clear and cold and impossibly bright.

The second followed. Starbone. Then Sword, the star that had already held his sword intent like a blade laid on a table; it burst into silver with a sound in his mind like a sword clearing its sheath. Then Starlight, the newest, its light ringing like a chord.

Then the five unlit stars. They had no comprehensions yet; their nodes were dark. But they were part of the same frame, and the frame was waking, and they woke with it—fainter, paler, but silver too, silver to the core.

Nine stars. Nine silver stars.

He had been told, as every child in the Cang Kingdom was told, that a Nine-Star cultivator was a legend, one in a generation, if that. He had been told at the Star Mirror that he had none. He stood between the black spires at the heart of a fallen Immortal city, with nine silver stars blazing in his Star Sea, and he understood, very clearly, that neither of those things had been true about him.

He was not a Nine-Star of any kind the kingdom had a name for.

He was something the mirror had not been built to measure.

* * *

The breakthrough came on the heels of the waking, as if the stars had been holding it back.

His Star Sea *widened*. That was the only word for it. Since he had reached Early Star Sea, the sea in his dantian had been like a mountain lake, broad but bounded, its shores within reach of his awareness. Now the shores fled outward. The sea grew deeper, darker, vaster, a night sky turned inward, and his nine silver stars hung in it no longer crowded together but spaced across a great expanse, each one burning in its own region of the dark.

The qi of the inner zone—dense, heavy, the qi of a Celestial Domain's laws, a hundred thousand years thick—came pouring into him to fill the new space.

It hurt. It hurt the way Starbone Refining had hurt the first night on the workshop roof, cold and enormous, a flood through channels too narrow to hold it. His meridians strained and then *gave*, not tearing but widening, their walls stretching like the walls of a river in spring flood. His heart thundered. His skin burned and then went cold. The ache in his dislocated shoulder vanished as the qi flooded through the torn tendons and knit them; the cuts and bruises of the fall faded; the dull fog of exhaustion in his limbs burned off like mist.

He felt his body settle into a new weight. Heavier. Denser. Stronger, in a way that was not just muscle but *structure*, the whole of him more tightly joined.

Mid Star Sea.

It lasted, he guessed later, about a hundred breaths. When it was over, he was kneeling between the spires with his palms flat on the black stone, and the ring's silver light was fading, and he was afraid.

Because through the whole of it, he had felt something else. At the very edge of perception, far beyond the Valley, beyond the violet sky, beyond the World Membrane he could not yet see—something had *stirred*. The vast silver frame of the Lattice, on which the whole of existence hung, had trembled very slightly, like a spider's web when an insect brushes one far strand.

And then, slowly, it had gone still.

He looked down at his Star Sea. The Dusk-Veil Seal lay over it, fine and nine-sided, and through it his nine silver stars burned, and through it they looked like nothing—nine smudges of ordinary light that might have belonged to anyone.

*Adequate,* said the memory of Yu Xingzhou's voice.

He let out his breath, very slowly. Then he stood, and drew his father's sword, and turned toward the mist and the red light pulsing within it.

* * *

He found Cang Yi two li from the plaza.

He heard the fight first. Steel on steel, the snap of spent qi, a man's ragged shout. Then the mist parted around the base of a toppled tower and he saw the Third Prince with his back to a broken column, his red-lacquered spear in his hands and blood running from a cut above his eye. Behind him, Fang Rui sat slumped against the stone, one hand pressed to her side. In front of him stood three of Lu Hengshan's grey-robed retainers. Two more lay on the ground and did not move.

"—already told you," Cang Yi was saying, breathing hard. "If the Lu clan wants her, they'll have to go through me. And you've already seen what that costs."

"Your Highness," said the lead retainer, almost apologetically. He was a Late Star Sea, broad-shouldered, a scar through one eyebrow. "We have orders not to touch you. Please step aside."

"No."

The three of them moved together.

Gu Yan moved faster.

He had not tested the new weight of his body. He did not need to. He crossed the forty paces between the mist and the column before the retainers had taken two steps, and the lead man's eyes widened just as Gu Yan's sword came down.

He did not use a technique. He used a line. With the Lattice open—and it opened easier now, wider, the throb behind his eyes a dull murmur instead of a scream—the retainer's qi lit up like a lantern, and in the bright knot of energy at his shoulder, where his martial skill was gathering, there was a joint. Gu Yan's blade touched it, and the silver star of Sword in his Star Sea sang, and the edge of his sword intent went into the joint like a wedge into a split log.

The technique came apart. The qi scattered. The retainer cried out and staggered, his arm hanging, and Cang Yi's spear-butt took him across the temple and laid him out.

The other two had turned. One was Mid Star Sea, the other Early. Neither had any chance. The fight lasted perhaps four exchanges. When it was over, both lay groaning on the paving, and Gu Yan stood among them with his sword unbloodied, breathing evenly.

Cang Yi was staring at him.

"You were Early Star Sea three days ago," the prince said. "I watched the Star Mirror. I watched you at the Shard Beasts."

"I fell into a hole."

"A *hole*."

"A deep one." Gu Yan crouched beside Fang Rui. She looked up at him with her usual silence, but there was a question in her eyes. Her wound was a long gash under the ribs, ugly but not deep. "Can you walk, Senior Sister?"

"I can fight," said Fang Rui, which was the most words he had ever heard her say at once. She tried to rise and failed.

"Not tonight," said Cang Yi. He knelt beside her, tore a strip from his own sleeve, and pressed it to her side with brisk, practised hands. To Gu Yan he said, without looking up: "Captain Yan Zhao is somewhere east of here fighting Lu Hengshan. The old man stopped pretending a few hours ago. Half his retainers went for the disciples; the other half went for my guards. I've lost three Golden Crows." His jaw tightened. "Where are the others?"

"Lu Mingyuan has Mo Ji, Wei Zhen, and Lu Qing in a blood formation on a plaza west of here. There's a Blood Moon elder with him. Crimson Moth. Late Heaven Seal." Gu Yan stood. "Su Lingxi went to find Elder Han."

"And you?"

"I'm going to break it."

Cang Yi looked up at him for a long moment. The prince's face, streaked with blood and grime, was unreadable. Then he finished tying the bandage around Fang Rui's side, stood, and picked up his spear.

"Then I'm coming with you," he said. "Don't argue. You lost to me once. I want to see what you look like now."

* * *

They left Fang Rui hidden in the hollow of the fallen tower with a bundle of the retainers' swords and a promise to come back. They went west through the mist with the Lattice open in Gu Yan's mind and Cang Yi's spear-point glowing faintly gold ahead of them. Gu Yan followed the fault-lines, and the ripples, and the red pulse of the drill—and now he had something else, too. The stars. The Valley's strange, unfamiliar, heavy stars, which had looked down on this mist for a hundred thousand years, and which remembered, if he listened, where every light in it burned.

The plaza burned brightest of all.

They came out onto the rubble ring at its edge in the last hour before the Valley's false dawn. The formation lay below them, a hundred paces across, its red lines no longer pulsing slowly but *racing*, flickering, the light thrown up from them staining the mist the colour of a wound.

There were six disciples in the small circles now.

Mo Ji lay in the third. He was not moving. The thread of qi being pulled out of him along the channel was thick as a rope.

At the center, beside the iron basin, Crimson Moth stood with her arms spread and her red sleeves lifted like wings, and the red mist above the basin had gathered into a single turning sphere, dense and glistening, as large as a man's head.

"If the Immortal won't feed me," she was saying, to no one, to the mist, to the Valley, her voice shaking with fury, "then his little visitors will. Six young geniuses' blood refined whole, every drop, every star. It's not a Sovereign's step. But it's *something*."

At the rim of the circle, Lu Mingyuan stood with his hands folded in his sleeves, and he was not looking at the basin.

He was looking up, directly at the rubble where Gu Yan and Cang Yi crouched in the shadows. And he was smiling.

"Third Prince," he called pleasantly. "And the zero-star brother. I wondered which of you would come first."
