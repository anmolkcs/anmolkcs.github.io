# Chapter 39: Out of the Valley

They ran for a day and a half with the Valley dying around them.

The mist went first. By the time they reached the toppled tower where Fang Rui waited, it had thinned to a pale haze, and by noon it was gone altogether, and for the first time they saw the middle zone laid bare under the copper sun: a vast plain of shattered avenues and roofless halls stretching away toward the black spires, which stood on the horizon like a row of burnt matches. Then the gravity began to lurch. It swung from crushing to almost nothing and back again in the space of a few breaths, so that the disciples carrying stretchers stumbled and swore. The ripples came in waves now, unpredictable, folding the ruins like cloth. Twice Gu Yan saw a fault-line light up ahead of them with the Lattice and shouted a warning in time; once he did not, and a Golden Crow Guard who had been walking at the column's edge was simply gone, folded away, his shout cut off in the middle.

Nobody stopped. Han Qiu would not let them.

They found Gu Feng on the second morning, sitting against a broken wall at the edge of the outer zone with his sword across his knees and his cracked ribs bound with strips of his own robe. He had been walking toward the Veil for two days, alone, following the faint glow of it through the mist and then through the clear air. When he saw them coming, he stood up very carefully and bowed to Han Qiu, and then he looked past the Elder at Gu Yan, and at Mo Ji lying on a stretcher with his hand in splints, and his face did something complicated.

"He made me run," he said to Gu Yan. "I should have stayed."

"Then you'd have been in a circle," Gu Yan said. "And there'd have been seven."

Gu Feng looked at him for a moment. Then he nodded, once, and took one end of Mo Ji's stretcher from Tang Bo without being asked.

* * *

Shen Yu died in the outer zone, an hour from the Veil.

He had lost his leg to the first Shard Beast. Then Lu Hengshan's retainers, who had been charged with escorting him back to camp, had instead carried him into the middle zone and laid him in a circle, and the formation had taken more from him than from the others, because he had less to give. When the red sphere burst, the thread that had flown back into him had been thin. He had woken, briefly, and asked for water. He did not wake again.

Tang Bo, who had been his friend, carried him the rest of the way himself and would not let anyone help.

Captain Yan Zhao met them at the Veil. He had five Golden Crows left out of eight. His black-lacquered armour was split down one side, and there was a burn across his face that would scar. He knelt to Cang Yi and reported in a flat soldier's voice: Lu Hengshan had attacked his guards on the night of the ripple, with half of his retainers, in what the old man had clearly expected to be a quick ambush. It had not been quick. It had lasted until dawn.

"He is dead, Your Highness," said Yan Zhao. "By my hand. I have his head."

He did. It was wrapped in a Lu retainer's grey cloak, and Gu Yan did not look at it.

The Veil was no longer a shimmer. It had thickened into something like frosted glass, pale and humming, and its hum had risen to a whine that set every tooth in Gu Yan's head on edge. When Han Qiu put his hand against it, it gave, but slowly, like pushing into cold clay.

"Now," said the Elder. "Everyone. Don't stop."

They went through in a crush, stretchers first, the walking wounded after. Gu Yan went last but one, with Su Lingxi in front of him and Han Qiu behind. The Veil was not nothing this time. It was a long, freezing, suffocating pressure, like being pulled through a snowbank, and halfway through he felt it *tighten*—and then Han Qiu's hand was on his back and shoving, and he stumbled out onto the bare ring of earth beneath the outside world's blue sky with Su Lingxi catching his arm.

Behind them, Han Qiu stepped through. Behind Han Qiu, with a sound like a great door closing in an empty hall, the Veil went solid.

Gu Yan turned and looked back.

It was a wall again. Pale, shimmering, utterly still, stretching up into the sky. He opened the Lattice and looked at it, and the lines of the fallen Domain were there behind it, tight and ancient—but quieter now, settled, like a house after its last occupant has died. Somewhere at the heart of it, a vast golden body lay turning to stone at the bottom of a pit, beneath a sky of remembered lanterns, and a Star-Recorder had finally been relieved of his post.

Han Qiu was counting heads.

"Twelve went in," he said. "Ten came out. Shen Yu is dead. Lu Mingyuan is—" He paused. "Not here."

* * *

The return to Cangdu took five days, and on the third day, word reached them by flying talisman that Lu Mingyuan had come home.

He had appeared at the gates of the Lu clan's estate in the capital two nights earlier, the message said, gravely wounded, his right arm crippled, claiming to have been held prisoner by the Blood Moon Sect and to have escaped at great cost. The Lu clan had closed its gates. The Lu patriarch, Duke Lu Zhengyang, had requested an audience with the King.

"Of course he did," said Cang Yi, very quietly, when Han Qiu read them the message by the fire. It was the only time Gu Yan ever heard bitterness in the prince's voice.

The audience took place in the Hall of Nine Lamps on the evening after they arrived, and Gu Yan was summoned to it.

He had never been inside the royal palace. He had seen its roofs from the Academy's towers: golden tiles stacked in tiers above the city like a mountain range of the sun. Inside, the Hall of Nine Lamps was a single enormous room of red pillars and black floors, so long that its far end was dim, and at that end, on a throne carved from a single block of green jade, sat the King of the Cang Kingdom.

Gu Yan had wondered what a Mid Mandate would feel like. Now he knew.

It was not pressure, the way Crimson Moth's domain had been pressure, or Han Qiu's. It was *weight*. The air in the hall was heavier than the air outside, and it had a shape, and the shape was the King's. The light of the nine great lamps that gave the hall its name bent very slightly toward the throne, as if pulled. When the King breathed, Gu Yan felt the hall breathe with him. This was a man who had fused his Heaven Seals into a Mandate, and the Mandate let him bend the laws around him to his will, and in this room, his will was the law. Gu Yan had the sudden, unsettling certainty that if the King wished him to stop breathing, the air itself would decline to enter his lungs.

The King was perhaps fifty, broad and grey-bearded, with Cang Yi's straight brows and a face that gave away nothing at all.

Principal Wen Taiyuan stood to the right of the throne, an old scholar in plain brown robes. Han Qiu stood beside him. To the left stood the Duke of Lu.

Lu Zhengyang was a big, soft-spoken man in his sixties, with the same courteous, slightly apologetic manner as his grandson. He was Heaven Seal 8th Seal. He did not look at Gu Yan, or at Cang Yi, or at Mo Ji, who had insisted on coming with his arm in a sling and stood glowering beside Gu Feng. He looked at the King, and he bowed very low, and he told his story.

The Lu clan had been betrayed. A steward, Lu Fu, who had served the household for twenty years, had been revealed as an agent of the Blood Moon Sect. He had planted blood-puppet marks on Lu Hengshan and on young Lu Mingyuan before the expedition—marks that let the Blood Moon elder control their actions from afar. Everything that had happened in the Valley had been done by puppets. Lu Hengshan, poor Hengshan, had died without ever knowing what he did. Lu Mingyuan had broken the mark at the cost of his own arm and escaped.

The steward Lu Fu had been found that morning in his quarters, dead by his own hand, with a confession written in his own blood. The Duke produced it. It was very thorough.

"The Lu clan is shamed, Your Majesty," said Lu Zhengyang, and he knelt on the black floor. "We have harboured a serpent. We will pay any reparation the throne decides. We will compensate the families of the dead. We ask only that our name, which has served the Cang for four hundred years, not be blackened by the crime of one servant."

There was silence in the Hall of Nine Lamps.

"Disciple Lu Qing," said the King. His voice was quiet and filled the hall to its corners. "You were in the formation. What did you see?"

Lu Qing stood at the back of the Academy disciples. He had said almost nothing since the plaza. He stepped forward now, pale and thin, his eyes on the floor.

Gu Yan watched him. Lu Qing had been lying in a blood circle, awake, while his cousin explained that he would be honoured at home as a martyr. Lu Qing had heard everything.

"I—remember very little, Your Majesty," said Lu Qing. His voice shook. "I was drained. It was like a dream. I remember red light. I remember Cousin Mingyuan's voice. I don't remember what he said." A pause. "He didn't sound like himself."

He did not look up. Across the hall, the Duke of Lu did not look at him either.

Mo Ji made a sound like a kettle beginning to boil. Gu Feng put a hand on his good arm.

The King was silent for a long time.

"The Lu clan will pay fifty thousand high-grade spirit stones in reparation to the Academy and the families of the dead," he said at last. "It will surrender every member of its household for examination by the Royal Inquisitors. Lu Mingyuan will be examined for traces of a blood-puppet mark. If none are found—" he paused, and the air in the hall grew heavier "—this throne will remember."

"Your Majesty is merciful," said the Duke, and touched his forehead to the floor.

* * *

"He doesn't believe it," Cang Yi said afterward.

They were standing on the palace steps in the cold autumn dark, the prince and Gu Yan, a little apart from the others. Below them the capital's lanterns were coming on, ten thousand points of gold spreading out across the city.

"Then why—"

"Because the Lu clan holds the whole south-west of the kingdom, and four other dukes are watching to see what happens to it. Because Lu Hengshan is dead and Lu Fu is dead, and dead men make excellent culprits. Because the only living witness is a frightened boy who has to go home to that household every night." Cang Yi's jaw was tight. "Because my father can break the Lu clan, but if he does it without proof, three other dukes will decide he might break them next, and then we'll have a war, and the Blood Moon Sect will feast on it." He was silent a moment. "A Mandate can bend the air in a hall. He can't bend the kingdom. Not all of it. Not at once."

Gu Yan said nothing. He was thinking of Lu Mingyuan's face on the plaza. *If the Cang Kingdom is a pond, then being a duck in it is not much of an ambition.*

"He saw your stars," Cang Yi said. "Lu Mingyuan. The silver."

"I know."

"So did I." The prince turned his head and looked at him. "I haven't told anyone. I won't. Han Qiu told me not to, and I'd have kept quiet if he hadn't." A faint, wry smile. "And I still haven't had my rematch. I watched you fight him instead of me."

"We'll have one."

"We will."

Cang Yi went down the steps to where Yan Zhao waited with the horses. Gu Yan stood alone a while longer. Then he heard light footsteps behind him and turned.

Su Lingxi had her compass open in her hand. She did not say anything at first. She simply came and stood beside him at the top of the steps and held it out between them, so that the lantern-light fell on its face.

For months, every time she had held it near him, the needle had done nothing. It had slid past him as if he were not there.

Now it moved.

Slowly, the needle turned toward him, and then—not toward him. *Past* him. It swung until it pointed straight upward, at the night sky above the palace roofs, and there it stayed, trembling very faintly, like a plumb line hung from a beam.

"Ever since the plaza," Su Lingxi said quietly. "Ever since you showed it. I don't know what it means. The compass reads fate. It shows you the threads that tie a person to the world." She lifted her eyes from the needle to his face. "Everyone's threads go out, Gu Yan. Sideways. To other people, other places. Yours doesn't."

She looked up at the sky.

"Yours goes *up*."
