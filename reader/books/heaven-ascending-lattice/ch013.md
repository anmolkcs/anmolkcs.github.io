# Chapter 13: The Entrance Examination (I)

The square before Starfall Academy's gate could have held all of Jade Ridge Town, and on the morning of the examination it was full.

Gu Yan had never seen so many cultivators in one place. They stood shoulder to shoulder in the grey dawn light, eight or nine thousand of them at least, young men and women from every corner of the kingdom: clan heirs in brocade with retainers holding parasols over them; farm boys in patched hemp with nothing but a staff; a group of shaven-headed youths from some frontier monastery standing in perfect silence; girls in the green of the southern lake country, with swords on their backs and their hair pinned with river-pearls. The air hummed with qi. With so many cultivators breathing in one place, it had gone thick and restless, like the air before a storm.

"I'm going to be sick," said Gu Lan cheerfully. "Not from nerves. From the smell. Who wears that much plum-blossom oil?"

"Southern Wu family," said Mo Ji, who had found them at the edge of the square within minutes, as though he had been sniffing for them. "They bathe in it. It's supposed to calm the spirit."

"It's not calming mine."

Gu Feng was silent, his gaze fixed on the gate. It was a single arch of grey stone, plain and enormous, and beyond it the ground rose in a long slope to the Academy's halls. And between the gate and the halls, climbing the slope, ran the stair.

It was white. That was all that could be said of it from here. A broad white staircase, perhaps ten zhang wide, going straight up the hill without a turn, its steps so many that they blurred together into a smooth pale ribbon. At the top, very small, a terrace and a pavilion. Beyond, the Star Tower, its nine silver bands faint in the morning light.

"Nine hundred and ninety-nine steps," said Mo Ji. "Every step, the pressure doubles. Or triples. Or—nobody agrees. My cousin's friend said he got to step three hundred and his ears bled."

"Your cousin's friend," said Gu Feng, "sounds like an unreliable source."

"He's a very reliable source. He can't hear in his left ear."

* * *

A bell rang from somewhere inside the Academy, deep and long, and the square went quiet.

An old woman in grey robes stood on the gate's arch. She did not raise her voice; it simply arrived in every ear, as close as a whisper.

"Starfall Academy admits five hundred disciples each year," she said. "The examination has three stages. The first is the Qi-Pressure Stair. It measures cultivation, will, and the body's ability to endure. Your rank on the stair is your first score. You may stop at any time. If you fall, the stair will put you down gently. If you try to climb past what your body can bear, it will put you down less gently." She paused. "Do not try to climb past what your body can bear."

The gate opened.

The first thousand candidates surged forward, and the rest followed in waves.

Gu Yan let himself be carried with the crowd to the foot of the stair. He set his foot on the first step and felt it at once: a weight settling on his shoulders, light as a folded blanket. On the second step, a little heavier. On the tenth, it was like carrying a sack of millet. Around him, candidates were taking the first hundred steps at a run, laughing, racing each other.

"Slow," said Gu Feng beside him, not looking at him. "Everyone who runs at the bottom stops in the middle."

"Since when are you giving me advice?"

"Since my grandfather paid a man in Three Wells two silver taels to tell me about it."

Gu Yan smiled and climbed at a walk.

By the hundredth step the crowd had thinned. The pressure had become a living thing, not a weight on the shoulders but a weight on everything, pressing down on his skull and his lungs and the soles of his feet, as though the air itself had become water and he was walking along the bottom of a lake. The runners were slowing now, red-faced, some already sitting down on the steps with their heads between their knees. A boy ahead of him in fine silk took one more step, went white, and simply slid backward down the stair as if a hand had picked him up by the collar, to be set down gently at the bottom among a crowd of others.

By two hundred, Gu Lan stopped.

"That's it," she said. She was 1st Vein, only a few weeks opened, and her face was grey with effort. "No, that's really it. If I take another step, I'll throw up on the step, and then everyone behind me will have to step in it." She sat down. "Go on. I'll cheer."

"Two hundred is good," said Gu Yan.

"Two hundred is fine. Go."

At three hundred, Mo Ji began to roar.

It was not a word. It was the sound a bull makes, and he made it every time he lifted his foot, and with every roar the earth-qi in his legs flared brown and heavy, and he climbed. He climbed like a man pushing a cart up a hill, not elegant, not efficient, but unstoppable. Candidates around him stared. One or two laughed. He ignored them and went up.

Gu Feng was beside Gu Yan still, climbing in a steady, disciplined rhythm, his breathing measured. Gu Yan matched him. His own body was holding up better than he had expected. The starlight in his bones carried the pressure the way seasoned oak carries a roof: it flexed, but it did not crack. It was his meridians that suffered. The two he had opened burned like overfilled pipes, the qi in them pushed back and churning under the pressure.

At four hundred and fifty, Gu Feng stopped.

He did not fall. He stood on the step with his fists clenched and his legs trembling and his teeth set, and he tried to lift his foot, and could not. He tried again. Sweat ran off his jaw onto the white stone.

"Gu Feng—"

"Don't." His voice came out strangled. "Don't say anything. I'm not—I'm stopping. I'm *choosing* to stop." He breathed. "Stopping is knowing when the work is done. Isn't that what you said?"

"Yes."

"Then go on, carpenter." Something that was almost a smile. "I'll be very angry if you stop below me."

Gu Yan went on.

* * *

At five hundred steps, the world narrowed to the stair.

He could not hear the crowd below. He could hear his own heart, and the scrape of his sandals, and, somewhere above him, Mo Ji still roaring, though more hoarsely now. There were perhaps two hundred candidates still climbing, spread over the steps ahead and behind. The pressure no longer felt like water. It felt like stone. It felt like the stair was trying to fold him in half and press him flat into its surface like a pressed flower.

Five hundred and twenty. Five hundred and thirty. His vision began to shake at the edges.

*This is wrong,* he thought, distantly. *This is just pushing.*

He had been climbing the way Mo Ji climbed. The way everyone climbed. Bracing against the pressure, holding it off with flesh and qi and stubbornness, as if it were a single great weight to be borne. But nothing in the world was a single thing. Everything had a grain.

He stopped on step five hundred and thirty-four, and, with what felt like the last of his strength, opened the first node's sight.

The stair lit up.

Not the stone. The pressure. It was made of lines, as everything was: fine, dense threads of force running down the slope from the top, thousands upon thousands of them, like the fibres in a plank. And they were not uniform. They came in pulses. A wave pressed down from above, and passed, and for a fraction of a breath behind it the lines thinned, and then the next wave came. Like the rhythm of a saw. Like breathing.

Everyone was climbing against the waves. Bracing through them.

He waited. Felt the wave pass over him, crushing. Felt it thin.

And stepped, in the thinning.

The pressure was still there. But for that one step it was like walking through a doorway instead of a wall. He was on five hundred and thirty-five and his knees had not buckled. He waited for the next wave, let it roll over him, bowed his head beneath it the way a reed bows under a current. Thinning. Step.

Five hundred and thirty-six.

It was not easy. The waves were close together, and they grew closer the higher he went, and his mind ached from holding the sight open, a dull spike behind his eyes. But he climbed. Step after step, in the rhythm of the stair's own breathing. He passed a girl from the southern lake country who had sat down with her sword across her knees. He passed one of the shaven-headed monks, who was standing with his eyes closed, chanting under his breath, and who opened one eye to watch him go by with an expression of mild interest.

At six hundred he passed Mo Ji.

The big youth was on his hands and knees, gasping, his fists braced on the step above him. His face was purple. He looked up as Gu Yan came level, and his eyes widened.

"How," he croaked, "are you *walking*?"

"Badly," said Gu Yan. "Breathe with it. Don't push. When it thins, move."

"When what—"

A wave passed. "Now."

Mo Ji, to his eternal credit, did not argue. He heaved himself up one step. The next wave hit him and he grunted. "That's—" He gasped. "That's *harder*. How is that—"

"Wait for the gap."

They climbed four more steps together. Then Mo Ji's arms gave way and the stair picked him up very gently and slid him away down the slope, shouting something that sounded like *you owe me wine.*

* * *

At seven hundred, the stair began to work on his meridians.

It had been churning the qi in them for some time. Now the pressure on them was so great that the two opened channels felt like they would burst; and at the same time, between the waves, the stair's own force was flowing *through* him, through every gap in his body, pushing at every closed door.

He saw it the way he had seen the seal of his second meridian on the carriage roof. The third meridian, running from his navel down toward his right hip. Sealed. Silted. And the stair's pressure-lines, as they passed through his body, were pushing against that seal in rhythm, wave after wave, like a battering ram that did not know what it was battering.

*Use it.*

He did not climb. He stood on step seven hundred and thirteen and closed his eyes, and he let the next wave come down on him, and instead of bowing under it, he took it in.

It was like swallowing a millstone. He felt the pressure flood through his body, through the starlight-hardened bones that could carry it, into his two opened channels, which flared white with agony. He gathered it, folded it the way Grandpa folded a long cut into a short one, aimed it along the grain of the seal—

The third meridian tore open.

There was a sound in his head like a board splitting along its whole length. Qi roared down through his belly into his right leg and back up, a hot torrent, and joined the slow circling wheel in his chest. The wheel grew. It spun faster, three spokes now instead of two, and the qi it drew from the air was no longer smoke drifting toward a chimney but a steady wind. He could feel the stair's pressure differently now, too: not lighter, but held, as if his body had grown a third leg and stopped wobbling.

3rd Vein.

He opened his eyes. He was trembling all over and a thin line of blood was running from his nose, warm on his lip. He wiped it with the back of his hand and climbed on.

* * *

He reached step seven hundred and ninety-one before the stair finally said no.

It did not throw him. He simply found, between one wave and the next, that his body had nothing left to give. His mind was a sheet of white pain. The sight had gone; he could no longer see the lines at all, only white stone and the blurred figures of the few dozen candidates still above him. Far above, near the top, three or four figures were still climbing, and one of them, alone, was so near the terrace that he could have reached out and touched it.

Gu Yan sat down on the step.

The stair lifted him, kindly, and he slid down through air that grew lighter and lighter until he was set on his feet at the bottom, among hundreds of others, and Gu Lan was hitting him on the arm and shouting.

"Seven hundred and ninety-one! I counted! You were in the top fifty, the proctor said, you were—you're *bleeding*—"

"Nosebleed," said Gu Yan. His tongue felt thick. "It's nothing."

Gu Feng was sitting on the ground nearby with his back against a pillar. He looked up at Gu Yan, and his face did something complicated, and then he simply nodded once. Mo Ji was lying flat on his back on the paving with his arms spread wide, staring at the sky.

"Seven hundred and eighty-four," Mo Ji said to the sky. "I was told by the proctor. Seven hundred and eighty-four. Seven steps." He turned his head. "I'm buying the wine. I'm going to buy so much wine. You're going to drink all of it and suffer."

Gu Yan laughed, which hurt.

Up on the stair, someone had reached the top. A roar went up from the crowd at the foot, and he looked and saw a single figure in dark red standing on the terrace, small and straight, looking down. The gong in the pavilion rang once.

"Third Prince," someone said nearby, in a hushed voice. "Cang Yi. Nine hundred and ninety-nine. First try."

Gu Yan looked at the tiny red figure on the terrace and felt nothing so simple as envy. He felt as he had on the ridge above the Central Plain. *There it is. That's how far up the stairs go.*

Then something made him turn his head.

At the edge of the square, by one of the gate pillars, stood a girl in pale blue. She was slight and very still, her hair bound with a plain silver pin, and in her open palm she held a small round object of white jade that caught the light. A compass. Its needle glinted.

She was not looking at the stair, or at the prince on the terrace. She was looking at him. Directly, and with an expression he could not read at all, the expression of someone who has added up a column of figures three times and got a different answer each time.

When their eyes met, she did not look away. She looked down at the compass in her hand, frowned slightly, and closed her fingers over it.

Then the crowd shifted between them, and she was gone.
