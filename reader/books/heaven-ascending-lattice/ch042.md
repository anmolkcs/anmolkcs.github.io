# Chapter 42: The Envoy of Taixu

It took the ship most of a morning to cross the last hundred li, and by the time it reached the city, all of Cangdu was on its roofs.

Gu Yan watched from the Observatory terrace, which Su Lingxi had somehow arranged for them to stand on, along with Mo Ji, Gu Lan, and half the Observatory's junior astronomers pretending to take measurements. Below them the lantern boats on the Mandate River had stopped drifting. The markets had emptied. On the walls, a hundred zhang high, the soldiers of the city guard stood in their ranks with their faces turned up like children.

The ship was the size of a palace.

That was not a figure of speech. Gu Yan had walked past the Royal Palace of the Cang Kingdom a dozen times, and he knew roughly how far it was from the Gate of Heavenly Peace to the rear wall of the inner court: something over two li. The ship was longer. Its hull was a pale, silvery wood he did not recognise, layered in overlapping plates like the scales of a fish, and its nine decks rose one above the other in tiers of carved railings and curling eaves, each deck a little narrower than the one below, so that the whole vessel looked like a mountain someone had turned on its side and taught to float. Instead of sails it carried three enormous fans of woven light, half-transparent, that turned slowly and threw rainbows on the clouds.

"It's not *possible*," said Mo Ji. "Something that big. What's holding it up?"

Gu Yan was trying to see.

He had made the mistake, as it first approached, of looking at it properly. The lines had come — and kept coming. Formation-lines ran through every plank of the hull. They did not lie side by side as they did in the academy's walls; they were woven, layer through layer, thousands of strands crossing and recrossing and folding back on themselves in patterns so dense that they seemed to shimmer. There were formations inside formations. There were lines that seemed to go *inward*, into a direction he had no word for, and come back out somewhere else. For a moment he had the dizzy sense that he was not looking at one ship but at a ship that contained a hundred smaller ships folded up inside it like paper.

Something warm ran over his lip. He wiped it with the back of his hand and found it red.

Su Lingxi silently passed him a handkerchief.

"Don't look so hard," she murmured. "Some things are not built to be understood from the outside."

"What does your compass say?"

She glanced down at the jade disk in her palm. The needle, which usually trembled faintly toward whatever person or object she was reading, was pointing steadily at the ship — and past it, west, toward the far heart of the world. "That it came from somewhere very large," she said, "and that it is going back."

* * *

The ship stopped above the Heavenward Plaza, a vast square of white stone before the palace where coronations were held. It did not descend. It simply hung there, a thousand zhang up, and its shadow covered the whole plaza and three streets on either side, so that noon became dusk.

From its lowest deck, a single figure stepped out into the air and walked down.

He walked as a man walks down stairs, unhurried, one foot after the other, except that there were no stairs. By the time he reached the plaza, the court of the Cang Kingdom was already arranged to meet him: ministers in purple and crimson, the dukes of the great clans, the Principal of Starfall Academy and his elders in black, and at their head, on the steps of the palace itself, the King.

Gu Yan had seen the King twice before, both times at a distance. He was a broad-shouldered man in his sixties with a short grey beard and the stillness of someone who had not needed to hurry in a very long time. He was Mid Mandate. Within the borders of the Cang Kingdom there was no one, living or dead, who could make him bow.

He bowed now. Not deeply — a measured inclination of the head and shoulders, one ruler to the representative of a greater power. Behind him the whole court bent like wheat in a wind.

From the Observatory terrace Gu Yan heard Gu Lan draw a breath.

The envoy returned the bow, a little less deeply. He was not at all what Gu Yan had expected. He looked about fifty, with a lined, pleasant, slightly tired face, and he wore a plain robe of undyed grey linen with no ornament except a jade token at his belt carved with two characters: *Tai Xu*. He might have been a schoolmaster, or a prosperous tea merchant on his way to visit relatives.

"Qin Shang," said Su Lingxi quietly, beside him. "Ninth Seal of Heaven Seal. They say he is one step from Mandate and has been for twenty years, because the Holy Land keeps sending him on errands."

"Errands," said Mo Ji. "Like *this*?"

"Like this."

Gu Yan let himself look at the envoy, carefully, the way one tests ice. He reached for the lines.

There were none. Or rather, there was nothing he could read. Looking at Qin Shang was like looking at a mountain at night: he knew it was there, he could feel the size of it pressing against the dark, but there was no surface for his eyes to catch on. The envoy's qi did not leak. His body did not betray its own structure. He was simply *closed*, the way a sealed jar is closed.

Gu Yan withdrew at once.

Down in the plaza, Qin Shang's head turned. It was a casual movement, the glance of a man looking over a crowd, and his eyes passed across the Observatory terrace with the same mild interest as they passed across the rooftops and the walls. But for the space of half a heartbeat, they paused. Gu Yan felt, very faintly, the Remnant's seal over his Star Sea grow cold, like a shutter when the wind leans on it.

Then the envoy's gaze moved on, and he was greeting the King, and Gu Yan let out a breath he had not known he was holding.

* * *

The address was given that evening in the great hall of Starfall Academy, because the Tournament was the academy's to host and because, Principal Wen Taiyuan said dryly in his introduction, "there is no other room in the city with a ceiling high enough for what our guest is going to tell you."

Every inner-court disciple was there, and every elder, and representatives of every clan whose sons and daughters had entered the Tournament. The Lu clan sat in the third row, the patriarch's chair conspicuously empty. Lu Mingyuan was there, pale and composed in sky-blue silk, and did not look at Gu Yan once. Two months ago the Lu clan had stood before the throne and sworn it knew nothing of any blood formation in Fallen Star Valley; a steward named Lu Fu had been found dead by his own hand with a confession written in blood, and Lu Mingyuan had returned to the academy with his reputation dented and his position untouched. Watching the back of his head, Gu Yan found that he was not angry. He was waiting.

Qin Shang spoke without notes and without raising his voice, and it carried to every corner of the hall.

"I am told," he began, "that in the Cang Kingdom it is considered a great achievement to ignite seven stars before the age of twenty."

A murmur. Several heads turned toward the royal bench, where Third Prince Cang Yi sat beside his elder brothers with an expression of polite attention.

"It is a great achievement," Qin Shang went on. "Please do not mistake me. I have visited eleven kingdoms in the last two years. In most of them there was no one at all under twenty with seven stars. Your prince would be the pride of any of them." He paused. "The Taixu Holy Land has roughly thirty thousand disciples at Star Sea or above. Perhaps a thousand of them have seven stars. Perhaps forty have eight. In the last century we have had two with nine, and one of them is dead."

He said it the way another man might mention the price of rice. The hall had gone so quiet that Gu Yan could hear the lamp flames.

"I tell you this not to shame you," the envoy said, "but so that you understand what you are reaching for. The Eastern Wastes holds a hundred and eighty kingdoms. The Holy Land watches all of them. Every ten years we send someone like me to every one of them, and from each we take a handful of the young — three, usually, sometimes none. Those we take come to a place where they are no longer the brightest. Some of them find that unbearable and go home. Some of them die. Some of them —" he smiled slightly — "become people the rest of the Eastern Wastes learns to be careful of. Our Holy Land Master is a Sovereign of the Third Step. When he was nineteen, he was the second-best fighter in a kingdom smaller than yours."

He let that sit.

"Three of you," Qin Shang said. "The top three of your Tournament will be presented to me. Whether I take them is my decision. Whether they come is theirs." He nodded to the Principal. "That is all."

The hall stayed silent a moment longer. Then, in a rush, everyone began to talk at once.

Gu Yan sat very still in the middle of it. *Thirty thousand.* He tried to imagine it: thirty thousand Star Sea cultivators, more than the whole Cang Kingdom held, gathered in one place. And above them, Heaven Seal elders by the hundreds, Mandate experts by the dozen, and somewhere at the summit, a man who had climbed to the very peak of the Lower World — to the realm that could set foot upon the Sky Pillar.

And beyond that, the Remnant had said, in his cracked and laughing voice, there was more. There was always more.

His father had gone that way.

* * *

Later, when the hall had emptied and the lamps on the Observatory roof had been trimmed for the night watch, Gu Yan and Su Lingxi stood at the parapet and looked at the ship.

It still hung over the Heavenward Plaza, lit now from within, a thousand windows glowing amber against the dark, so that it seemed a second city floating above the first. Its fans of light had slowed. They turned in the night like the wings of some vast, sleeping moth.

"Thirty thousand," said Su Lingxi.

"I was thinking the same thing."

"No, you weren't. You were thinking about your father." She did not look at him. "You always get the same line between your eyebrows."

He touched his forehead, half-amused. "Can your compass see that too?"

"I don't need a compass to see that." She turned the jade disk over in her fingers. "When I was small, my grandfather told me that the Observatory exists because the Su family once had an ancestor who reached the Holy Land and came back. She spent the rest of her life on this roof, charting the sky, trying to work out how far away it was. She said that from the Holy Land, on a clear night, you could see that the stars were not where they seemed. That there was something in front of them." She paused. "We have never found it. We only have her charts."

"The World Membrane," said Gu Yan.

"Perhaps." She looked at him at last. "If you are chosen — when you are chosen — you will go."

It was not a question. He answered it anyway. "Yes."

"Good," she said. And then, after a moment, more quietly, so that he almost lost it under the wind: "So will I."

Neither of them said anything after that. Below, the city's lanterns burned on the river, small and gold and very far away.

* * *

Han Qiu found him on the stairs going down.

The elder was in his night robe, which Gu Yan had never seen before, and carried no sword, which he had also never seen before. He looked, for the first time since Gu Yan had known him, uncertain.

"Walk with me," he said.

They walked along the outer gallery of the Star Tower in silence until they reached a place where the wind was loud enough to cover voices. Then Han Qiu stopped.

"The envoy dined with the Principal after the address," he said. "I was there. They spoke of the Tournament, the Lu clan, the Blood Moon Sect on our western border. Ordinary matters." He was looking out at the ship, not at Gu Yan. "At the end, as he rose to leave, Qin Shang said one more thing. He said it lightly, as if it had just occurred to him."

Gu Yan waited.

"He said: 'One of your disciples carries a seal that no one in this kingdom could have made. Nor, I think, anyone in the Holy Land.'" Han Qiu turned his head. "Then he thanked the Principal for the wine and went back to his ship."

The wind pulled at their robes. Far above them, in the floating city, one amber window went dark.
