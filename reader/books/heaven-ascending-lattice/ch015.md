# Chapter 15: The Entrance Examination (III)

"I don't know," Gu Yan said.

It was the only safe answer, and it had the advantage of being mostly true. He did not know why the needle hung still. He could guess. He could feel the Lattice at the back of his mind, nine nodes in their small square frame, and he remembered the grid he had glimpsed in the illusion going out and out forever, and he thought that perhaps a thing woven into *that* was not woven into anything a jade compass had been built to read.

But Grandpa's voice was louder than any guess. *Never speak of lines to anyone.*

Su Lingxi looked at him for a long moment. "You're lying," she said. "But not very much. You don't know, and you suspect something, and you won't tell me what."

"That's very precise."

"I am a very precise person." She hung the compass back on her sash. "It doesn't matter. If we're both admitted, I will be watching you." She said it the way someone else might say *I will be taking notes on the weather.* "Good evening, Gu Yan."

She bowed the exact correct degree again, and walked away into the dusk.

Mo Ji, who had come up behind him at some point and heard the end of it, let out a long low whistle.

"What," said Gu Yan.

"Nothing. Nothing." Mo Ji's broad face was creased with delight. "Only, my cousin's friend says the Observatory girls never talk to anyone outside the Observatory. And you've been in Cangdu four days."

"She thinks I'm a broken instrument."

"Better than being nothing." Mo Ji clapped him on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him. "Come on. Wine. I owe you seven steps of it."

* * *

The duels were held in the Academy's Martial Field, a vast sunken arena on the eastern slope, its tiers of stone seating cut directly into the hillside. Fifty fighting rings had been marked on the sand below with lines of white chalk, each with its own proctor in grey. The fifteen hundred surviving candidates were drawn in pairs by lot. Each would fight once. Winners were not guaranteed admission, nor losers refused; the proctors were not scoring who won, said Elder Ruan, but *how.*

The stands were crowded. Disciples of the Academy had come to watch, hundreds of them in the blue-grey robes of the outer court and the deeper blue of the inner, lounging on the stone, eating sunflower seeds, making bets. Here and there sat older figures, teachers and elders, whose presence made the air around them feel heavier.

Gu Yan's lot came up in the second hour, in the thirty-third ring.

His opponent was already waiting when he stepped over the chalk. A young man of nineteen or so, lean and long-armed, in a dark green robe embroidered at the hem with a tiny silver deer. He held a spear of black wood with a narrow steel head. His qi sat in him like a coiled spring, dense and compact: 4th Vein, Gu Yan judged, close to 5th.

"Qu Hai," said the young man, with a short bow. "Of the Qu family, in service to the Lu clan."

A silver deer. The same badge as Steward Pang. Gu Yan bowed in return. "Gu Yan. Jade Ridge."

"I know." Qu Hai's voice was quite neutral. "Steward Pang mentioned you."

So. Gu Yan did not let it show on his face. He glanced up, without meaning to, at the stands above the thirty-third ring.

In the shade of a pavilion there, a little apart from the crowd, sat a young man in white.

He was perhaps eighteen. He sat very straight, with a folded fan resting on his knee, and his face was fine-featured, pleasant, and calm. Two other disciples sat a respectful distance behind him. He was not looking at Gu Yan in particular. He was simply looking at the ring, with the patient, attentive air of a man watching a game of weiqi from a few moves in.

Gu Yan did not need anyone to tell him who it was.

"Begin," said the proctor.

* * *

Qu Hai came in fast and low, and the spear came with him.

It was a good spear-art. Gu Yan knew that within three exchanges. The black spear did not stab so much as *flow*, a continuous circling motion that kept its tip always moving and always threatening, so that every thrust grew naturally out of the last. Qi ran down the shaft in a smooth green current and gathered at the steel head, and when it struck the sand beside Gu Yan's foot, the sand burst upward a full zhang.

He gave ground. He drew his father's sword and gave ground, parrying, turning the spear aside with the flat of the iron, each block jarring his arm to the shoulder. The Flowing Cloud Sword was a technique of yielding and redirection; he had drilled its forms a thousand times in the clan yard and on the road; but Qu Hai was a full realm-step above him and had been training with that spear for a decade, and every time Gu Yan tried to flow around a thrust, the spear was already curling back toward him.

He took a cut across the forearm. Then another across the ribs, shallow, which stung like a wasp. In the stands someone laughed.

*Stop fighting the spear,* he told himself. *Look at the man.*

He opened the sight.

Qu Hai lit up in lines. The green qi spiralling down his arms; the way the spear's circling motion began not in his hands but in his hips and the soles of his feet; the rhythm of it, smooth and unbroken. A beautiful structure. Every part supporting every other part, like a well-built roof.

But a roof had a ridge-beam. Every structure had the one join that everything else rested on.

There. When the spear drew back to begin a new circle, Qu Hai's weight shifted onto his rear foot for the space of half a breath, and all the lines of the technique gathered there, at the ball of his left foot, like ropes gathered in a single hand. For that half-breath, everything depended on that foot.

Gu Yan let the next thrust come. He did not parry. He turned his body sideways and let the steel head slide past his chest so close it tore his robe, and as the spear drew back to circle, he stepped in. Not toward Qu Hai's body. Toward his left foot.

The iron sword swept low.

It did not cut. He turned it at the last instant and struck flat, with the full weight of a Starbone-refined arm behind it, against the side of Qu Hai's planted ankle.

Qu Hai's foot went out from under him. And because all the lines of his technique were gathered at that point, all of them went with it. The spear's circle broke. The green qi on its shaft sputtered. For one moment the young man was simply falling sideways, his spear swinging wide, his guard open.

Gu Yan's sword tip stopped a finger's breadth from his throat.

The ring was quiet.

"Yield," said Qu Hai, after a moment, from the sand. His voice was perfectly calm.

"Gu Yan," said the proctor. He wrote something.

Gu Yan lowered the sword and held out his hand. Qu Hai looked at it, then took it and let himself be pulled up. He tested his ankle, winced, and looked at Gu Yan with an expression that was not anger. It was closer to reassessment.

"You weren't looking at the spear," he said.

"No."

Qu Hai nodded slowly. He picked up his weapon, bowed, and walked out of the ring, limping only slightly.

Gu Yan glanced up at the pavilion.

The young man in white had opened his fan. He was fanning himself gently, his face as calm as before. As Gu Yan watched, he turned his head very slightly toward one of the disciples behind him and said something, and the disciple bowed. Then the young man's eyes moved, without hurry, and came to rest on Gu Yan.

They were pleasant eyes. Interested. The eyes of a man who has noticed that one stone on the board is not where he expected it to be.

He inclined his head a fraction, courteously, as one does to an acquaintance across a street. Then he looked away.

"Lu Mingyuan," muttered Mo Ji, when Gu Yan came back to the stands to have his arm bound. "He nodded at you. Why did he nod at you? Lu Mingyuan doesn't nod at people."

"He's being polite."

"That's what I'm worried about."

* * *

In the fourth hour, the whole arena stood up.

It began at the far end of the field, a ripple of movement through the stands, a sound like wind in a bamboo grove. Then everyone was craning to look at the twelfth ring.

The Third Prince had stepped into it.

He was seventeen, a little taller than Gu Yan, in a plain robe of dark red with no ornament but a jade clasp at the collar. He carried no weapon. His face was grave, square-jawed, unremarkable except for the eyes, which were very steady. He bowed to his opponent with exactly the same courtesy Gu Yan had seen Qu Hai give: no more, no less.

His opponent was the shaven-headed monk from the stair, the one who had opened one eye to watch Gu Yan pass. Gu Yan had heard since that the monk was from the Stone Bell Monastery on the northern frontier, that he was twenty and at the Peak of Vein Awakening, a hair's breadth from the Star Sea, and that he had broken a training post as thick as a man's waist with one palm.

"Brother Huijing," said Cang Yi. "I've read of your monastery's Diamond Palm. I'd be honoured if you didn't hold back."

The monk smiled serenely. "Your Highness is kind. This humble one will try not to embarrass you."

"Begin," said the proctor, who looked as though he would rather be anywhere else.

The monk struck first. His whole body seemed to swell; his skin took on a dull bronze sheen, and his palm came forward with a sound like a temple bell, the air in front of it folding and booming. It was a tremendous blow. Gu Yan, three hundred paces away, felt the wind of it on his face.

Cang Yi raised one hand.

There was a flash. Not of fire, not of lightning; of *starlight,* a cold silver-white glare that lit the whole twelfth ring for one instant as bright as noon. Gu Yan, without thinking, had the sight open, and in that instant he saw something he had never seen before: in the prince's lower belly, where a cultivator's dantian lay, a dark space like a small night sky, and in it, burning, stars. He saw them only for a heartbeat before his eyes watered and the sight blew out like a candle in a gust. He did not count them. He could not. There were *many.*

When he could see again, the monk was sitting on the sand outside the chalk line, ten paces away, looking surprised. His bronze sheen was gone. He was not hurt; he had simply been moved, as a man might pick up a chair and set it somewhere else.

Cang Yi lowered his hand. He walked across the ring, bowed to the monk, and offered him his hand to rise.

"Your palm was very strong," he said. "If I had only been at Vein Awakening, I would have lost."

The monk took the hand, stood, and bowed back deeply. "Your Highness," he said, "is a liar. But a very courteous one."

The arena erupted.

"Star Sea," people were saying all around Gu Yan. "Early Star Sea, at seventeen—" "My uncle says seven stars, he heard it from the palace—" "Seven? Impossible, there hasn't been a seven-star since—" "Six, at least six, did you see the light—"

Gu Yan sat still in the noise, with his bound arm aching, and looked at the prince walking calmly out of the ring.

Star Sea. A realm above. He had seen it now, with the sight: a sky inside a person. He thought of the drawing in the dirt by the caravan fire, of the deacon's voice. *In the Taixu Holy Land, a Star Sea cultivator is a servant who sweeps the courtyards.*

The staircase went up, and up, and up.

* * *

The results were posted at sunset on a wall of white plaster by the Academy gate, in characters a hand high. Five hundred names, in order of score.

Cang Yi's name stood first. Nobody was surprised.

Gu Yan's was two hundred and twelfth. Mo Ji's, four hundred and ninth; he read it four times, and then picked Gu Yan up off the ground in a bear hug. Gu Feng was three hundred and fortieth, and stood in front of the wall for a long while in silence, and then said, quietly, "Good," and nothing more. Gu Lan was four hundred and ninety-sixth.

"Four hundred and ninety-*six*," she said. "Four from the bottom. That's the best kind of rank. Nobody expects anything from me. I can be a delightful surprise for years."

Below the main list was a shorter one, for candidates admitted under the Academy's special agreements with the great houses of the kingdom: the Royal Observatory, the Ministry of Rites, the Imperial Alchemy Bureau. At the top of the Observatory's list, by a wide margin, was a single name.

Su Lingxi.

Gu Yan read it, and then, on some instinct, looked around.

She was standing at the far edge of the crowd, alone, looking not at the wall but at him. When she saw that he had seen her, she lifted the white jade compass on its silk cord, very slightly, so that it caught the last of the sunset.

Then she turned and walked up the hill toward the Academy, toward the dark halls under the ancient trees, and the tall, slender tower threaded with nine silver beads that was just now beginning, in the gathering dusk, to glow.
