# Chapter 46: The Lu Clan Moves

Lu Wenhe was not what Gu Yan had imagined either.

He had pictured the patriarch of the Lu clan as an older Lu Mingyuan — elegant, cold, sky-blue silk. The man who walked along the edge of the central platform the next morning, before the quarterfinals, was short and round-shouldered and walked with a cane, and wore a brown scholar's robe with ink on the cuffs. He had a mild, crumpled face, like a grandfather in a village teahouse. He stopped at each of the four great formation pillars that anchored the platform's protective wall, laid his hand flat against the stone, and closed his eyes for a time, as a physician feels for a pulse. Then he nodded to the Ministry formation masters who followed him, said something polite, and moved on.

Heaven Seal, Eighth Seal. Gu Yan could feel it from the tunnel mouth, fifty zhang away — not as pressure, the way the envoy's presence pressed, but as weight, the way one feels a deep pool in the dark without seeing it.

"He's checking them for flaws," said Mo Ji, beside him. "After what happened with Lu Kang. To show the Lu clan has nothing to hide." He sounded unconvinced.

"Maybe," said Gu Yan.

He let the lines come, carefully. The formation pillars were old, eight centuries old, and their lines were deep and slow, like the roots of ancient trees. Where Lu Wenhe's palm had rested on each one, nothing had changed that Gu Yan could see. No new line had been carved. No old one had been broken.

But on the third pillar, as Lu Wenhe lifted his hand away, a single strand of the formation's oldest line had shifted. Not broken. *Loosened* — the way a peg works a hair's breadth out of its mortise, so that the joint looks sound but will give at the first real load.

Gu Yan watched the patriarch walk away down the row of pillars, tapping his cane, and said nothing. There was nothing to say that anyone would believe. A loosened joint in an eight-hundred-year-old formation looked exactly like age.

He went to find Han Qiu instead.

* * *

The quarterfinals began at the second bell.

Gu Yan fought first, against Wei Shan — the Northern Garrison captain who had beaten Gu Feng in the round of thirty-two — a square, weathered man of twenty-four at Peak Star Sea with five stars, who fought with a heavy shield and a short war-axe and had the patient, unhurried manner of someone who had held a line against beasts in the snow for a living. He bowed. Gu Yan bowed. Gu Yan's ribs ached under their bandages where Ye Han's frost had gone in, and his head still throbbed faintly from yesterday's step. He set his feet and drew.

He had told Han Qiu about the third pillar. The elder had listened, said nothing, and gone to sit in the Starfall box at the very front, with his sword across his knees. Principal Wen Taiyuan had taken the seat beside him. It was the first time Gu Yan had seen the Principal come down from the royal pavilion all Tournament.

"Begin."

Wei Shan came forward behind his shield like a wall walking.

It was a hard fight from the start. The shield was a fine spirit weapon, Earth-grade, and its lines were dense and tightly knit. Gu Yan's sword skated off it. Each time he found an angle around it, the axe came out from behind like a snake from under a rock. Twice he was forced to the edge of the platform. Once the axe-haft caught him across the bandaged ribs and he nearly dropped his sword. Wei Shan never hurried. He simply pressed, and pressed, and waited for Gu Yan to tire.

In the fortieth exchange, Gu Yan found the line he wanted — a weakness not in the shield but in the arm behind it, where Wei Shan's old wounds had stiffened the elbow — and he was gathering himself to cut along it when he felt the platform move.

Not shake. *Move*. The way a floor moves when a beam beneath it gives way.

He looked down. The formation-light that formed the wall around the platform's edge, which was meant to stop stray qi from reaching the crowd, had changed colour. It had been pale gold. Now it was deepening to amber, to orange, and it was no longer standing straight up from the edges. It was leaning inward. All four walls were bending in toward the centre of the platform, toward the two fighters, like the sides of a closing hand.

On the third pillar, a single loosened strand had come entirely free.

The formation was not broken. That would have been too obvious. It had been *turned*. All the force that was meant to push outward, protecting ten thousand spectators, was now being drawn inward, and gathering, and pressing down on the platform from every side at once.

The air grew heavy. Then very heavy. Gu Yan's knees bent under it. Across from him, Wei Shan grunted and went down on one knee behind his shield, his face purple, his eyes wide with incomprehension.

It was not meant for Wei Shan, Gu Yan thought, with a strange cold clarity. It was meant for whoever stood at the exact centre of the platform. And he could see now, in the lines, that the gathering force was not spread evenly at all. It was *focused*. It was converging on a single point, a pace in front of him and slightly to the left, where he had been about to step to make his cut.

Someone had read his fighting the way he read others'. Someone had watched him for six days and knew where he would be.

He looked up. Across the arena, high on the western tiers, the Lu box's awnings were half-drawn. In the shadow beneath them, a short round-shouldered figure sat with both hands folded on the head of a cane. One finger was very slightly raised.

The force came down.

* * *

Gu Yan moved before he had decided to.

He did not use the step. He could not — his head was still split from yesterday, and he knew, the way one knows a cracked bone, that if he tried to fold space now, it would fold him instead. He did the only other thing he could. He threw himself backward, *out* of the point of convergence, and as he went he cut — not at the force, which was far beyond him, but at the one strand of the formation's line that was nearest, the place where the bent wall met the jade. A carpenter's cut, along the grain.

It was like striking a mountain with a chisel. The shock went through his arms and his shoulders and drove him flat on his back. But the strand parted. One wall of the four lurched outward, and the converging force, losing a quarter of its frame, slid sideways off its point.

It slid toward him.

He saw it coming, a wave of crushing amber light, and his body would not move fast enough, and he thought, quite clearly, *Grandfather will be angry*.

Then there was a sword between him and the light.

He never saw Han Qiu cross the distance from the Starfall box. One moment the elder was seated; the next he was standing on the platform over Gu Yan with his blade raised flat, and the amber wave struck the sword and broke on it like surf on a rock. Han Qiu's feet sank an inch into the jade. His sleeves split to the shoulder. But the light parted around him, and Gu Yan, lying in the shadow of that sword, felt the pressure ease from his chest.

And then the Principal raised his hand.

Wen Taiyuan did not rise from his chair. He did not seem to exert himself at all. He lifted his right hand, palm down, and lowered it, as a man might quiet a table of noisy students — and the formation went still. All four walls, three bent and one broken, simply stopped. The amber faded to gold. The pressure lifted from the platform as if it had never been.

Gu Yan felt the Principal's Early Mandate domain brush past him: a vast, dry, patient presence like the inside of an old library, where every book was a law and all of them were in order.

Across the arena, Lu Wenhe rose to his feet, leaning on his cane.

"A disaster averted," he said. His voice was thin and carried well. "Thank the heavens for Principal Wen. I warned the Ministry only this morning that the third pillar was dangerously old. I see I was not wrong. The Lu clan will, of course, pay for the repair—"

"Be silent."

* * *

The King had not raised his voice. He had not stood. He was sitting where he had sat all Tournament, in the centre of the royal pavilion, with the Taixu envoy at his right hand and his sons at his left.

But the Arena of Ten Thousand Steps was no longer the same place.

Gu Yan, lying on his back on the jade, felt it happen. Every line he could see — in the stone, in the air, in the formation pillars, in the bodies of the people on the tiers — *changed*. Not broke. Not bent. They *aligned*. As if every thread in a vast and tangled weave had, all at once, remembered that it belonged to a single loom. The wind that had been gusting through the bowl stopped. A banner that had been snapping above the northern tiers hung motionless in the middle of a flap. Dust hung in the air. Ten thousand hearts — Gu Yan could feel them, a sea of small, frantic drums — slowed together and beat as one.

The sky above the arena had not changed. But it *belonged* to someone now.

This was a Mandate domain. Not the Principal's dry, contained library, but a King's: the law of the place, for as far as his will extended, replaced by his will. Within it, gravity was what the King said it was. Breath came because he permitted it. Gu Yan, staring up through the motionless banners at the pale sky and the Taixu ship beyond, understood for the first time in his body, rather than his head, why the founders of kingdoms were Mandate cultivators. It was not that they were strong. It was that, within their domain, they were the shape of the world.

In the Lu box, Lu Wenhe had sat down again. He had not chosen to. His knees had simply folded under him, and his cane lay on the floor, and his crumpled grandfather's face had gone the colour of old paper.

"In my arena," the King said, "accidents do not happen."

He let that sit in the motionless air for one long breath.

"The royal formation masters will examine every pillar. Patriarch Lu will remain in his seat until they are done, and then he will remain in my city until I say otherwise. Guards will stand with him, for his protection." The faintest dryness. "The Tournament will continue."

The domain withdrew. The banner finished its flap. Ten thousand people breathed out at once, in a sound like wind through a forest.

Han Qiu lowered his sword and looked down at Gu Yan. "Can you stand?"

"Yes."

"Then stand. You have a fight to finish."

* * *

He finished it. Wei Shan, grey-faced and shaking, insisted on continuing, and they fought for another twenty exchanges on the repaired platform under the eyes of six royal formation masters, and in the end Gu Yan cut along the line in the stiff elbow and the axe fell from Wei Shan's hand. The captain bowed to him very deeply, and to Han Qiu even more deeply, and walked off without a word.

The other quarterfinals went as the crowd had expected, mostly. Mo Ji fought Cang Yi with two splinted fingers and a swollen eye and lasted thirty-one exchanges, which was nineteen more than anyone had bet on, and conceded with the prince's spear-tip at his throat and a grin on his face. Su Lingxi defeated a royal guard commander's daughter with needles and patience. And Lu Mingyuan, last of all, walked onto the platform against a Duke of Pingyang's heir — Peak Star Sea, seven stars, the strongest name left in the bracket after the prince — and won in four exchanges, so quickly and so cleanly that the crowd did not cheer at all. They only murmured.

The semifinal pairings went up on the board in gold that evening.

*Cang Yi — Su Lingxi.*

*Gu Yan — Lu Mingyuan.*

* * *

The note was under his door when he came back to the dormitory that night.

It was folded once, on fine sky-blue paper that smelled faintly of sandalwood, and it was not signed. It did not need to be. The calligraphy was elegant, unhurried, and perfectly steady; Gu Yan, looking at its lines, could not find a single tremor in it anywhere.

*Tomorrow I will stop hiding.*

*Will you?*
