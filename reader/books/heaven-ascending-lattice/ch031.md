# Chapter 31: The Valley That Fell

The expedition left Cangdu an hour before dawn, while the lantern boats on the Mandate River were still drifting home with their wicks burned low.

Gu Yan rode in the third spirit-horse carriage with Mo Ji, Gu Feng, and a box of ration cakes that Mo Ji had already opened twice. Through the slatted window he watched the capital's great western gate shrink behind them, a hundred zhang of grey stone that had seemed, a few months ago, like the end of the world's possibilities. Now it looked like what it was: a wall. A good wall, well joined, the stress lines running clean down into foundations sunk deep in the river clay. But only a wall.

"You're doing it again," Mo Ji said around a mouthful of sesame cake.

"Doing what?"

"Staring at things like you're going to buy them." Mo Ji brushed crumbs off his broad knees. "It's a gate, brother. It opens, it closes. Eat something. Three hundred li is a long way to go hungry."

Gu Feng, sitting opposite, snorted without opening his eyes. He had reached Early Star Sea two weeks after Gu Yan, with four stars that the Star Mirror had read clearly and proudly, and since then he had carried himself with a new, quieter steadiness. The proud boy from the Jade Ridge clan trial was still there, but the edges had been sanded. "Let him stare," he said. "Last time he stared at something, it was a formation pillar, and the Formation Hall paid him forty spirit stones."

"And the time before that it was a boar," said Mo Ji, "and he got gored."

"I did not get gored. I got grazed."

"Gu Lan said gored."

"Gu Lan," said Gu Yan, "was standing behind a tree."

They had left her at the gate. At fifteen and 8th Vein she was too young for the Valley by the Academy's rules, and she had argued the point for three days before Elder Han Qiu ended the argument with a single look. She had pressed a pouch of dried plum into Gu Yan's hands at the carriage step and said, very seriously, *Bring everyone back, and bring back something shiny for me,* and then hugged Gu Feng so hard that the main-line heir had turned red to the ears.

* * *

The expedition was larger than Gu Yan had expected.

Starfall Academy sent twelve disciples under Elder Han Qiu. Cang Yi, the Third Prince, rode at the head of the Academy column on a silver-maned horse, his seven stars now settled at Mid Star Sea. Lu Mingyuan rode near him, courteous and composed in pale blue, Late Star Sea at eighteen and the highest-cultivated disciple of their year. Behind them came Fang Rui, Han Qiu's senior disciple, a lean young woman of twenty with a sword strapped across her back and a habit of saying nothing for hours; Lu Qing, a Lu clan cousin with a soft voice; Shen Yu and Tang Bo, two inner-court disciples who had made the list on merit points; Wei Zhen and Qiao Ning from the Formation Hall; and then the four from the outer court whose places had been argued over: Gu Feng, Su Lingxi, Mo Ji, and Gu Yan.

Mo Ji was 9th Vein, a full realm below the rest. He had been chosen for one reason, which Han Qiu had stated bluntly in front of the selection committee: "The Valley's gravity breaks light bodies. That boy's body is a block of iron. He goes." Su Lingxi had been chosen because the Royal Star Observatory had demanded a seat for one of its own, and she was the only Observatory disciple whose star-reading could function in a place where, as the Observatory's records said, *the stars are not our stars.*

And Gu Yan had been chosen because Han Qiu had added his name to the bottom of the list in his own hand, and nobody on the committee had wanted to ask a Heaven Seal 6th Seal sword master why he was spending a seat on a zero-star disciple.

Behind the Academy came the others. The Lu clan sent a team of its own: seven retainers in grey under a Lu elder named Lu Hengshan, a heavy-jowled man at Heaven Seal 3rd Seal who greeted Han Qiu with great warmth and was greeted in return with great politeness, which in Gu Yan's experience of Han Qiu meant something close to dislike. The royal family sent eight Golden Crow Guards in black-lacquered armour under Captain Yan Zhao, Heaven Seal 2nd Seal, whose duty, officially, was to guard the Third Prince, and who watched the Lu retainers rather more than he watched the road.

Thirty-odd cultivators, then, heading west toward a place that opened once every ten years.

On the second evening, at a waystation where the plains began to buckle into dry hills, Gu Yan found Su Lingxi sitting on the low wall of the horse yard with her jade compass in her lap. The needle was pointing west and trembling.

"It's been doing that since noon," she said, without looking up. She had the Observatory habit of starting conversations in the middle. "Not toward north. Not toward any star I know. Toward the Valley."

"Is that bad?"

"It's interesting." She tilted the compass so that the lamplight ran along its engraved rings. "My great-grandmother's notes say that within fifty li of Fallen Star Valley, every Observatory compass turns toward it, as if the Valley were heavier than the sky. She thought it was a lodestone. I don't." A pause. "And it still shows nothing for you. You stand right there and the needle doesn't so much as glance."

"I'm used to being ignored by needles."

She finally looked at him. Her eyes in the lamplight were the cool dark of deep water. "You're not ignored. You're *missing*. It's different." Then, as if she had said more than she meant, she closed the compass with a click. "Han Qiu wants us to sleep early. The road into the hills is worse."

He did not tell her that when he looked west with the Lattice, faintly, at the very edge of perception, he could see lines that did not belong to the hills at all.

* * *

They reached Fallen Star Valley at noon on the fourth day.

It did not look like a valley at first. It looked like a wound.

The hills of the western frontier rolled out in tawny, wind-polished folds, scattered with thorn and stunted pine, and then, without warning, the land simply *stopped*. A cliff dropped away in front of them, and beyond the cliff lay a basin some forty li across, ringed with jagged black ridges that rose like the broken rim of a bowl. At the center of the ring hung a haze, a pale wall of shimmering air that went up and up until it vanished into the sky.

"The Veil," Han Qiu said. The Elder had dismounted at the cliff's edge and stood with one hand resting on the pommel of his sword. He was a tall, spare man with grey at his temples and a face like a closed door. "For ten years it is solid. For twenty days it is not. Today is the first day."

Below them, on the basin's floor, Gu Yan could see the camps of others who had come: a few small sect banners, the pennant of some frontier garrison, mercenary tents. None came close to the Veil itself. They had left a wide ring of bare ground around it, and that ring was scattered with old bones.

"Those who try to enter on the nineteenth day," said Han Qiu, following his gaze, "and are still inside on the twenty-first, become part of the ground. Remember that. When I say we leave, we leave."

They descended by a goat track. Up close, the Veil hummed. It was not a sound, exactly; Gu Yan felt it in his teeth and in the old ache of his Starbone, a low pressure that rose and fell like breath. When he opened the Lattice in his sea of consciousness, the familiar grid of nine nodes—three lit now, Structure, Starbone, and Sword, glowing their steady silver among the six dark ones—trembled, as though something had plucked the frame.

He looked at the Veil with the lines.

The world around him was full of lines, as it always was. Stress lines in the rocks. Qi paths in the cultivators beside him, bright in Han Qiu and Lu Hengshan, tidy and hot in Cang Yi, dense and earthy in Mo Ji. The Veil was different. The Veil was a seam. Two sets of lines met there and did not agree with each other: on this side, the familiar grain of Azure Dust World, warm and slightly loose like aged timber; on the far side, something tighter, harder, older, laid out on a pattern so large that he could only see the edges of its joints.

It was like looking at a beam from a temple that had fallen into a woodshed.

Blood warmed his upper lip. He wiped it quickly with his sleeve and closed the Lattice.

"You're bleeding," Su Lingxi said quietly, beside him.

"Dry air."

She did not believe him, and did not say so.

* * *

They passed through the Veil in single file.

Gu Yan had braced himself for pain. Instead there was a moment of nothing at all, as though he had stepped off a stair that wasn't there, and then his foot came down on stone and his whole body was suddenly *heavier*. His knees bent before he could stop them. Behind him, Mo Ji grunted like an ox and came through with his fists clenched; ahead, Tang Bo staggered and caught himself on Shen Yu's shoulder.

"Gravity is two and a half times the outside," Han Qiu said, not turning. "It shifts. Keep your qi in your legs and your eyes on the ground."

Then Gu Yan looked up, and forgot to keep his eyes anywhere.

The sky was the wrong colour.

It was not blue. It was a deep, bruised violet shading toward bronze at the horizon, as if the whole vault were lit by a sunset from some other world. There was a sun—or something like a sun—hanging low and enormous, a dim copper disk three times the width of the one he knew, and it did not move as he watched it, and it cast no warmth. Across the violet drifted long pale ribbons, like clouds, except that they were straight, perfectly straight, and they crossed each other at precise angles.

And under that sky stood a city.

He had thought he understood scale. He had stood on Cangdu's walls and seen the capital spread out for thirty li. He had climbed the Star Tower. He had sat in the Principal's lecture hall and heard the words *three thousand worlds* and felt them as a dizziness.

This was different, because this he could touch.

The ruins began two hundred paces from the Veil and ran away into the violet distance farther than he could see. The nearest structure was a wall, or the stump of one, and each brick in that wall was larger than the Gu clan's ancestral hall. The bricks were a dark, glassy grey veined with faint gold, so smoothly dressed that their seams were thinner than a hair. Beyond the wall rose the broken shafts of towers, some snapped halfway up, some leaning at angles that should have brought them down a thousand years ago. One had fallen entirely and lay across the plain like a felled tree, and its broken end, facing them, was a circular cross-section a hundred zhang across, hollow, with a stair spiralling around its inner face whose individual steps were taller than Gu Yan.

Far beyond that, half-lost in haze, something that might have been a dome caught the copper light.

Nobody spoke. Even Lu Hengshan, who had surely heard descriptions, stood with his mouth slightly open.

"Fallen Star Valley," said Han Qiu, and for once there was something other than flatness in his voice. "The Academy's records say it fell from the sky one hundred thousand years ago, burning, and when the fire went out the land was this. The Observatory says it is a piece of another world." He glanced at Su Lingxi. "Principal Wen says it is a fragment of a Celestial Domain. That this was, once, a city of Immortals."

"How large was it," Cang Yi asked softly, "if this is only a piece?"

No one answered him.

Gu Yan opened the Lattice again, carefully, just a sliver.

The lines of the ruins leapt up at him. And for the first time in his life, they did not stop where the stone stopped. They ran out of the broken ends of the walls, out of the snapped tops of the towers, into the empty air, faint as spider silk, continuing the shape of what had been there. He saw the ghost of the wall rising another three hundred zhang above its stump. He saw the towers completing themselves in faint silver, their tops lost in the violet. He saw roads laid in a grid so vast that the Veil-basin of forty li was barely a corner of it.

And at the edge of everything, so faint he could not be sure it was real, he saw the lines continue past the rim of the Valley, past the black ridges, upward—into the sky, where they ended, raggedly, like threads pulled from a torn sleeve.

*It was torn off,* he thought. *Something tore this away from where it was joined.*

His head throbbed. He let the Lattice close, and the ghost-city vanished, and only the ruins remained, vast and silent under a sun that did not move.

* * *

They made camp that first night in the lee of the great wall, beneath one of the bricks.

Han Qiu set the watches and laid out the plan. The Valley had three zones. The outer zone, where they were now, was dangerous but known: ruins, shifting gravity, and the creatures the records called Shard Beasts. The middle zone lay beyond a ring of mist ten li inward, where the ruins grew denser and the gravity worse, and where several Academy teams over the centuries had lost members. The inner zone lay at the heart, behind a ring of black spires, and no one who entered it in living memory had ever been confirmed to come out.

"We stay in the outer zone for the first eight days," Han Qiu said. "The middle zone for six, if conditions allow. We do not approach the inner zone. Any disciple who goes near the spires without my order will be expelled, if he lives." His eyes went around the fire and lingered, Gu Yan thought, on him.

Gu Yan met the gaze and did not look away. He did not nod, either.

Later, when the others slept and Mo Ji was snoring like a forge bellows, Gu Yan lay on his back on the cold glassy stone and looked up at the violet sky. There were stars in it now. They were not his stars. The Cang Kingdom's constellations were gone—the Plough, the Weaver, the Nine Lamps—and in their place hung unfamiliar patterns, larger and brighter and very still.

Without meaning to, he began to draw on them.

It happened the way it did on the workshop roof in Jade Ridge, the way it had happened every night since: the Lattice stirred, and starlight came down into his bones in thin cold threads. But this starlight was different. It was heavier, denser, so sharp that his Starbone rang with it like a struck bell. Pain bloomed along his ribs and down his spine. He clenched his jaw and held on.

In his Star Sea, the nine gray stars that the Star Mirror could not see turned slowly in their frame. For a single heartbeat, one of them—the third, his Sword star—flickered as if something inside it had blinked.

Then, far away toward the Valley's heart, from the direction of the black spires he could not see, he felt something answer.

It was not a sound. It was a tug, faint and unmistakable, as if somewhere at the heart of this broken city a single silver thread had been pulled taut—and the other end was tied to his chest.
