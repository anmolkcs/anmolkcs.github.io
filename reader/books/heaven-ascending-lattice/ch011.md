# Chapter 11: The Road to Cangdu

On the first morning, Jade Ridge Town was gone by the third bend.

Gu Yan had watched for it. He sat at the back of the carriage with the shutter pushed up, and he watched the ridge itself, the pines, the grey line of the clan wall, until the road dipped into a valley of terraced millet and the whole of his life folded behind a hill. Then there was only the road, the creak of the axle, and the breathing of the two spirit-horses ahead, whose hooves struck the packed earth with a sound like someone knocking on a door that never opened.

Across from him, Gu Lan had already fallen asleep against the cushions with her mouth slightly open. Gu Feng sat upright beside the door with his sword across his knees, eyes closed, cultivating, or pretending to. The deacon from Starfall Academy rode up front with the driver and had said perhaps twenty words since dawn.

Gu Yan rested his hand on the plain iron sword at his side. His father's sword. The wrapped hilt was worn smooth in two places, where two different hands had held it. Inside his robe, against his ribs, lay the sealed letter, and in the small pouch at his belt the token of red metal they had taken from the dead man on the hill. He had not looked at it since. He did not need to. He could feel its weight the way one feels a splinter.

He closed his eyes.

The Lattice waited in his sea of consciousness, as it always did now: nine nodes in a square frame of silver lines, and two of them lit. The first burned with a steady, grained light, like sun through a planed board. The second, the one he had lit on the last night under Grandpa's roof, glowed cooler and deeper, and when he touched it with his attention he felt the faint ache of starlight still settling in his bones.

Seven empty nodes. Lines running between them that went nowhere yet.

*Every thing holds because of how it is joined,* he thought. *So what am I joined to?*

The carriage hit a rut. Gu Lan woke with a start, wiped her mouth, and glared at him as if he had caused it.

"You were thinking loudly," she said.

"I was sitting still."

"You sit still loudly. It's very irritating." She pushed up her own shutter and leaned out. "How far is it, really? Ten days? I asked Third Uncle once and he said Cangdu was so far that the moon rises there half an hour earlier."

"That's not how the moon works," said Gu Feng, without opening his eyes.

"How would you know? You've never been either."

Gu Feng's mouth tightened, but after a moment he said, "Fair."

It was the first joke Gu Yan had heard him make. He filed that away, too.

* * *

On the second day they joined the caravan.

It was waiting for them at a crossroads market town called Three Wells, where the road from Jade Ridge met the Eastern Post Road: forty wagons, a hundred armed outriders in brown, and eight more carriages like theirs, each flying a small blue pennant stitched with a falling star. Starfall Academy's recruits, gathered from the towns of three prefectures.

The deacon climbed down, exchanged a bow with another deacon, and returned with the information that they would travel with the caravan the rest of the way.

"Bandits?" asked Gu Lan.

"Beasts," the deacon said. "And other things." He looked, briefly, at Gu Yan. Not at his face; at the pouch on his belt. Then he climbed back up front.

So he knew about the token. Of course he did. He had been the one to wrap it in oilcloth and hand it back, saying, *The Academy will want to see this. Until then, it is yours to carry.*

Over the next days Gu Yan learned the other recruits' names in the evenings, when the caravan circled its wagons and lit fires. A pale boy from a salt-merchant family who already had the 3rd Vein open and could not stop mentioning it. A quiet girl, older than the rest, who had cultivated in secret for four years from a book found in a magistrate's kitchen, and who talked about qi the way Grandpa talked about timber.

Gu Feng took it worse than either of the others. On the fourth evening the salt-merchant's son asked him his cultivation, and when Gu Feng answered honestly, the boy said, "Ah. Well. The capital is big," in a tone of kindly condolence, and Gu Feng went and sat by himself on a wagon tongue for an hour.

Gu Yan brought him a bowl of millet porridge and sat beside him. They had not spoken much since the trial. It was difficult to know what to say to someone you had beaten in front of his whole clan.

"He's 3rd Vein because his father bought him a hundred pills," Gu Feng said at last. "I saw them. In a lacquered box. He eats them like candied plums."

"Then he'll have a very strong stomach."

Gu Feng snorted. He ate a little. "You didn't have to hold back, at the end," he said. "In the final. I know you did."

"I didn't hold back. I stopped."

"Is there a difference?"

Gu Yan thought about it, the way he would think about a joint that didn't sit right. "Holding back is not using what you have," he said. "Stopping is knowing when the work is done."

Gu Feng looked at him for a long moment. Then he finished the porridge and handed the bowl back. "You talk like your grandfather," he said, and it was not entirely an insult.

* * *

On the sixth day the land opened.

They had been climbing for a morning through low hills, and the caravan crested a long ridge, and the whole world fell away in front of them.

A plain. Not a valley, not a basin; a plain, so wide that the far side of it was not land at all but a pale blue haze that merged into the sky. Rivers lay across it like dropped silk ribbons, flashing where the sun touched them. There were cities on it, grey smudges with smoke rising, five, six, more, and between them a lacework of roads and canals and green-gold fields. Far to the north, a mountain range stood up out of the haze, white-capped, and above it the clouds were stacked in towers.

Even the salt-merchant's son was quiet.

Gu Yan stood on the wagon seat and looked, and looked. Jade Ridge Town could have been dropped into that plain and lost like a pebble in a rice paddy. He had seen maps. A map was a drawing. This was a thing, and it went on.

"The Central Plain of the Cang Kingdom," said the deacon, who had come up beside the wagon. "Seventy million people live on it. Cangdu is four days that way." He pointed vaguely into the haze. "You will not see it until the last day."

That night, for the first time, the deacon lectured.

He did it without ceremony. The recruits were sitting around the main fire, and he simply stood, waited until the talking stopped, and began, the way a man begins something he has done many times and still believes worth doing.

"Most of you," he said, "have spent your whole lives within a day's walk of where you were born. Today you saw the Central Plain, and you thought it was big. Good. Remember that feeling. You will need to multiply it, and keep multiplying it, for the rest of your lives."

He picked up a stick and drew in the dirt at the edge of the fire. A small, rough oval.

"The Cang Kingdom. Ours. Founded nine hundred years ago by a man who reached the Mandate realm and could bend the law of a land to his will. Roughly thirty thousand li from east border to west. There are a few hundred million of us."

He drew more ovals around it. Then more. Then more, until the dirt was crowded with them, overlapping, and he stopped only because he ran out of ground.

"The Eastern Wastes," he said. "Our kingdom is one of a hundred and eighty in it. Some are smaller than us. Many are much larger. Some are ruled by sects instead of kings. Over all of them, loosely, like a hand resting on a table, sits the Taixu Holy Land. You will hear that name again. In the Taixu Holy Land, a Star Sea cultivator is a servant who sweeps the courtyards."

The salt-merchant's son laughed uncertainly. The deacon did not.

He stepped back and drew one great curved line enclosing all the little ovals, and then three more such shapes beside it, so that the whole formed a rough four-lobed mass.

"The Eastern Wastes," he said again, tapping the first. "One of four great regions. Together they form one continent." He drew a final circle around all of it, and then, after a moment's thought, added, outside the circle, more marks, smaller and vague, like islands in fog. "And that continent is one of the lands of Azure Dust World. There are seas beyond it no ship from our kingdom has crossed. There are other continents. Nobody I have ever met has seen them."

Silence around the fire. A log settled and sent up sparks.

"So," said the deacon. "When you reach Cangdu and see its walls and think, *this is the centre of the world,* remember this drawing." He dropped the stick into the fire. "Starfall Academy is the greatest academy in our kingdom. It is a well. You are frogs. I say this with affection. A frog who knows it is in a well is already halfway to the rim."

He sat down. After a while, people began to talk again, quietly.

Gu Yan did not talk. He was staring at the marks in the dirt, the little oval that was everything he had ever heard of, lost among a hundred and eighty others, inside a region, inside a continent, inside a world whose edges no one he knew had seen.

And then he looked up.

The stars were out. Here on the plain, with no ridge to cut the sky, there were more than he had ever seen at once, a dense river of them pouring from one horizon to the other. Grandpa's voice came back to him from the workshop roof: *It's a ceiling, Yan'er. A painted ceiling. Everyone thinks it goes on forever.*

If the kingdom was an oval in the dirt, what was the drawing of the sky?

Something in his chest hurt, and it was not grief and it was not fear. It was the feeling of standing at the bottom of a staircase so tall the top was lost in cloud, and knowing, with complete certainty, that he was going to climb it.

* * *

He could not sleep. Around midnight he climbed onto the roof of the carriage, wrapped in his blanket, and lay on his back on the cold lacquered boards.

The Lattice responded before he asked it to.

He felt the second node stir, the cool one, and through it something like a thread let down from far above. Starlight. Not the light that fell on everyone, which he could see silvering the wagon tops and the sleeping horses, but a finer thing riding inside it, a cold, clean pressure that only the Lattice seemed able to catch. It came down through his forehead and spread through him in fine lines, following the grid that the Lattice had laid across his body over those weeks on the workshop roof: down the long bones of his arms, into the joints of his fingers, along his spine like water finding the grain of a board.

It hurt. It always hurt. He breathed through it the way Grandpa had taught him to breathe through a long saw-cut, slow and even.

But tonight something was different. The starlight did not only sink into bone. Some of it ran sideways, into the channel he had torn open on the hill above Jade Ridge, the first of the nine primary meridians, which since that night had carried a thin trickle of qi like a stream in a dry season. The starlight poured into it, and the stream swelled, and pressed, and found the end of the opened channel where the meridian ran on, sealed and silted, toward his left shoulder.

He saw it. With the first node's sight he saw it: not a wall but a seam, a place where the meridian had been closed like two boards glued edge to edge. Glue had a grain. Every join had a weakness, and the weakness was always where the two pieces had not quite agreed.

He guided the swollen stream there. Not straight on. At an angle, along the seam.

The meridian cracked open with a sound only he could hear, like ice going on a pond in spring.

Qi rushed through. His left arm went hot from shoulder to fingertip, then cold, then simply *alive* in a way it had not been a moment before, as if he had been wearing a glove all his life and someone had pulled it off. The second meridian filled, connected to the first, and the two together began to turn, slowly, a small wheel of qi circling through his chest. The pull on the outside air grew stronger; he could feel the night's thin spiritual qi drifting toward him like smoke toward a chimney.

2nd Vein.

He lay there panting, sweat cold on his face, and laughed silently up at the stars.

When he finally sat up, he found the deacon standing below the carriage in the dark, looking at him. How long he had been there, Gu Yan could not say.

"Starlight," the deacon said quietly, "does not usually do that."

Gu Yan said nothing. His heart was beating very hard. *Never speak of lines to anyone.*

After a moment the deacon nodded, as though Gu Yan had answered something. "There are a great many techniques in the world that use the stars," he said. "The Royal Observatory has dozens. You will find, in Cangdu, that people are much less surprised by things than they were in Jade Ridge." A pause. "And much more interested."

He walked away toward the fire.

* * *

The last four days were the long, flat road across the plain, with cities passing on either side and the traffic thickening hour by hour: ox-carts, canal barges, a troop of cavalry whose horses had scales on their legs. On the ninth day the caravan began to break apart, wagons turning off toward their own destinations, until only the Academy's carriages remained.

On the evening of the ninth day, someone started shouting from the carriage ahead.

Gu Yan pushed up the shutter. The sun had just gone down. The western sky was a dull violet, and the plain ahead was in shadow.

But on the horizon, something was glowing.

Not a fire. It was too wide and too steady for a fire, a band of warm gold light lying along the edge of the world, and above it the low clouds were lit from beneath, gold and rose, as though a second sun were rising there in the wrong place, at the wrong time.

"Is that—" Gu Lan began.

"Cangdu," said the deacon from up front. "The lanterns are being lit. We are still a hundred li away."

A hundred li. Gu Yan stared at the glow. Jade Ridge, on a festival night, could light up a single hillside.

As he watched, a point of light detached itself from the band on the horizon and rose, slowly, steadily, into the dark sky, until it hung far above the city like a new star. Then another. Then a dozen.

"What are those?" he asked.

The deacon was quiet for a moment. "Lantern boats," he said. "Tomorrow you'll see them up close." Then, in a different voice: "And one of them, if I'm not mistaken, is coming this way."

Gu Yan looked again. One of the risen lights was not hanging still. It was moving, growing larger, sliding toward them over the darkening plain with the silent certainty of something that knew exactly where it was going.
