# Chapter 2: Nine Empty Nodes

He did not sleep again that night, and before the rooster on the Wang family's wall had started its daily argument, Gu Yan was sitting cross-legged on the floor of the loft with his back straight and his hands resting on his knees, looking inward.

The Gu clan's meditation method was called the Nine Furnace Body Art, a Mortal-grade middle technique that every child in the compound began learning at eight. It was not subtle. It taught a cultivator of Body Forging to sink the mind into the body like a stone into a well, to feel each layer being tempered, the skin and flesh and tendon and bone, and to guide the faint heat of the breath toward whichever layer was being worked. Gu Yan had practised it every morning for eight years. He knew the inside of his own body the way he knew the inside of the workshop: where everything was, what creaked, what was sound.

This morning he did not go down into the body. He went the other way.

It was harder than he had expected. The mind did not like to look at itself; it kept sliding off, like a chisel off a knot. He breathed and waited and let the thoughts settle, the way one waits for silt to settle in a basin of river water, and slowly the dark behind his eyes grew deep. Not the darkness of closed eyelids. Something wider. He had the sense of standing on the shore of a still black sea, with no wind on it and no far side.

This, then, was the sea of consciousness the old texts talked about, the place where a cultivator's spirit lived. Most people of Body Forging could not perceive it at all. Gu Yan suspected he was perceiving it now only because something was hanging over it.

The frame.

From here, it was not an afterimage. It was a structure. Four silver lines, two running across and two running down, crossing to make a square divided into nine smaller squares. The lines were thin as hairs and perfectly straight, so straight they made his own sense of straightness feel clumsy. Where they crossed, and at the four outer corners and the midpoints of the edges, there were nodes: nine points of dull grey, each about the size of a lotus seed, each holding a quiet that was somehow deeper than the dark around it.

They were empty. He could feel that they were empty, the way a carpenter can feel that a mortise is cut and waiting for its tenon. Each one was a place where something was meant to go.

He looked closer, and saw what he had glimpsed last night: the silver lines did not stop at the edges of the frame. At each end they continued, very faint, for perhaps the width of a finger, and then dimmed into nothing. Not ended. Dimmed. As if the lines went on, and he simply could not see that far.

He reached toward the central node.

It was not reaching with a hand. It was more like leaning his attention against it, the way one leans on a door to see if it will give. The node did not give. Instead the pressure came back at him, all at once and from every direction, and something behind his eyes went white and hot.

Gu Yan jerked back into his body. He was bent over with his forehead almost on the floorboards, and there was a dull pounding in his skull as if someone were driving pegs into it. Something warm ran over his upper lip. He touched it, and his fingers came away red.

* * *

The memory came while he sat there holding his sleeve to his nose, unbidden, the way smells bring memories.

Winter. The old house, before the workshop, the one in the main compound with the moon gate and the plum tree. Rain on the tiles, heavy and steady. A lamp. His mother had already been dead two years by then, and he had been sleeping in his father's room because he did not like the sound of the rain alone. He had woken because the lamp was lit and his father was kneeling by the bed, still in his travelling cloak, which was soaked through and smelled of wet wool and something else, something sharp and metallic that Gu Yan had not had a name for at the time. Ozone, he thought now. The smell after lightning.

Gu Changfeng had been a tall man, with a long, quiet face and eyes that always seemed to be looking at something slightly farther away than whatever was in front of him. That night his eyes had been looking only at his son.

He had held out a stone. It was grey and smooth, the size of a quail's egg, without a single marking on it. Gu Yan remembered thinking it looked like the kind of stone you skipped across a pond.

"Yan'er," his father had said. "This will be cold. Don't be afraid. It won't hurt you. It will only show you what is already there."

He had pressed it to Gu Yan's chest, just over the heart. It had been unbelievably cold, cold enough to stop his breath, and then it had simply not been there any more. It had gone into him the way water goes into dry sand. His father had let out a long breath, the kind a man lets out after setting down a heavy load, and put his hand on Gu Yan's head.

"Sleep," he had said. "In the morning, be good to your grandfather."

In the morning he had been gone. That same winter, the old man had come back from the eastern road with his meridians in ruins, and the clan had moved them both out to the edge of the compound. For a long time Gu Yan had thought the two things were the same thing. He was no longer sure.

*It will only show you what is already there.*

He took his sleeve away from his nose. The bleeding had stopped.

* * *

His grandfather saw the blood the moment Gu Yan came down the ladder.

There was a smear of it on his sleeve and, he realised too late, a crust of it on his lip. The old man was at the workbench sharpening a gouge. He stopped with the blade on the stone.

"What happened?"

"Nothing. I was meditating."

"The Nine Furnace Art doesn't make children bleed from the nose. It makes them sweat and complain." Gu Shouyi put the gouge down. "What were you doing?"

Gu Yan hesitated. He had never told anyone about the frame. But this was his grandfather, and the old man had sat with his thumb on a knot nobody else could see.

"There are lines," he said. "In my head. Since Father... There's a frame, with nine points. I tried to push on one of them, and..."

He did not get any further, because his grandfather had crossed the workshop in three strides, faster than Gu Yan had seen him move in ten years, and taken his wrist in his good hand.

The grip was iron. There was no cultivation in it any more, but there was a carpenter's strength, which is a great deal. Gu Shouyi's face had gone pale and hard, and he was looking not at his grandson but at the door and the shuttered window, as if checking that they were shut.

"Listen to me," he said, very quietly. "You do not speak about lines. Not to Lan. Not to the patriarch. Not to any teacher, any elder, any friend, any girl. Not to me, unless we are alone and the doors are closed. Do you understand?"

"Grandfather, I..."

"*Do you understand?*"

"Yes."

The old man held his wrist a moment longer. Then his grip loosened, and something went out of his shoulders, and he looked suddenly very old.

"Good," he said. He let go and turned back to the bench, picked up the gouge, put it down again. "Good. Don't push on things you don't understand. Not with your head. Your head is the only one you have." He paused. "Wash your face. You have training."

Gu Yan stood there for a moment, his wrist aching where the old man had held it. He had a dozen questions. He looked at his grandfather's back, and at the gouge lying unsharpened on the stone, and asked none of them.

* * *

The clan training yard lay at the heart of the Gu compound, a square of packed red clay a hundred paces across, ringed by a covered gallery where elders could sit and watch and drink tea out of the sun. Along the eastern side stood a row of training stakes, posts of ironwood as thick as a man's thigh, bound with iron bands, sunk four chi into the ground. They had been hit so often over the generations that their faces were polished smooth and dark as old bronze.

Thirty-odd youths of the clan were gathered when Gu Yan arrived, main family and branch together, from twelve-year-olds still at 2nd Forging to the eldest at eighteen. The news about the trial had gone through the compound like a spark through dry straw. There was a different quality to the noise this morning: sharper, more nervous.

Instructor Gu Tieshan let them talk until he arrived, then stopped them with a look. He was a broad, scarred man of forty at the 4th Vein, the clan's chief martial instructor for as long as Gu Yan had been alive, with a voice built for shouting across fields.

"I know what you're all thinking about," he said. "Good. Think about it. And then remember that a month is not long enough to become anything you aren't already. A month is long enough to become what you are, properly. So. For the little ones who weren't listening last year, and the big ones who think they know it all already." He held up one thick finger. "What is Body Forging?"

Silence. A twelve-year-old near the front put up his hand and said, "The first realm, instructor."

"The first realm. The foundation. Every expert you have ever heard of, every elder at the Academy, the King himself on his throne, stood where you stand now, being hit by a stick." A few nervous laughs. "Nine Forgings. The first, the skin, until a blade skids on it. The second, flesh. The third, tendons. The fourth and fifth, the bones, outer and inner, until they ring like iron when struck. The sixth, the marrow, so the blood that comes out of your bones comes out strong. Seventh, the organs. Eighth, the blood itself. And the ninth..." He closed his fist. "The ninth is when all of it becomes one thing. A 9th Forging cultivator can lift nine thousand jin. Can run a horse into the ground. And can take a blow that would turn any of you into a puddle."

He let that settle.

"After the ninth comes Vein Awakening, when you open the meridians and draw qi into yourself, and your fists stop being only fists. And after that..." He shrugged. "Star Sea. Patriarch Gu Hong is at Late Star Sea, and he is the strongest man in Jade Ridge. Beyond that, the realms belong to people you will meet at Starfall Academy, if you're lucky, and should bow to if you're not. Right. Stakes. Three hundred strikes each, then I'll see where you are."

They went to the stakes.

Gu Yan took the one at the end of the row, as he usually did. It was the oldest, or at least the ugliest: its iron bands had been replaced so many times that the wood under them was scarred in rings, and at chest height there was a long, dark seam where some youth a generation ago had cracked it and the clan carpenter, possibly his own grandfather, had glued and clamped it back together.

He set his feet and began the Iron Hide Fist. One. Two. The shock of each blow ran up his arm and down into the slow heat of his marrow. Thirty. Forty. Down the row the thud of fists on wood rose into a ragged drumbeat.

On the hundred and twentieth strike, without meaning to, he looked at the stake the way he looked at timber.

The silver lines rose out of the wood. He saw at once that the old glued seam was not the weakness. The repair was sound; the glue and clamping had been done by someone who knew their work. The true weakness was a hand-span lower and on the other side, where years of blows had been landing slightly off-centre, always from right-handed fighters, always at the same angle. The lines there were bent and bunched, thinned to a single strand at one point, like a rope frayed down to its last fibre. The stake was holding by habit.

The pounding in his skull from this morning came back, very faintly. He let the lines go.

A hundred and twenty-one. A hundred and twenty-two. He should leave it alone. He knew he should leave it alone.

On the hundred and fiftieth strike he shifted his feet a little, turned his hips a little, and put his fist, with no more force than any of the others, exactly on the frayed point.

There was a crack like a branch breaking in frost. The ironwood stake split down its length from the strike point to the ground, the two halves leaning apart, an iron band springing loose and clanging off across the clay.

The drumbeat down the row stopped.

Thirty faces turned toward him. Gu Yan looked at his fist, which was not even bruised, and then, carefully, at the stake.

"It was already cracked," he said, to the general silence. He pointed at the old glued seam. "See? Here. It must have been weak for years."

Instructor Gu Tieshan came over. He looked at the split stake, and at the seam, and at Gu Yan.

"Hm," he said. "Old wood." He raised his voice. "Did I tell you to stop? Two hundred more! Gu Yan, take the next stake."

The drumbeat started again. Gu Yan moved to the next stake and began his strikes, keeping his eyes on the wood's ordinary, visible grain. At the far end of the row, he noticed, Gu Feng had not resumed. The main family's best youth stood with his hands loose at his sides, looking at the broken stake with an expression of thoughtful interest, and then at Gu Yan, and then he turned back to his own post and struck it so hard the iron bands rang.

When the session ended and the yard emptied, Gu Yan glanced back once from the gate.

The instructor was still standing by the split stake, alone. He had picked up one of the halves and was turning it in his hands, running his thumb along the break. The break was perfectly clean, and perfectly straight, and it ran nowhere near the old glued seam at all.

Gu Tieshan looked at it for a long time. Then he took a small notebook from his belt, wrote something in it, and tucked it away.
