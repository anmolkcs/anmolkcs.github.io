# Chapter 21: Drained

By morning the body was gone, and the Academy was pretending it had never been there.

The deacons had carried Zhou Pei away on a covered litter before first light. By the dawn bell, someone had scrubbed the flagstones at the mouth of the lane between buildings five and six, though there had been no blood to scrub. By the second bell, the whole of the outer court had been summoned to stand in rows in the yard before building six, two hundred disciples in pale blue robes, and nobody spoke above a murmur.

"Zhou Pei," Mo Ji said under his breath. He had spent the hour before dawn asking questions in the way only Mo Ji could, which was loudly and of everyone. "Outer court, third year. Fourth Vein. He sharpened my practice blade once for nothing, because he said I was holding the whetstone like a brick."

"Were you?"

"Probably." Mo Ji did not smile. "They're saying qi deviation."

Gu Yan said nothing. He was seeing the lane again, as he had seen it in the lantern light: the young man on his back with his arms flung out, the skin of his hands creased like paper left in the sun. And beneath the skin, through the lines, the meridians. Not broken. Not torn. Scraped hollow, every one of them, the way a dry riverbed is hollow.

It was exactly like the beast in Black Pine Forest, months ago and a thousand li away. Drained, not torn. And beside the body, curled on the stones, a single thread of dark red.

"Qi deviation," he said quietly, "does not look like that."

Mo Ji glanced at him. "Then what does it look like?"

"Like something drank him."

A deacon raised his voice at the front. "The matter is in the hands of the Discipline Hall. The morning lessons proceed as scheduled. Anyone found spreading rumours will lose fifty merit points." He paused, then added in a softer voice, as if he did not entirely believe his own words, "Disciple Zhou suffered a deviation while practising alone at night. Let it be a lesson to all of you about reckless cultivation."

The rows began to break apart, slowly, the way ice breaks in spring.

Someone stopped beside Gu Yan. He did not need to turn to know who; the faint cold that hung around her like the air near a winter window was distinctive.

"You looked," Su Lingxi said.

"So did you."

She held her jade compass loosely in one hand, the lid closed. Her expression was as composed as ever, but her knuckles were pale. "My compass reads fate threads. Every living person has them, even the dying. Some fray, some knot, some simply end." She did not look toward the lane. "His did not end. It was cut short and the cut end was pulled. Like a thread drawn through the eye of a needle by someone on the other side."

"Pulled toward where?"

"That," she said, "is the question the Discipline Hall has decided not to ask."

* * *

Elder Han Qiu's courtyard sat high on the northern slope of the Academy, where the wind came down off the mountains with nothing to stop it. There was a single plum tree, a stone table, a rack of plain swords and a grove of green bamboo that rattled like dice in a cup. It was the morning after the leaf, and the first morning of his instruction. *Do not make me regret it,* the old man had said.

He was not late. Han Qiu was already standing in the bamboo, hands behind his back, a narrow figure in grey whose presence pressed on the air like the edge of a cliff.

"You saw the body," Han Qiu said.

It was not a question, so Gu Yan did not answer it.

"The Academy is a tree with many roots," the Elder said. "Some of those roots go into the palace. Some go into the ducal houses. When a disciple of the outer court dies strangely, the tree does not shake. It waits to see which root is rotten. That is how trees survive storms." He turned. His eyes were very dark and very still. "You are a recorded disciple of mine. That means you are now a twig on one of my branches. If you go digging around rotten roots, you will snap, and I will have to pretend I did not notice. Do you understand?"

"I understand, Elder."

"Good. You will ignore it, then?"

Gu Yan considered lying, and decided that lying to a man who could read the intent in a falling leaf was a poor use of his morning. "I'll be careful."

Han Qiu's mouth did something that, on another face, might have been the beginning of a smile. "That is not the same answer." He drew a bamboo practice sword from the rack and tossed it to him. "Cut that stalk."

Gu Yan looked at the bamboo. A good stalk, thick as his wrist, green and slick. He let the lines come, lightly. He saw the fibres running long and straight, the nodes where they knotted, the hollow inside, the faint weakness just above the third node where the wind had bent it last week. He set his feet, breathed through his fifth vein, and cut.

The practice blade bit and stopped. A clean notch, a third of the way through. Bamboo dust drifted.

"Again."

He cut again, at the weakness this time. The stalk split with a crack and the top half leaned, caught by its neighbours.

"You found where it was already broken," Han Qiu said. "That is a carpenter's cut. A good carpenter. Your grandfather would be pleased." He walked over, took the practice sword out of Gu Yan's hand and, without seeming to aim, flicked it across another stalk. There was no sound. A moment later, the stalk slid apart along a perfect diagonal and both halves stood upright, as if they had not yet been told.

"Where was the weakness in that one?" Gu Yan asked.

"There wasn't one." Han Qiu handed back the sword. "Yesterday I told the yard that a cut is a line chosen. You cut the leaf along its grain. That was the beginning, not the lesson. Listen, because I will say this once and then I will expect you to spend a year understanding it. The world is full of lines. You see more of them than most, I think; your eyes move strangely when you look at things. But seeing a line is not cutting it. A sword does not follow the lines the world gives you. A sword *chooses* one, and then the world must agree."

"And if the world doesn't agree?"

"Then your sword is not sharp enough." Han Qiu turned back toward the plum tree. "Again. Until the bell."

Gu Yan cut until the bell, and his hands bled, and the bamboo refused to agree.

* * *

They went out on the third night, after the Discipline Hall had sealed the lane and the rumours had already begun to fade in the way that rumours do when people are afraid of them.

Mo Ji insisted on coming. Su Lingxi came because, as she put it, the deal they had struck in the Observatory archive did not specify that she would let her research subject get himself killed before she finished studying him.

"I'm flattered," Gu Yan said.

"Don't be. You are an anomaly, not a friend." She paused. "Not only a friend," she amended, so quietly that Mo Ji, three paces behind, did not hear.

The lane between buildings five and six was silver under a half moon. Discipline Hall paper talismans hung across its mouth, humming faintly. They did not touch them. Gu Yan crouched on the flagstones just outside, where the body had lain, and opened his eyes the other way.

At first there was nothing. The stones were cold, their cracks old and ordinary. He pushed harder. The ache started immediately, a needle behind his eye, then a nail.

And there, very faint, like the ghost of a hair on a polished table, a thread of crimson.

It was not qi as he knew it. Qi lines were living things, flowing, branching. This was thin and taut and slightly sticky, as if it had been drawn out of something and dried in the air. It lay across the flagstones in a gentle curve and ran along the foot of the dormitory wall, downhill.

"There's a line," he whispered. "Red. Blood qi, I think. Old. It goes downhill."

Su Lingxi opened her compass. The needle, a sliver of star-iron in a sea of carved constellations, shivered and turned. She frowned at it. "Something passed here carrying a great deal of other people's fate. The residue is like smoke after a fire. Follow your line. I'll tell you if it lies."

They followed it.

Along the wall, down a flight of steps behind building five, and into the stone culvert that carried rainwater from the outer court toward the lake. The thread grew fainter, then briefly stronger where something had paused, then fainter again. Gu Yan's nose began to bleed somewhere in the culvert. He wiped it on his sleeve and kept going. Mo Ji walked behind him with one hand on his shoulder, guiding him over stones he could no longer see because his eyes were full of lines.

The culvert ran east beneath the boundary between the outer court and the inner court residences. Above them were the walled courtyards that wealthy families rented for their children; the ducal houses kept the largest.

The thread climbed out of the culvert through an iron grate, crossed a narrow lane, and ended against a wall.

Gu Yan stood with his palm flat against the plaster. On the other side, through the faint lines of a privacy formation, he could feel the shape of a garden, a pond, a pavilion. Above the gate further down the lane hung a lacquered board with two characters in elegant gold: *Lu Residence.*

"It goes in," he said. "Or it stops here. I can't tell which. The wall has a formation on it; it cuts the line off."

Mo Ji's jaw worked; he had not forgotten the Lu stewards at his father's shop. "Then it's them."

"It's a line that ends at a wall," Su Lingxi said quietly. She closed her compass. "My needle points the same way. But a line that leads to a wall is not a confession. The Lu Residence shares this lane with three other courtyards and a kitchen gate that every servant in the inner court uses. If you take this to the Discipline Hall, they will ask what you saw, and you will say *lines*, and they will ask what lines, and then they will start asking about you instead."

Gu Yan said nothing. His grandfather's old warning was in his head: *Never speak of the lines.*

Footsteps sounded at the end of the lane: two patrol deacons, lanterns swinging. The three of them stepped back into the shadow of the culvert and waited until the light passed. When they emerged, the thread had faded completely, as if the night had finally finished drinking it.

"Not proof," Gu Yan said. "But not nothing."

"Not nothing," Su Lingxi agreed. She looked at the blood on his sleeve and, after a moment, handed him a folded handkerchief that smelled faintly of cold pine. "Clean your face. You look like you lost a fight with your own head."

* * *

He could not sleep. Mo Ji snored across the room like a bellows with a grudge. Gu Yan sat cross-legged by the window with his father's iron sword across his knees and turned the night over and over in his mind.

The thread ending at the wall. The bamboo that would not agree. *A cut is a line chosen.*

He closed his eyes and sank into the Lattice.

Nine nodes joined by silver lines, a frame without a picture. The first node, top left, glowed with a soft, grainy light, the understanding he had earned watching his grandfather fit a tenon into a mortise without a single nail: *every thing holds because of how it is joined.* The second, beside it, burned with cold starlight, the comprehension written into his bones on a hundred nights of agony on the workshop roof and the dormitory eaves. *A body can be remade by what it takes in.*

The third node, at the end of the top row, was dark.

He thought about the first node. It told him how things held together. The grain. The joints. Where the world was already weak. That was why he had found the crack in the bamboo; the first node had shown it to him, and his sword had simply gone where it was told.

That was not a cut. That was obedience.

He thought about Han Qiu's stalk sliding apart where there was no weakness at all.

*Every thing holds because of how it is joined.* Then every thing could be unjoined. Not only at the joints the world had already made, but anywhere a line could be drawn. The grain told you how a thing had been put together; the sword was the decision about how it would come apart. The first was understanding. The second was will.

He saw it suddenly, completely: the world was not only lines he could find. It was lines he could *choose*. Every surface, every stalk, every body and every formation was crossed by infinite possible lines, and nearly all of them were nothing, only possibilities, like the cuts a carpenter imagines before he lifts the saw. A cut was the moment one possibility was made true. And the sharpness of a sword was not in its edge. It was in how completely the swordsman refused every other line.

The iron sword on his knees grew cold.

In the Lattice, the third node kindled. It did not glow softly like the first or burn like the second. It lit like a line drawn with a single stroke of a brush: thin, white, absolutely certain. Silver raced from it along the frame to the second node, then to the first, and for one breath the entire top row of the Lattice rang like a struck bell.

He felt it in his body. The grain-sense, the starbone strength and the new, cold clarity snapped together into a single row, each reinforcing the others: his perception sharper because it now had a purpose, his body steadier because it now had an edge to carry, his will finer because it had something solid to stand on. Resonance. A complete line in a frame of nine.

He opened his eyes. Moonlight lay across the floor. He lifted the iron sword, not drawing it, only resting his thumb on the scabbard, and looked at the candle stub on the windowsill.

He chose a line.

He did not move. But the candle stub slid apart into two neat halves along a diagonal, and both halves stood, and the wick in the upper half kept burning.

"Hm," said a voice from the doorway.

Gu Yan nearly dropped the sword. Han Qiu stood in the dark of the corridor, hands behind his back, as if he had been standing there for some time. Mo Ji snored on, oblivious.

"Elder," Gu Yan managed.

"I felt a sword wake up on this side of the mountain," Han Qiu said. "I came to see whose." He looked at the candle for a long moment. The flame trembled in the draught from the window, still burning, still cut. "Three days ago I told you it would take a year."

"I'm sorry, Elder."

"Don't be sorry. Be early." He turned to go, then paused. "The Star Tower opens to this season's new disciples the day after tomorrow. Climb it. You'll learn things about your body that I can't teach you." His voice dropped, so low that it was almost lost under Mo Ji's snoring. "And stop walking along culverts at night with blood on your sleeve. The Discipline Hall is not the only one who has eyes in that lane."

He was gone before Gu Yan could ask what he meant.
